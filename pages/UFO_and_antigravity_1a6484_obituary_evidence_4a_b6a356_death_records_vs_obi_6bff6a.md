## Introduction

In disputed deaths involving UFO, [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) or unconventional-propulsion researchers, an obituary can establish that someone died and preserve a useful biography. It normally cannot establish the medical or medicolegal facts needed to decide whether a death was natural, accidental, suicidal, homicidal or genuinely unresolved. That is the job of death certification and, where necessary, coroners, medical examiners, post-mortems, toxicology and police investigation.

This distinction is particularly important when an obituary gives no cause of death and later internet accounts fill the silence with claims of suicide, poisoning, assassination or mysterious illness. A blank obituary is not evidence for any of those possibilities. The correct next question is whether an official death certificate or associated medicolegal record exists, what it actually records, who certified it, and whether the finding was final or still pending. US [death certificates]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }}) are expressly designed to record diseases, injuries, contributing conditions and, in relevant cases, the circumstances of external causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/manuals/2025/2a-2025.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2a 2025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instructions for Classifying the Underlying Cause-of-Death, ICD-10, 2025...</span></span></span>

## What medical certification records

A death certificate is not simply a more detailed obituary. It belongs to a formal vital-registration system in which designated medical or medicolegal professionals certify information for legal and public-health purposes. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) describes the medical-certification section as recording diseases, morbid conditions, injuries and poisonings that caused or contributed to death, together with circumstances surrounding [accidents]({{ 'accidents/' | relative_url }}) or violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/manuals/2025/2a-2025.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2a 2025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instructions for Classifying the Underlying Cause-of-Death, ICD-10, 2025...</span></span></span>

That structure can preserve several facts that a memorial notice ordinarily does not:

The distinction between immediate and underlying cause matters. Someone might ultimately die from an acute physiological event, but simply naming that final event can conceal the disease, injury or poisoning responsible for it. The CDC therefore instructs certifiers to record the sequence leading to death rather than merely the terminal event. Its guidance defines the underlying cause as the disease or injury that initiated the chain of events leading to death, or the circumstances of the accident or violence that produced a fatal injury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/manuals/2025/2a-2025.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2a 2025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instructions for Classifying the Underlying Cause-of-Death, ICD-10, 2025...</span></span></span>

That makes a properly completed certificate potentially far more informative than memorial wording such as “died suddenly”, “passed unexpectedly” or no cause given at all. Those phrases may be socially meaningful, but they are not medical classifications.

The principle is not uniquely American. In England and Wales, government guidance says the Medical Certificate of Cause of Death establishes the medical cause for registration. Once registered, a certified copy of the register entry — commonly called the death certificate — contains an exact copy of the cause-of-death information from that medical certificate. Since September 2024, deaths not investigated by a coroner are also subject to independent scrutiny through the statutory medical-examiner system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/medical-certificate-of-cause-of-death-mccd-guidance-for-medical-practitioners/guidance-for-medical-practitioners-completing-medical-certificates-of-cause-of-death-in-england-and-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.uk.</span></span></span>

## Cause and manner are different questions

One of the most consequential errors in disputed-death research is treating “cause of death” and “manner of death” as interchangeable.

**Cause of death** describes the medical process, disease, injury or poisoning responsible for death. **Manner of death** classifies the circumstances in which that cause arose. US certification practice recognises natural, accident, suicide, homicide, pending investigation and could-not-be-determined classifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/manuals/2022/2b-sectionv-2022.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2b sectionv 2022</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instructions for Classifying Multiple Causes of Death, 2022 - Section V...</span></span></span>

The difference becomes obvious in an injury case. “Blunt-force trauma” may be the cause; homicide may be the manner. A poisoning could be accidental, suicidal or homicidal depending on the evidence about how the substance was administered. The same physical cause therefore does not automatically establish intent.

This matters directly to claims that a controversial researcher was “suicided” or murdered. A certificate identifying a particular injury answers a different question from a medical examiner's classification of that injury as suicide, homicide or undetermined. Conversely, the fact that someone suffered a fatal injury does not by itself demonstrate who caused it or why.

The CDC's medicolegal guidance shows that a suicide classification is supposed to involve more than the mere existence of a self-inflicted injury. Evidence can include pathology, toxicology, investigation, psychological information, witness statements and evidence bearing on the deceased person's intent. Medical examiners and coroners may likewise use police reports and discussions with relatives and friends when determining manner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/misc/hb_me.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medical Examiners&#x27; and Coroners&#x27; Handbook on Death Registration and Fetal Death Reporting...</span></span></span>

“Homicide” also requires careful interpretation. In death certification it is a medicolegal classification, not necessarily a finding of criminal murder against a particular person. The medical examiner determines the manner of death; criminal responsibility is a separate question for investigators, prosecutors and courts. That distinction prevents an official homicide classification from being stretched into evidence for a particular alleged conspiracy.

## A certificate can confirm uncertainty rather than eliminate it

Official certification is stronger evidence than memorial language, but it should not be treated as an infallible final answer. One particularly important feature is that the system can formally record uncertainty.

US death-registration rules provide classifications for both **pending investigation** and **could not be determined**. CDC coding guidance explicitly recognises cases in which an investigation has not established whether an injury was accidental, suicidal or homicidal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/manuals/2022/2b-sectionv-2022.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2b sectionv 2022</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instructions for Classifying Multiple Causes of Death, 2022 - Section V...</span></span></span>

That distinction is valuable in controversial cases. “Pending” means that a researcher has encountered an unfinished determination, not evidence that officials secretly know the answer. Toxicology, additional investigation or other findings may still be outstanding. “Could not be determined”, by contrast, can be the final medicolegal conclusion when the available evidence does not make one manner sufficiently more compelling than the alternatives. CDC guidance describes the latter category as appropriate when competing manners remain comparably supported after the available information has been considered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/misc/hb_me.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medical Examiners&#x27; and Coroners&#x27; Handbook on Death Registration and Fetal Death Reporting...</span></span></span>

Certificates can also be amended. Connecticut regulations, for example, provide a formal process for changing cause-of-death information when the original diagnosis changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eregulations.ct.gov/eRegsPortal/Browse/RCSA/Title_19aSubtitle_19a-41Section_19a-41-10/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eregulations.ct.gov">[eRegulations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eregulations.ct.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e Regulations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2005</span></span></span> This means researchers dealing with a disputed death should distinguish an initial certificate from an amended or final version rather than assuming that the first document encountered represents the ultimate finding.

The practical rule is simple: **uncertainty written into an official record should be reported as uncertainty**. It should neither be converted into proof of foul play nor quietly rewritten as a definite natural death or suicide.

## Eugene Mallove shows what the stronger record changes

The 2004 death of unconventional-energy advocate Eugene [Mallove]({{ 'mallove/' | relative_url }}) provides a useful control case because the underlying event was genuinely homicidal. Here, the difference between memorial evidence and medicolegal evidence is concrete rather than theoretical.

A Norwich Police Department release preserved in FBI records states that Connecticut's Office of the Chief Medical Examiner conducted a post-mortem examination and ruled Mallove's death a **homicide caused by blunt-force trauma**. The same police notice describes forensic testing of evidence from the crime scene and Mallove's recovered vehicle and lists property believed to have been stolen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/fbifiles/scientists/eugenemallove-fbi2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents2.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents2.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault U.S. Department of Justice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultU.S. Department of JusticeJanuary 29, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 29, 2021</span></span></span>

Those facts do substantially more evidential work than an obituary could. They establish that “homicide” is not merely language subsequently adopted by writers interested in Mallove's unconventional-energy work. There was an official post-mortem finding, and police were conducting a criminal investigation.

But the same documents illustrate the limit of death certification. “Homicide caused by blunt-force trauma” does **not** establish that Mallove was killed because of his research. Cause and manner answer the medical and medicolegal questions; motive requires different evidence. A claim connecting his killing to suppressed energy technology would therefore require police evidence, court records, communications, witness evidence or some other reliable material establishing that connection.

This is precisely why source matching matters. An official homicide finding can disprove a claim that Mallove died naturally, but it cannot by itself prove a research-related assassination.

## Recent Alabama deaths present an access problem

The distinction becomes especially important when dealing with researchers whose deaths are recent and whose [obituaries]({{ 'obituaries/' | relative_url }}) omit a cause. Amy [Eskridge]({{ 'eskridge/' | relative_url }}), for example, died in Alabama in 2022, and online discussion has subsequently attached competing claims to her death. In such a situation, simply repeating a later assertion that the death was suicide does not solve the evidential problem created by a cause-free obituary.

There is also a practical reason why independent verification may be difficult. Alabama's Department of Public Health states that death certificates are confidential for **25 years after death**. During that period, access is limited to specified relatives, legal representatives and certain other eligible people. Only after 25 years may anyone obtain the certificate on payment of the required fee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Public HealthDeath Certificates &#124; Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH)March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

For recent Alabama cases, therefore, failure to locate a public death certificate is not evidence that no certificate exists or that officials have suppressed it. The state's normal access law itself can prevent an unrelated researcher from obtaining one.

That point materially changes how disputed claims should be written. If a publicly accessible obituary confirms a death but does not state the cause, while the official certificate remains legally restricted, the defensible conclusion is that the **publicly reviewed evidence does not establish the medical cause**. A secondary source claiming suicide, overdose, cancer or another cause must be assessed on its own sourcing. It should not be silently upgraded to the status of an official finding.

Access rules vary sharply by jurisdiction. Connecticut, for example, permits anyone aged 18 or over to purchase a certified death certificate, although sensitive information such as a decedent's Social Security number is more restricted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.ct.gov/dph/vital-records/death-certificates" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.ct.gov">[CT.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.ct.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">death certificates</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">death certificates</span></span></span> Researchers therefore need to establish the law and records system of the place where the death was registered rather than assuming that all American death certificates are equally public.

## Which source should support which disputed claim?

For [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) [researcher deaths]({{ 'missing-denominator/' | relative_url }}), the most reliable approach is not to ask which single source is “best”. Different records answer different questions.

An **obituary or funeral notice** is usually appropriate for identity, date of death, age, family relationships, employment, institutional affiliations and the way someone's life was publicly memorialised. It can also establish that a researcher really was associated with UFO, antigravity or unconventional-energy work when the notice explicitly says so.

A **death certificate or medical cause-of-death certificate** is the appropriate source for the certified medical sequence leading to death. In the United States, it may additionally record manner of death, injury circumstances and information about whether an autopsy contributed to the determination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/manuals/2025/2a-2025.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2a 2025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instructions for Classifying the Underlying Cause-of-Death, ICD-10, 2025...</span></span></span>

A **medical-examiner or coroner file** becomes more important when the question is *why* an unusual death received a particular classification. Autopsy findings, toxicology and investigative information can contain evidence that is necessarily compressed on the certificate itself. Connecticut law, for example, separately addresses medical-examiner examination, autopsy and scientific records, illustrating that these materials constitute a deeper evidential layer than the vital certificate alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/pub/chap_368q.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cga.ct.gov">[Connecticut General Assembly]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cga.ct.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Connecticut General Assembly Chapter 368q</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Connecticut General Assembly Chapter 368q</span></span></span>

A **police investigative record** is the relevant source for crime-scene evidence, witnesses, suspects and investigative theories. **Court records** are stronger still for what prosecutors alleged, what evidence was tested in adversarial proceedings and whether anyone was convicted. Neither an obituary nor a death certificate can substitute for those sources when the disputed proposition concerns the identity or motive of a killer.

That creates a useful hierarchy for recurring claims in this field:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* “The researcher died on this date” — an obituary or vital record may suffice.
* “The researcher died from this disease or injury” — seek medical certification.
* “The death was officially classified as suicide or homicide” — seek the certificate or medical-examiner/coroner finding.
* “Toxicology detected a particular substance” — seek the toxicology or post-mortem record rather than an unsourced retelling.
* “Investigators suspected another person” — seek police or prosecutorial records.
* “The person was murdered because of antigravity or UFO research” — neither the obituary nor the word *homicide* on a death record proves that motive; independent investigative evidence is required.

</div>

## When an obituary is not enough evidence

The most important lesson is not that death certificates are automatically conclusive. It is that they are **designed to answer questions an obituary is not designed to answer**.

Official death certification is produced within a governed medical and vital-statistics process. In the United States, the certifier is typically a physician, medical examiner or coroner, and federal guidance specifies how causal sequences and manner-of-death classifications should be recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/131005" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stacks.cdc.gov">[CDC Stacks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stacks.cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Physician’s Handbook on Medical Certification of Death: 2023 Revision...</span></span></span> In England and Wales, medical certification similarly forms the basis of the legally registered cause of death, with independent medical-examiner scrutiny now covering deaths that do not proceed to a coroner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/medical-certificate-of-cause-of-death-mccd-guidance-for-medical-practitioners/guidance-for-medical-practitioners-completing-medical-certificates-of-cause-of-death-in-england-and-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not make errors, amendments or disputed judgements impossible. Cause-of-death certification is itself a professional determination, and difficult cases may remain pending or ultimately undetermined. What it provides is a documented official conclusion, an identifiable certifier and a framework in which uncertainty can itself be recorded.

For alleged suspicious deaths among UFO and antigravity researchers, that is a crucial evidential boundary. An obituary that omits the cause of death creates a **gap**, not a mystery with a predetermined answer. The gap should be filled, where records are obtainable, with the death certificate and relevant medicolegal evidence. Where those records are restricted, unavailable or inconclusive, the uncertainty should remain visible rather than being replaced by whatever explanation became most widely repeated online.
