## Introduction

The strongest version of a common-[motive claim]({{ 'motive-claim/' | relative_url }}) is not that several scientists worked on “UFOs”, “[antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }})”, aerospace or advanced energy. It is that several researchers possessed the **same sensitive information, threatened the same programme, or took actions that created the same identifiable reason for somebody to silence them**. That distinction matters because the research categories used in suspicious-death narratives are often so broad that they create apparent connections between people whose actual work, institutions and circumstances were quite different.

The public record does contain narrower facts worth investigating: James E. McDonald challenged official treatment of UFO evidence; Ning Li pursued unconventional superconducting-gravity research that received US Army funding; Amy Eskridge publicly discussed gravity modification and expressed fears about suppression; Eugene Mallove aggressively promoted [cold fusion]({{ 'cold-fusion/' | relative_url }}) and alleged institutional resistance. But these facts do not by themselves establish a shared motive. In some cases, later evidence actively weakens the proposed connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/1968-07-29-house-symposium-on-ufos-committee-print-7-house-symposium-on-ufos-1968" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[ufotransparency.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">House of Representatives (90th Congress, 2d Sess, No. 7), U.S. House Committee on Science and Astronautics · 1968 · UFO TransparencyJuly...</span></span></span>

## What would a specific shared motive need to show?


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

A useful way to test a common-motive theory is to move from **category** to **object**. “Advanced propulsion” is a category. A particular prototype, classified programme, technical document, set of experimental results or disclosure plan is an object. A coordinated-killing hypothesis becomes materially stronger only when several researchers can be connected to something comparably specific.

</div>

That means asking whether two or more cases share a demonstrable chain such as: the same restricted project; the same contractor or government unit; access to the same non-public data; communications with the same officials; possession of the same disputed technology; or an intention to reveal the same protected information. Timing could strengthen such a link if separate deaths or threats followed identifiable disclosure events involving that common material.

The Ning Li record illustrates why specificity matters. Li and Douglas Torr published work on superconductors and gravitational effects in the 1990s, and a later US Army Aviation and Missile Command agreement awarded AC Gravity $448,970 for a “Gravito-Electro Magnetic Superconductivity Experiment”. The stated objective was experimentally testing predictions associated with Li's theory. This is substantially more concrete than saying merely that Li worked on exotic propulsion: there was an identifiable technology, company, funding arrangement and military office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-gravity" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

But evidence that *Li* had a defence connection is not evidence that another researcher was targeted for knowing what Li knew. To build a common motive around that programme, one would need to connect another death or disappearance to AC Gravity's work, its experimental results, the Army agreement, the same personnel, or closely related protected information. Publicly available evidence located for these cases does not establish that chain.

The distinction is particularly important because Li herself is a cautionary example of how an information gap can be mistaken for a disappearance. Her public scientific profile did diminish sharply, but a 2021 Huntsville obituary documented her death at the age of 79. A 2023 Huntsville Business Journal investigation then contacted her son and reported that she had remained in Huntsville rather than mysteriously vanishing abroad. Whatever uncertainty remains about the later disposition of her research, the earlier narrative that the scientist herself simply “disappeared” was substantially undermined by additional biographical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/guestbook/ning-li" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral HomeNing Li Obituary July 27, 2021 - Berryhill Funeral Home &amp; CrematoryJuly 27, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2021</span></span></span>

That is the standard a common-motive claim must survive: not merely an intriguing absence of public information, but positive evidence connecting the researchers to the same thing someone had a reason to protect.

## Why “advanced science” and government funding are too broad

Research overlap can sound much narrower than it really is. “Antigravity”, for example, can encompass speculative gravity modification, superconducting experiments, theoretical gravitation, unconventional propulsion and historical claims about aerospace projects. “UFO research” is broader still, potentially encompassing witness interviews, radar analysis, atmospheric physics, government-document research and speculation about propulsion.

James E. McDonald demonstrates the problem. The University of Arizona's archive describes his papers as containing correspondence, case material, interviews and government records arising from his investigations of unidentified flying objects and official responses such as [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) and the Condon study. In his 1968 congressional appearance, McDonald was identified as a senior atmospheric physicist, and his testimony concentrated on the scientific treatment of UFO reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lib.arizona.edu/special-collections/collections/james-e-mcdonald-papers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lib.arizona.edu">[University of Arizona Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lib.arizona.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Arizona Libraries James E</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McDonald papersPapers, 1904-1997 (bulk 1958-1971). Mostly correspondence, cases, reports, interviews, and printed materials relating to J...</span></span></span>

That makes McDonald highly relevant to the history of UFO investigation. It does **not** automatically put him inside the same technical programme as Li's later superconducting-gravity experiments. McDonald's surviving archive provides an unusually large body of material against which a more specific connection could in principle be tested. The fact that both careers can be placed beneath a modern umbrella such as “[UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) research” is therefore a starting point for investigation, not evidence of a shared motive.

Eugene Mallove produces an even more revealing category problem. Mallove was an aerospace-trained science writer and a prominent advocate for cold fusion and unconventional energy. Those interests are sometimes grouped with gravity research under expansive labels such as “suppressed energy technology”. Yet cold fusion and superconducting gravity modification are different technical claims. Treating advocacy for one as evidence of access to secrets about the other silently converts a loose cultural association into an asserted operational connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Mallove" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eugene Mallove</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eugene Mallove</span></span></span>

More importantly, Mallove's murder now has substantial case-specific evidence that must be weighed against a research-suppression motive. A 2025 Connecticut appellate decision recounts that Mallove was killed at a Norwich rental property after recently evicting its tenants; the subsequent prosecution involved people connected to those former occupants. Mozelle Brown was convicted of murder and conspiracy to commit murder in 2014, while Chad Schaffer had earlier pleaded guilty to manslaughter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawBrown v. Commissioner of Correction:: 2025:: Connecticut Appellate Court Decisions:: Connecticut Case Law:: Connecticut Law...</span></span></span>

That does not make every question about Mallove's death philosophically impossible. It does, however, create a major evidential burden for anyone claiming that his energy advocacy was part of the same motive behind another researcher's death. A common-motive theory would have to explain why the documented participants and property dispute are insufficient, or demonstrate that those events somehow connect to the alleged research-suppression operation. Simply placing Mallove beside antigravity researchers on a list does neither.

Government involvement presents the same problem. Li's Army-funded prototype research is documented. McDonald interacted with government UFO material and testified publicly before Congress. Huntsville itself is an aerospace and defence research centre. But “had government connections” encompasses an enormous population of scientists, engineers, contractors and academics. It becomes probative only when the **same relevant government connection** appears across cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.zpenergy.com/modules.php?file=article&amp;mode=flat&amp;name=News&amp;order=0&amp;sid=838&amp;thold=-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zpenergy.com">[ZPEnergy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zpenergy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gravity-Waves &amp; Missing Scientists at American Antigravity.Com...</span></span></span>

The analytical danger is therefore denominator blindness. If one starts with deceased or publicly absent researchers and searches backwards for any shared feature — aerospace employment, government funding, unconventional physics, security clearance, UFO interest — some overlap is almost inevitable. The important question is not whether a similarity can be found, but whether that similarity is rare, specific and causally connected to the alleged targeting.

## The Huntsville overlap is real, but what does it establish?

Ning Li and Amy Eskridge provide a much closer comparison than, for example, Li and McDonald. Both were associated with Huntsville, Alabama, and both were publicly connected to gravity-modification ideas. Li had worked at the University of Alabama in Huntsville before forming AC Gravity; Eskridge's obituary states that she graduated from the same university and later became president and chairwoman of the Institute for Exotic Science in Huntsville.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/guestbook/ning-li" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral HomeNing Li Obituary July 27, 2021 - Berryhill Funeral Home &amp; CrematoryJuly 27, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2021</span></span></span>

That geographical and thematic overlap deserves more attention than a generic claim that both were “advanced scientists”. It creates sensible follow-up questions: Did Eskridge possess Li's unpublished experimental results? Did they share collaborators? Did Eskridge's work derive from AC Gravity's protected research rather than merely from the wider antigravity literature? Did the same government office, contractor or individual interact with both women? Is there authenticated correspondence connecting their projects?

Those questions identify precisely what is missing from the stronger version of the common-motive claim. Public documentation confirms Li's research lineage and military-funded experiment, and it confirms Eskridge's involvement with an organisation devoted to exotic science. But thematic and geographical proximity still does not demonstrate shared possession of a particular secret.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.zpenergy.com/modules.php?file=article&amp;mode=flat&amp;name=News&amp;order=0&amp;sid=838&amp;thold=-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zpenergy.com">[ZPEnergy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zpenergy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gravity-Waves &amp; Missing Scientists at American Antigravity.Com...</span></span></span>

The Li case also complicates attempts to interpret every cessation of publication as coercion. Her obituary confirms that she lived until 2021, while the later interview with her son reported a personal and professional history that was far more complicated than the older “missing antigravity scientist” story suggested. The lesson is broader than Li: disappearance from academic publishing, especially after defence work, cannot automatically be converted into evidence of physical disappearance or elimination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/guestbook/ning-li" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral HomeNing Li Obituary July 27, 2021 - Berryhill Funeral Home &amp; CrematoryJuly 27, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2021</span></span></span>

## Disclosure attempts could make timing meaningful

The most potentially important evidence for a common motive would be a repeated sequence in which different researchers independently approached disclosure of the **same identifiable protected material** and then experienced documented threats or harm traceable to the same source.

McDonald did engage in unusually public advocacy. His 1968 congressional testimony argued that the UFO problem deserved serious scientific attention, and his extensive surviving papers document his engagement with government investigations and records. That establishes disclosure activity in a broad sense. It does not, without another evidential step, show that he possessed a particular secret technology later known to Li, Mallove or Eskridge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/1968-07-29-house-symposium-on-ufos-committee-print-7-house-symposium-on-ufos-1968" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">House of Representatives (90th Congress, 2d Sess, No. 7), U.S. House Committee on Science and Astronautics · 1968 · UFO TransparencyJuly...</span></span></span>

Eskridge presents a different kind of timing claim. Material circulating from a 2020 interview records her expressing fears about what could happen to people who attempted to make antigravity work public; her later death in 2022 has consequently become central to suppression theories. Her contemporary obituary independently confirms her date of death, age and leadership of the Institute for Exotic Science, although it does not establish the later claims about the manner or motive of her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufouap.com/case-files/amy-eskridge/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufouap.com">[UFOUAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufouap.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">amy eskridge</span></span></span>

Statements of fear deserve preservation and investigation, especially where they pre-date a death. But they have to be handled carefully. A researcher's belief that unnamed actors are threatening them is evidence that the researcher reported feeling threatened; it is not automatically independent evidence that a particular organisation carried out those threats. The evidential value would rise sharply if contemporaneous messages identified specific people, programmes or disputed information and those details were independently corroborated.

Timing has the same limitation. “Researcher discusses disclosure, then dies” is suggestive chronology. “Several researchers separately prepare to disclose the same document or technology, receive independently authenticated threats from the same intermediary, and are harmed shortly afterwards” would be cross-case evidence. Those are very different propositions.

This distinction also prevents retrospective selection from doing too much work. Once a death is regarded as suspicious, almost any preceding interview, disagreement, funding change or publication can be labelled the event that supposedly triggered it. A convincing motive hypothesis should therefore make predictions that can be checked against records: what information was threatened, who wanted it contained, when they learned disclosure was imminent, and what action followed.

## Broad overlap can actually obscure the strongest leads

Treating every unusual scientific death as part of one suppression programme can make the hypothesis harder rather than easier to test. McDonald's UFO advocacy, Li's superconducting-gravity experiments, Mallove's cold-fusion activism and Eskridge's exotic-science work differ enough that combining them under a single “advanced technology” label risks erasing precisely the distinctions needed to discover a genuine link.

A better approach is to narrow before connecting. Li's documented Army-funded experiment suggests looking for people who had direct access to AC Gravity's work or its results. McDonald's archive makes it possible to look for named government contacts, documents and disclosure plans rather than merely invoking “UFO secrecy”. Mallove's case requires any suppression hypothesis to confront the later criminal convictions and the documented rental-property circumstances. Eskridge's statements make contemporaneous communications and independently corroborated threat reports more valuable than later retellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.zpenergy.com/modules.php?file=article&amp;mode=flat&amp;name=News&amp;order=0&amp;sid=838&amp;thold=-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zpenergy.com">[zpenergy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zpenergy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gravity-Waves &amp; Missing Scientists at American Antigravity.Com...</span></span></span>

The resulting standard is demanding but straightforward. **Shared subject matter is weak evidence; shared sensitive information is stronger. Shared government exposure is weak evidence; the same programme or officials are stronger. Similar timing is weak evidence; independently documented disclosure of the same secret followed by linked threats is stronger.**

On the currently accessible record, there are genuine overlaps worth examining, particularly around Huntsville and gravity-modification research. There are also genuine information gaps, including the limited public record of what followed Li's Army-funded work. But the larger claim — that multiple UFO or antigravity researchers were eliminated for a single common reason — requires a bridge that broad research overlap cannot supply. Until that bridge consists of identifiable programmes, information, actors or corroborated communications, “they all worked on sensitive advanced science” remains a hypothesis-generating observation rather than evidence of a common motive.
