## Introduction

The 17 July 1957 RB-47 incident is important less because it supplies a clean answer to the UFO question than because it shows how difficult an apparently strong military case can become to reconstruct. A six-man US Air Force electronic-reconnaissance crew reported unusual visual and electronic contacts while flying from the Gulf Coast across Louisiana and Texas towards Oklahoma. Ground radar was also reported as tracking an object during part of the episode. Yet [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) did not receive the principal paperwork promptly, the incident was later investigated under the wrong date, some analysts worked without the original file, and the official identification became entangled with a separate American Airlines near-collision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology.patrickgross.org/htm/aiaa1971.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology.patrickgross.org">[Ufology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: AIAA and the UFO problem, the RB-47 case...</span></span></span>

Atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald made the RB-47 case prominent precisely because he believed this fragmented record concealed a much stronger case than official summaries suggested. His later recovery of contemporary documents materially improved the evidence base. It did not, however, turn the episode into a perfectly documented instrument record. The resulting dispute is therefore a useful warning for the wider history of Project Blue Book: classification, administrative delay, incorrect dating and retrospective witness interviews can change what later investigators are actually able to test.

## What the RB-47 crew reported

The aircraft was an RB-47 electronic-reconnaissance version of the B-47 Stratojet, carrying pilot Lewis D. Chase, co-pilot James H. McCoid, navigator Thomas H. Hanley and three electronic-warfare officers: John J. Provenzano, Frank B. McClure and Walter A. Tuchscherer. Its specialised equipment was designed to detect and analyse radar transmissions rather than simply to display reflected targets in the manner of an ordinary search radar. The distinction matters because some of the most disputed evidence concerned a radar-like signal received by the aircraft's passive electronic-countermeasures equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/program/collect/rb-47.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramRB-47July 30, 1997...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 1997</span></span></span>

According to the reconstruction McDonald published after interviewing the crew and finding Air Force documents, McClure first noticed an unusual signal after the aircraft crossed the Mississippi coast. Later, at 1010Z, Chase and McCoid saw an intense bluish-white light approach from roughly the aircraft's eleven-o'clock direction, cross ahead of them and disappear towards the right. The surviving intelligence material records that visual observation and gives the aircraft's position as approximately 32°00′N, 91°28′W, in Louisiana.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

The episode became more significant when the crew associated later visual observations with signals detected by the electronic-warfare equipment. McDonald's account says McClure's receiver detected a strong signal at about 3,000 MHz from approximately the direction in which the light had disappeared. Another operator reportedly checked the same general frequency and bearing. McDonald stressed that, as the RB-47 continued westwards, the indicated source appeared to maintain its relative position rather than behave as the signal from an ordinary fixed ground radar should.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

Near Texas the crew contacted the 745th Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron at Duncanville, whose call sign was "Utah". Ground-radar observations then became part of the case. McDonald's reconstruction describes periods in which the aircrew's visual observation, the airborne direction-finding equipment and the ground radar appeared to correspond. This claimed multi-channel correlation — rather than merely the sighting of a strange light — was why he considered the incident unusually difficult to explain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology.patrickgross.org/htm/aiaa1971.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology.patrickgross.org">[Ufology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: AIAA and the UFO problem, the RB-47 case...</span></span></span>

There are nevertheless limits to how literally the later narrative can be read. McDonald himself acknowledged that crew members differed over details. For example, recollections varied over how far north the phenomenon remained with the aircraft. He also noted that the ground-radar personnel important to reconstructing the Duncanville portion had not been located for his interviews. The evidence is therefore stronger than a story reconstructed solely from one witness's decade-old memory, but weaker than a complete preserved set of raw electronic and radar recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/rb-47-ufo-encounter-july-17-1957/amp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault The RB-47 UFO Encounter,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultThe RB-47 UFO Encounter, July 17, 1957 - The Black Vault Case Files...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 17, 1957</span></span></span>

## How the case reached Blue Book

The documentary chronology explains much of the later confusion. McDonald reported locating a three-page teletype message from the 745th squadron at Duncanville, filed at 1557Z on 17 July itself. He also found a four-page summary prepared by E. T. Piwetz, the intelligence officer of the 55th Strategic Reconnaissance Wing, following an August request from Air Defense Command. A 12-page Airborne Observer's Data Sheet was prepared by Chase on 10 September. These documents are important because they place substantial elements of the account much closer to the event than the interviews conducted a decade later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology.patrickgross.org/htm/aiaa1971.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology.patrickgross.org">[Ufology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: AIAA and the UFO problem, the RB-47 case...</span></span></span>

Project Blue Book, however, was not apparently the immediate destination of this material. McDonald's reconstruction says Air Defense Command forwarded the summary and observer's data to Blue Book only months after the flight; surviving accounts give October and November stages in that process. In other words, an incident involving a Strategic Air Command reconnaissance aircraft, electronic-intelligence equipment and Air Defense Command radar did not begin life as a straightforward Blue Book investigation. It travelled through military intelligence and air-defence channels before becoming a Blue Book case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/rb-47-ufo-encounter-july-17-1957/amp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault The RB-47 UFO Encounter,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultThe RB-47 UFO Encounter, July 17, 1957 - The Black Vault Case Files...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 17, 1957</span></span></span>

Classification further complicated later access. McDonald noted that the principal case-file documents had originally been classified SECRET, although details of the equipment were no longer classified by the time he published his analysis. That history does not by itself demonstrate concealment of a UFO conclusion: an electronic-intelligence aircraft's equipment, capabilities and operational reporting naturally involved sensitive Cold War information. It does mean, however, that later civilian investigators could not assume that everything relevant had originally been routed, indexed and preserved as an ordinary UFO report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

This distinction is especially relevant to claims of suppression surrounding UFO research. The RB-47 record demonstrates a genuine archival problem — sensitive material moving through several military organisations and reaching Blue Book late — without establishing that documents were deliberately withheld in order to hide an extraordinary explanation. Administrative fragmentation and security classification are enough to create substantial gaps in a historical reconstruction.

## Why the wrong date mattered

The most consequential error concerned the date. When the University of Colorado's Air Force-funded UFO study — usually called the [Condon project]({{ 'condon-project/' | relative_url }}) — examined the RB-47 episode in the late 1960s, investigators initially worked from a September 1957 date rather than 17 July. The AIAA UFO Subcommittee's later presentation of the case stated explicitly that the Blue Book file was not located during the Colorado investigation because of the dating error and that radar-propagation and weather work was performed for 19 September rather than 17 July.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

That was not a trivial clerical mistake. Weather conditions are central to evaluating whether anomalous propagation — unusual bending of radar or radio waves through the atmosphere — could produce misleading returns. An atmospheric analysis for the wrong night cannot reliably establish what propagation conditions existed during the actual encounter. Likewise, searches for flight movements, radar operations and other possible conventional sources become unreliable if investigators begin with the wrong day.

The error also affected how the evidential quality of the case was judged. Without the contemporary file, Colorado investigator Roy Craig concluded that evaluation depended essentially upon crew recollections roughly ten years after the event and that these were insufficient for identification. Gordon Thayer's technical treatment likewise emphasised the shortage of information and left the case tentatively unidentified. Those were defensible cautions on the material then available to them — but McDonald's subsequent recovery of the 1957 documents showed that the premise that no contemporary report survived was incorrect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

McDonald was himself initially caught by the dating problem. An earlier version of his discussion described the episode as occurring on 19–20 September 1957. His later work corrected it to 17 July after the case documents were found. That sequence is revealing: the error was not simply a mistake by one hostile investigator. Even a researcher intensely interested in the case could inherit a wrong chronology when records, memories and filing information failed to line up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/rb47.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: RB-47 radar visual multiple witnesses cases, July 17, 1957...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 17, 1957</span></span></span>

## The airliner identification created another paper trail problem

Blue Book's disposition adds a separate complication. Its file associated the RB-47 case with American Airlines Flight 655 and treated an airliner as the identification. Yet Flight 655 was involved in a different incident on 17 July: a near-collision in the El Paso region. Later documentation identified the other aircraft in that encounter as American Airlines Flight 966.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2014/02/the-rb-47-case-american-airlines-and.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin RandleA Different Perspective: The RB-47 Case, American Airlines and Original SourcesFebruary 21, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 21, 2014</span></span></span>

This matters because the geographical and chronological distinction makes it difficult to treat the American Airlines episode as a complete explanation of what the RB-47 crew reported hundreds of miles away. Research into the original airline incident found a November 1957 Department of Commerce letter stating that the Flight 655 near-collision occurred near El Paso and that the initially unidentified object had been established as Flight 966. The existence of a genuine, initially unexplained airline encounter on the same date therefore appears to have helped create an enduring documentary conflation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2014/02/the-rb-47-case-american-airlines-and.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin RandleA Different Perspective: The RB-47 Case, American Airlines and Original SourcesFebruary 21, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 21, 2014</span></span></span>

That does not establish that every RB-47 observation represented a single extraordinary object. It establishes something narrower but historically important: the identification written into the Blue Book record cannot simply be quoted as though the file contained an uncontested demonstration that Flight 655 accounted for the whole RB-47 sequence. The separate airline incident needs to be disentangled before the remaining evidence can even be assessed.

## Why later explanations remain disputed

Once McDonald recovered the documents, the debate changed from "there is only a ten-year-old recollection" to a much harder question: what combination of ordinary phenomena, equipment behaviour and witness interpretation could produce the recorded sequence?

McDonald treated the correspondence between visual observations, passive electronic detection and ground radar as the core difficulty. His argument did not rest simply on the credibility of military witnesses. It rested on the claimed independence of the channels: an optical misperception does not automatically explain an electronic signal, while a receiver anomaly does not automatically explain a ground-radar return. His 1971 AIAA presentation therefore portrayed the RB-47 incident as an example of why scientifically interesting cases could disappear beneath inadequate investigation or premature identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

Sceptical analyst Philip J. Klass subsequently proposed a composite conventional explanation rather than accepting McDonald's interpretation of a single anomalous source. His analysis considered such possibilities as ground-radar signals received by the electronic equipment, ordinary astronomical or meteor phenomena and aircraft. The technical dispute has continued because the answer depends heavily on reconstructing the aircraft's exact position, the characteristics and operation of contemporary radar installations and what precisely the RB-47 equipment displayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/projectbluebook/ProjectBlueBook-RB47-July17-1957.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents2.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents2.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault•</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">• • - • PROJECT 10073 RECORD CARD •...</span></span></span>

The frequency itself illustrates the problem. McDonald's published reconstruction refers repeatedly to a signal of about 3,000 MHz and describes the RB-47's ALA-6/APR-9 system as equipment intended to intercept radar transmissions. That places ordinary S-band ground radar squarely among the hypotheses that must be examined rather than dismissed. But explaining an intercepted radar signal is not automatically equivalent to explaining every visual and ground-radar observation attributed to the episode. The central historical question is whether later accounts correctly correlated those separate observations in time and bearing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

This is precisely where the missing evidence matters. There is no surviving continuous raw record that allows a modern analyst simply to replay the encounter. The case instead combines contemporary intelligence summaries, an observer questionnaire, reported ground-radar information, technical descriptions and interviews conducted years later. Each has evidential value, but they are not interchangeable.

## What can safely be concluded from the records


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The strongest conclusion from the RB-47 file is methodological rather than exotic. There really was a documented military incident on 17 July 1957; substantial contemporary paperwork survived; and McDonald's archival work corrected an important misunderstanding created when investigators were using the wrong date and could not find that material. The episode therefore supports his criticism that the official UFO record could be more complicated than a Blue Book identification card suggested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology.patrickgross.org/htm/aiaa1971.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology.patrickgross.org">[Ufology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: AIAA and the UFO problem, the RB-47 case...</span></span></span>

</div>

At the same time, the archival history weakens claims of certainty in either direction. The recovered documents make it inappropriate to dismiss the episode merely as an unsupported story remembered a decade later. Conversely, incomplete preservation, absent raw radar recordings, unlocated ground-radar witnesses, discrepancies in recollection and the difficulty of reconstructing the precise electronic environment prevent the surviving file from demonstrating what the phenomenon actually was. The Condon-era conclusion that the case was puzzling or unidentified is therefore better understood as a statement about unresolved evidence than as proof of an extraordinary craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/en1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RB - 47 Case. July,1971...</span></span></span>

For the broader history of McDonald's conflict with Project Blue Book, that distinction is crucial. The RB-47 case offers concrete evidence for his argument that delayed reporting, weak file retrieval and premature or confused identifications could obstruct serious retrospective analysis. It does **not** supply documentary evidence that officials deliberately destroyed or concealed the case to suppress researchers, still less that such suppression extended to suspicious deaths. What survives instead is a particularly instructive example of a Cold War case becoming harder to judge as sensitive military reporting, Blue Book administration, mistaken dates and later reconstruction became layered on top of one another.
