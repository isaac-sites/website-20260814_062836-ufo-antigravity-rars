## Introduction

Reports that Amy Eskridge believed she was being attacked with directed radio-frequency or microwave energy are among the most striking claims surrounding the Huntsville researcher’s [final months]({{ 'final-months/' | relative_url }}). The public record does establish that Eskridge was filmed describing an apparent attack, that photographs circulated showing redness or lesions she attributed to it, and that messages attributed to her discussed a possible radio-frequency emitter. What it does **not** establish is that a directed-energy weapon caused those injuries. No publicly available medical examination, exposure measurement, forensic analysis of her home, authenticated device evidence or law-enforcement finding has demonstrated such an attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/uncovered-video-ufo-scientist-amy-eskridge-investigation-1793250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">th &#124; IBTimes UKApril 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2026</span></span></span>

That distinction matters because directed-energy technology is real. The US military openly develops high-energy laser and high-power microwave systems, and sufficiently intense radio-frequency energy can produce heating and burns. But the existence of that technological category cannot authenticate a particular alleged exposure. Eskridge’s photographs, video and statements document **what she reported experiencing**; without case-specific physical evidence, they cannot establish what produced it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46925" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 11, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 11, 2024</span></span></span>

## What Eskridge reportedly believed was happening

The most direct material is a video that became widely reported in April 2026. In it, Eskridge shows her hands and says they had been badly burned while she was typing. She points towards windows and says she believed someone could “beam” energy through them. She had apparently tried blocking a window and reported feeling physical relief afterwards. Contemporary coverage of the resurfaced footage describes visible redness or apparent burns on her hands, but does not identify any contemporaneous clinical diagnosis establishing their cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/uncovered-video-ufo-scientist-amy-eskridge-investigation-1793250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">th &#124; IBTimes UKApril 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2026</span></span></span>

The account becomes more technically specific in messages later publicised by retired British Army officer Franc Milburn. According to reporting based on those messages, Eskridge said that a member of her research circle whom she described as having weapons expertise had examined both her injured hands and a mark on a window. She reported his opinion that an “RF k-band emitter” powered by several car batteries and operated from an SUV was a possible explanation. Later accounts attributed to Milburn also described photographs of injuries to her hands and other parts of her body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://localnews.com/2026/04/22/read-the-chilling-texts-ufo-linked-scientist-sent-before-being-found-dead-that-raise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: localnews.com">[localnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">localnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Local NewsRead the chilling texts UFO-linked scientist sent before being found dead that raise – Local NewsApril 22, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 22, 2026</span></span></span>

This material establishes several propositions of genuine relevance to the case: Eskridge was describing alleged directed-energy targeting before her death; the allegation was not invented wholly after 2022; she apparently photographed physical symptoms she associated with it; and she discussed a proposed RF mechanism with other people.

It does not establish the proposed mechanism itself. The identification of a K-band emitter is reported through Eskridge’s account of another person’s assessment. The supposed operator, vehicle, emitter and power system have not been publicly produced or independently identified. Nor has a technical examination demonstrating RF damage to the window been published.

That evidential distinction is easily lost when later accounts shorten “Eskridge believed an associate had identified a possible RF emitter” into “Eskridge was attacked with a microwave weapon”. Those are materially different claims.

## What the photographs and video can actually establish

Photographs can be strong evidence that an injury existed while still being weak evidence of its cause. Eskridge’s images and video may therefore matter without proving a directed-energy attack.

At their strongest, the surviving visual material can potentially document the appearance, distribution and timing of visible skin changes. It can also corroborate that Eskridge was concerned about those symptoms at the time. What an ordinary photograph generally cannot determine by itself is whether redness, blistering or another lesion resulted from radio-frequency heating rather than thermal contact, inflammation, vascular changes, chemical exposure, infection, dermatological disease or another cause.

That limitation is particularly important here because there is no publicly available contemporaneous dermatology report, biopsy, thermal-injury assessment or other medical record tying the photographed abnormalities to electromagnetic exposure. News reports describe the images as burns, lesions or apparent burns because that was how Eskridge or associates interpreted them; those descriptions should not be mistaken for a forensic diagnosis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/uncovered-video-ufo-scientist-amy-eskridge-investigation-1793250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[ibtimes.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">th &#124; IBTimes UKApril 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2026</span></span></span>

The window claim has the same problem. A scorch-like or otherwise unusual mark on glass could become important physical evidence if the pane were preserved and examined. Investigators could potentially characterise its material changes, exclude ordinary heat or mechanical causes and compare the damage with experimental predictions for the alleged exposure. Public reporting instead provides photographs and witness recollections. There is no published laboratory analysis showing that the window experienced high-power RF irradiation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://localnews.com/2026/04/22/read-the-chilling-texts-ufo-linked-scientist-sent-before-being-found-dead-that-raise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: localnews.com">[Local News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">localnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Local NewsRead the chilling texts UFO-linked scientist sent before being found dead that raise – Local NewsApril 22, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 22, 2026</span></span></span>

The video adds something photographs cannot: it records Eskridge’s own contemporaneous interpretation and her attempt to respond to what she perceived as the source. But her report of feeling better after blocking the window does not independently identify the physical cause. Without measurements showing that the barrier altered an RF field, the before-and-after sensation cannot establish that electromagnetic radiation was present.

## Could radio-frequency energy cause injuries of this kind?

In principle, sufficiently intense radio-frequency energy can affect human tissue. The relevant question is therefore not whether electromagnetic energy can ever cause injury, but whether there is evidence that such an exposure occurred in Eskridge’s home.

The 2020 National Academies review of the illnesses reported by US personnel in Havana and elsewhere provides useful technical context. It distinguished lower-level RF exposure from sufficiently high exposures capable of producing significant heating and noted that high-level exposure can cause thermal effects, including burns. The committee also concluded that the distinctive acute symptoms reported by some US government personnel were consistent with directed, pulsed RF energy, while emphasising major uncertainties about the source and circumstances of any putative exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25889/chapter/6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalacademies.org">[National Academies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalacademies.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalacademies.org.</span></span></span>

That finding is frequently invoked in discussions of Eskridge because she reportedly referred herself to “Havana Syndrome”. But it does not provide forensic evidence for her case. The National Academies committee was examining a different population with a particular cluster of acute symptoms, including directional sound or pressure sensations, dizziness, tinnitus, vertigo and cognitive problems. Its assessment concerned the plausibility of a mechanism for those cases, not a diagnostic test by which any unexplained burn or neurological complaint could be classified as an RF attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK566407/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Later US intelligence assessments also complicate any simple equation of anomalous health incidents with secret microwave attacks. In 2023, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence reported that most US intelligence agencies considered it “very unlikely” that a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported incidents examined by the intelligence community, although agencies expressed differing confidence levels and acknowledged collection gaps. The Government Accountability Office still described anomalous health incidents in 2024 as a poorly understood condition requiring improved treatment and monitoring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2023/3674-dni-statement-on-the-intelligence-community-assessment-on-ahis-1692377389" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[DNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on odni.gov.</span></span></span>

The responsible inference is therefore narrow: directed RF energy is a physically meaningful mechanism worthy of investigation when the evidence warrants it. Neither the National Academies report nor the anomalous-health-incident controversy independently validates Eskridge’s attribution.

## Why the alleged K-band device needs physical evidence

The reported “RF k-band emitter” explanation sounds unusually specific, which can make it appear more evidentially powerful than it is. In reality, technical specificity creates additional questions that should be testable.

K-band broadly refers to microwave frequencies, but naming a frequency range says little by itself about whether a device could produce the alleged injury. Investigators would need to know its radiated power, antenna design and gain, pulse characteristics, distance from the target, duration of exposure, beam geometry and what materials stood between emitter and person. If the proposed source were outside a building, transmission and reflection through the particular window and surrounding structure would also matter.

Power supply is another unresolved part of the allegation. The claim that several vehicle batteries could power an emitter does not demonstrate that such a weapon existed at the scene. A complete system would require equipment to generate and control the RF output and an antenna capable of delivering the required field towards the intended target. The military’s genuine high-power microwave systems illustrate why the distinction matters: operational directed-energy equipment consists of engineered power, thermal-management, beam-control and targeting systems, not merely an energy source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46925" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 11, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 11, 2024</span></span></span>

The Congressional Research Service describes high-power microwave weapons primarily in roles such as disabling electronics and communications or countering unmanned aircraft. Publicly documented US systems include THOR, Phaser and other substantial military demonstrators. CRS also stresses that directed-energy systems face practical constraints involving power, cooling, beam quality, atmospheric effects and technological maturity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46925" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 11, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 11, 2024</span></span></span>

None of that makes a smaller clandestine RF device impossible. It does mean that moving from “RF weapons exist” to “this particular portable emitter produced Eskridge’s injuries through her window” requires evidence about the alleged device and exposure conditions that has not appeared publicly.

## Why military directed-energy programmes do not prove Eskridge was targeted

There is no serious dispute that the United States develops directed-energy weapons. The Congressional Research Service defines them as systems using concentrated electromagnetic energy to incapacitate, damage, disable or destroy equipment, facilities or personnel. US research stretches back decades, and operational or experimental programmes now include both high-energy lasers and high-power microwave systems. The Navy deployed an operational laser prototype aboard USS *Ponce* in 2014, while the Air Force and Army have pursued multiple microwave and laser counter-drone programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46925" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 11, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 11, 2024</span></span></span>

The Department of Defense requested $789.7 million for directed-energy programmes in fiscal year 2025, according to CRS. A January 2026 CRS update also described continuing plans to raise laser power levels and improve the size and weight of future systems. Directed-energy weapons are therefore not speculative technology in the broad sense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46925" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 11, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 11, 2024</span></span></span>

But this is precisely where a common evidential error enters the Eskridge narrative. Establishing that a government or defence contractor possesses a class of technology proves capability at a general level; it does not prove deployment against a named individual. The same principle applies to conventional surveillance equipment, cyber tools or firearms. Capability evidence becomes case evidence only when something connects that capability to the event being investigated.

For Eskridge, no publicly available evidence currently supplies that bridge. There is no identified emitter recovered near her home, RF measurement recorded during an alleged episode, authenticated surveillance showing an operator deploying such equipment, technical examination linking the window damage to microwave exposure, medical finding specific to such exposure, or publicly released investigative conclusion attributing her injuries to directed energy.

Later headlines suggesting that Pentagon acknowledgement of directed-energy weapons “confirmed” or vindicated Eskridge’s story therefore go further than the evidence allows. The Pentagon’s possession and development of directed-energy systems establishes that the general technology exists; it does not retrospectively establish what happened inside Eskridge’s home.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11882" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Primer: Directed-Energy Weapons &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Primer: Directed-Energy Weapons &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span></span></span>

## What would materially strengthen or weaken the claim?

Because the mechanism is potentially testable, this part of the Eskridge case need not remain an argument over whether directed-energy weapons are “real”. The important question is whether evidence specific to the alleged incidents survives.

The strongest missing material would include contemporaneous medical records documenting the injuries; original photographs with reliable dates and metadata; the original video files; any preserved window or other material allegedly damaged during an exposure; police or incident reports made at the time; contemporaneous RF-spectrum measurements; security-camera or vehicle footage; and testimony from the weapons specialist Eskridge reportedly consulted. A technical reconstruction would also need the geometry of the room, window construction, proposed emitter position and plausible RF output.

Such evidence could cut both ways. A medical assessment identifying an ordinary cause for the lesions, for example, would substantially weaken the directed-energy interpretation. Conversely, a properly preserved physical sample displaying damage difficult to reproduce by conventional causes, combined with contemporaneous RF measurements or evidence of an emitting device nearby, would make the allegation significantly harder to dismiss.

The crucial point is that Eskridge’s own records are potentially **leads**, not conclusions. Her video can establish that she believed she was experiencing an attack. Her photographs can establish that visible physical abnormalities may have existed. Her messages can preserve the proposed mechanism and perhaps identify people who could be interviewed. Those are legitimate reasons for investigators to examine the claim; none substitutes for the missing forensic link.

## The unresolved evidence gap

Eskridge’s directed-energy allegations should neither be presented as established fact nor dismissed simply because the mechanism sounds unusual. Military high-energy laser and high-power microwave systems demonstrably exist, and scientific literature recognises that sufficiently intense RF exposure can produce biological effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46925" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 11, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 11, 2024</span></span></span>

The harder question is whether any such technology was used against Amy Eskridge. On the public evidence, that remains unproven. Her statements, photographs and reported messages provide unusually concrete documentation of what she believed was happening before her death, making the directed-energy story more than a wholly retrospective rumour. Yet the crucial chain of proof — **injury → electromagnetic exposure → deliberate emitter → identifiable operator** — has not been publicly established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/uncovered-video-ufo-scientist-amy-eskridge-investigation-1793250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[ibtimes.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">th &#124; IBTimes UKApril 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2026</span></span></span>

That gap is particularly important within the wider dispute over Eskridge’s death. Evidence that she feared directed-energy attacks helps reconstruct her reported state of concern and the harassment narrative she communicated to associates. It does not by itself establish that those fears reflected an actual attack, identify a perpetrator, or demonstrate a causal connection between the alleged harassment and her death. Until medical, forensic or technical records provide that missing connection, the directed-energy component of the Eskridge case remains a documented allegation attached to a technically possible class of weapons — not demonstrated evidence that one was used against her.
