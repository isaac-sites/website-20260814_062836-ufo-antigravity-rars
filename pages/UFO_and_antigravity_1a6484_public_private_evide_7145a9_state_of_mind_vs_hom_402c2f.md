## Introduction

In claims about suspicious deaths among UFO, [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) and unconventional-propulsion researchers, a warning written before death can look extraordinarily powerful: *I am being watched; people are threatening me; if I die, do not believe it was suicide.* Such a statement may be authentic, contemporaneous and deeply important. But it answers a different question from whether homicide occurred.

The central distinction is between **evidence of a person's [state of mind]({{ 'state-of-mind/' | relative_url }})** and **independent evidence of the events that person feared**. A genuine message can establish that its author expressed fear, expected harm or rejected the possibility of suicide. It does not, by itself, establish that surveillance actually occurred, identify who was responsible, demonstrate a connection to the person's research, or prove that another person caused the subsequent death. US evidence law explicitly reflects this distinction: statements about a person's then-existing mental or emotional condition can be evidence of that condition, while statements of belief generally cannot simply be used to prove that the believed event happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_803" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Legal Information InstituteRule 803. Exceptions to the Rule Against Hearsay &#124; Federal Rules of Evidence &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Informatio...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters particularly in the case of Amy Eskridge, the Institute for Exotic Science co-founder whose 2022 death has become central to recent allegations about researchers in advanced propulsion and related fields. Her death at 34 and her leadership of the Huntsville institute are independently documented. More recent reporting attributes to her striking pre-death messages denying that she would kill herself and describing harassment or threats. Those statements justify questions. They do not, without corroborating evidence, supply the missing proof of homicide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022</span></span></span>

## What contemporaneous fear can establish

A message created before a death has an important advantage over a story reconstructed afterwards: it fixes at least part of the evidence in time. If investigators can authenticate a text, recording or email, they can establish that the person communicated those words at or before a particular point. That can defeat the suggestion that the entire allegation was invented only after the death.

Eskridge provides a concrete example. Reporting in 2026 reproduced a message attributed to her and dated 13 May 2022, less than a month before her death, in which she reportedly said that if a report appeared saying she had killed herself, it should not be believed. Reports citing Franc Milburn, who says he corresponded with her, also describe her alleging harassment and intimidation intended to disrupt her work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/amy-eskridge-death-investigation-1794631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Times UK...</span></span></span>

Taken at face value and assuming authentication, such material can support several propositions. It can show that Eskridge was discussing the possibility of her own death before it happened; that she represented herself as unwilling to die by suicide; and that she associated the danger she perceived with hostile outside activity. Other reported communications may help establish whether these concerns were isolated remarks or part of a sustained pattern. Recent accounts, for example, attribute to her descriptions of stalking, threatening communications, physical injuries and other incidents she interpreted as attacks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/resurfaced-video-amy-eskridge-death-questions-1793252" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">resurfaced video amy eskridge death questions 1793252</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Business Times UKScientist Amy Eskridge&#x27;s Death Raises Questions After Being Warned &#x27;Go Public and Be Killed&#x27; in 2019 Video...</span></span></span>

That is meaningful evidence. It would be a mistake to dismiss it merely because it concerns someone's perceptions. State of mind can matter greatly in reconstructing a death. Investigators routinely collect information about a deceased person's medical and personal history alongside physical and scene evidence when determining cause and manner of death. The National Institute of Justice describes medicolegal death investigation as combining examination of the body and scene with relevant background information rather than treating any single category as sufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/forensics/overview-forensic-pathology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeOverview of Forensic Pathology &#124; National Institute of JusticeDecember 12, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 12, 2024</span></span></span>

But the evidential claim must remain precisely stated. A contemporaneous message saying *I believe someone is threatening me* is direct evidence that the writer made that assertion. It is not direct evidence that the suspected person actually issued the threat. A statement saying *I would never kill myself* is evidence relevant to the writer's expressed intentions at that moment. It cannot make suicide physically impossible at a later moment, and it does not establish that somebody else caused the death.

This is not merely a sceptical convention devised for unusual cases. Federal Rule of Evidence 803(3) embodies almost exactly this boundary. It permits certain statements about a person's then-existing state of mind, emotion, intent or plan while generally excluding statements of memory or belief when offered to prove the event remembered or believed. The accompanying explanation identifies the underlying danger: otherwise, evidence that someone held a belief could become a route for inferring that whatever produced the belief must therefore have happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_803" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Legal Information InstituteRule 803. ## Where the inference goes beyond the message

Suspicious-death narratives can compress several distinct propositions into one dramatic conclusion. In the Eskridge discussion, for example, the reasoning sometimes effectively becomes: she said she was threatened; she said she would not kill herself; she subsequently died in circumstances reported as suicide; therefore the people threatening her murdered her.

Each step requires its own evidence.

The first question is **authentication**. Did the deceased actually create the message or recording, and is the version now circulating complete and unaltered? [Screenshots]({{ 'screenshots/' | relative_url }}) are potentially useful evidence, but stronger authentication could come from the original device, account data, metadata, recipient's device, backups or mutually corroborating copies. The importance of authentication grows when material reaches the public years after the death or passes through several intermediaries.

The second question is **[corroboration]({{ 'corroboration/' | relative_url }}) of the alleged threat**. If a person reports being followed, investigators can look for CCTV, vehicle records, witnesses, telephone location information or repeated appearances by an identifiable person. If they report threatening calls, investigators can seek call records, recordings and subscriber information. If they report injuries caused by an attack, contemporaneous medical documentation, photographs with reliable provenance and forensic examination can help distinguish an externally caused injury from [competing explanations]({{ 'competing-explanations/' | relative_url }}).

The third is **attribution**. Even proof that harassment occurred would not automatically identify its source. Establishing that a researcher was genuinely followed is different from establishing that the follower represented a defence contractor, intelligence service, commercial rival or any organisation connected with UFO or propulsion research.

The fourth — and largest — step is **causation of death**. Evidence that someone threatened a researcher months earlier may establish motive, opportunity or a relevant investigative lead. To demonstrate homicide, however, it must eventually connect with the death itself: the scene, injuries, weapon or other mechanism, forensic findings, timeline, communications, witnesses, access and conduct of any suspected perpetrator. The National Institute of Justice's death-investigation guidance emphasises systematic documentation and evaluation of both the scene and the body, while forensic pathology uses post-mortem findings to establish the medical cause and manner of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/death-investigation-guide-scene-investigator-2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">death investigation guide scene investigator 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeDeath Investigation: A Guide for the Scene Investigator, 2024 &#124; National Institute of Justice...</span></span></span>

This produces a useful evidential ladder:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **The person expressed fear.**
2. **The person sincerely believed the threat was real.**
3. **An objectively verifiable threatening event occurred.**
4. **A particular person or organisation caused that event.**
5. **That actor had a connection to the research in question.**
6. **That actor caused the person's death.**

</div>

Evidence supporting one rung can make investigation of the next entirely reasonable. It does not eliminate the need to establish it.

## “I would never kill myself” is important, but not dispositive

A pre-death denial of suicidal intent deserves particular care because it seems to anticipate the very explanation later given for the death. Its evidential value is real: depending on authenticity, timing and context, it may be relevant to whether a suicide determination fits what was known about the deceased.

Its limitations are equally important. Intent is not permanently fixed. A truthful statement about one's intentions on one date does not establish one's intentions weeks later. Nor does a person's confidence about their future behaviour make alternative outcomes impossible. Conversely, it would also be unsound to assume that such a warning is meaningless simply because a death was classified as suicide. The appropriate question is whether the warning is consistent or inconsistent with the *totality* of the evidence.

The same caution applies in the opposite direction. A medical examiner's classification does not itself settle every legal question. A major 2025 National Academies review of the US medicolegal death-investigation system stresses that **cause of death** and **manner of death** are different concepts. Manner — categories such as natural, accident, suicide, homicide or undetermined — is a medicolegal classification rather than a legal verdict. The review also notes variation in how such classifications are reached and warns against treating “homicide” in death certification as synonymous with criminal guilt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK620155/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strengthening the U.S. Medicolegal Death Investigation System - NCBI BookshelfDecember 15, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2025</span></span></span>

The evidential discipline therefore works both ways. A researcher saying “I will not kill myself” does not prove murder. Equally, merely citing “suicide” as a reported official conclusion cannot substitute for examining the underlying forensic basis when that basis is genuinely in dispute. The strongest assessment comes from comparing the contemporaneous statements with the physical and investigative record.

## What would connect a perceived threat to the death?

The decisive evidence in a genuine homicide investigation would be material capable of crossing the gap between **fear before death** and **another person's involvement in the death**. Professional death investigation is designed around precisely this kind of convergence. Investigators document the scene and body, reconstruct timelines, obtain relevant background information and preserve material for forensic analysis; forensic pathologists use autopsy and scene findings to determine how injuries or disease produced death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/death-investigation-guide-scene-investigator-2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">death investigation guide scene investigator 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeDeath Investigation: A Guide for the Scene Investigator, 2024 &#124; National Institute of Justice...</span></span></span>

For a case involving alleged targeting of an unconventional-propulsion researcher, particularly valuable evidence would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Original communications and metadata** showing who issued identifiable threats, when they were sent and whether they continued near the time of death.
* **Independent corroboration of surveillance or attacks**, such as CCTV, access logs, witnesses, telephone or vehicle records and contemporaneous police or medical reports.
* **Scene and weapon evidence** capable of testing whether the physical circumstances fit self-infliction, accident or another person's involvement.
* **Autopsy, toxicology and other forensic findings**, interpreted alongside the scene rather than in isolation.
* **A defensible timeline**, including the deceased's communications and movements and the whereabouts or access of any suspected actor.
* **Evidence connecting the alleged aggressor to the research**, rather than assuming such a connection because the deceased worked on unusual technology.
* **Evidence connecting that same actor to the fatal event**, which is the step required to transform evidence of harassment or motive into evidence of homicide.

</div>

This distinction also explains why an absence of public records should not be converted into affirmative evidence of a cover-up. In Alabama, [death certificates]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }}) remain restricted for 25 years except to specified eligible people. State law also provides that law-enforcement investigative reports, field notes, witness statements and related investigative materials are not public records and are protected from routine disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alabamapublichealth.gov.</span></span></span>

Consequently, outsiders examining a recent Alabama death may genuinely lack records that would answer important questions. That creates an **information gap**, not evidence that the missing records contain proof of homicide. It also means that online assessments based principally on messages and secondary reporting can be much less complete than the records available to investigators or authorised family members.

## Why the distinction strengthens rather than dismisses scrutiny

Separating state-of-mind evidence from proof of homicide does not require treating allegations of threats as fantasy. The correct evidential response to a credible warning is investigation and corroboration.

Eskridge's reported communications are noteworthy precisely because, if authenticated, they pre-date her death and contain unusually explicit claims about what she believed was happening to her. Her obituary independently establishes that she died on 11 June 2022 at 34 and that she was president and chairwoman of the Institute for Exotic Science in Huntsville. Later reports attribute to her pre-death messages expressly rejecting suicide and describing harassment. Those are legitimate facts and claims to investigate rather than retroactively invented thematic similarities between an unrelated researcher and the UFO field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022</span></span></span>

What remains unresolved in the publicly accessible evidence is the crucial bridge from those statements to homicide. Public reporting of the messages does not itself establish who, if anyone, carried out the incidents Eskridge described; whether those incidents were connected to her research; or whether another person caused her death. Some 2026 reporting has explicitly acknowledged that no evidence presented there confirmed a direct connection between the alleged threats and her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/resurfaced-video-amy-eskridge-death-questions-1793252" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">resurfaced video amy eskridge death questions 1793252</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Business Times UKScientist Amy Eskridge&#x27;s Death Raises Questions After Being Warned &#x27;Go Public and Be Killed&#x27; in 2019 Video...</span></span></span>

That boundary is essential across the wider subject of allegedly suspicious deaths and disappearances involving UFO, antigravity or advanced-technology researchers. A deceased scientist's documented fear can be one of the most important reasons to examine a case more closely. It can establish chronology, identify leads, reveal possible motives and challenge simplistic retrospective accounts. But it cannot do the work of scene evidence, pathology, corroborated threats, attribution and causation.

The strongest formulation is therefore neither “the warning proves murder” nor “the warning proves nothing”. It is narrower and more useful: **a credible contemporaneous warning establishes evidence about what the person feared and expected; homicide requires independent evidence showing that an external actor actually caused the death**. Keeping those propositions separate is what allows genuinely suspicious evidence to receive scrutiny without turning an unresolved allegation into a conclusion.
