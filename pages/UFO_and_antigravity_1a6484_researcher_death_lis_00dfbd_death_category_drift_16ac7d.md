## Introduction

[Researcher death]({{ 'missing-denominator/' | relative_url }}) lists in UFO, [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) and alleged suppressed-technology narratives often acquire their apparent force by placing fundamentally different outcomes under one heading: **murder, suicide, accidental death, natural death, unexplained death and disappearance**. The resulting category may be described simply as “dead or missing”, “suspicious deaths”, or even “silenced researchers”. Yet those labels quietly remove distinctions that are crucial to deciding whether the cases really share a cause.

The problem is visible in the wave of US claims that attracted federal attention in 2026. PolitiFact traced 14 people being grouped together even though their circumstances ranged from confirmed shootings and reported suicide to apparent accidental overdose, unresolved disappearances, a missing hiker, deaths with no publicly stated cause and a missing person later found dead. It also found that the people did not all work together and were not all scientists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.politifact.com/article/2026/apr/28/missing-dead-scientists-nuclear-weapons-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: politifact.com">[PolitiFact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">politifact.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PolitiFact &#124; Fact-checking claims about missing, dead scientists: Were they researching UFOs, nuclear weapons?April 28, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 28, 2026</span></span></span>

That does not make every case uninteresting or fully explained. It means the proposed pattern has to survive a harder test: **does evidence connect the events after their different outcomes are restored, or does the pattern largely depend on treating every adverse outcome as equivalent?**

Then there are cases for which the cause of death simply has not been publicly established. PolitiFact found no public cause-of-death information for some names included in the 2026 cluster. Absence of publicly available information is an evidential gap, but it is not itself a manner of death and cannot automatically be converted into evidence of foul play.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.politifact.com/article/2026/apr/28/missing-dead-scientists-nuclear-weapons-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: politifact.com">[PolitiFact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">politifact.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PolitiFact &#124; Fact-checking claims about missing, dead scientists: Were they researching UFOs, nuclear weapons?April 28, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 28, 2026</span></span></span>

Finally, disappearance is not a cause of death at all. Anthony Chavez disappeared from Los Alamos in May 2025, prompting searches, examination of surveillance footage and follow-up on public tips. The Los Alamos Police Department described its investigation as an effort to locate a missing person; its initial release said he was not believed to be endangered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.losalamosnm.gov/Community/News-media/Search-Continues-Anthony-Chavez" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: losalamosnm.gov">[Los Alamos, NM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">losalamosnm.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Los Alamos, NMSearch Continues for Missing Person, Anthony Chavez</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Incorporated County of Los Alamos, NMMay 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 19, 2025</span></span></span> Monica Reza likewise disappeared while hiking in California's Angeles National Forest, generating an extensive search.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.com/missing-woman-60-last-seen-hiking-in-california-forest-4-days-ago-concerns-mounting-11761046" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.com">[People.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on people.com.</span></span></span>

Putting all of these people into a single count therefore performs an important conceptual transformation. The numerator no longer means “researchers murdered”, “researchers who died unexpectedly” or even “researchers whose deaths remain unexplained”. It means something closer to **people selected for having experienced any sufficiently concerning adverse event**. That is a much broader category.

## Why each outcome requires a different evidential test

The distinctions matter because each type of case raises a different question. A homicide requires evidence about the perpetrator, motive and relationship to the victim's work. A suspected disguised homicide requires evidence strong enough to overturn or seriously challenge the recorded manner of death. A disappearance requires evidence about what happened after the person was last seen. An unexplained death requires establishing the cause before attributing a motive. A natural death requires affirmative evidence if it is to be reclassified as deliberate killing.

Consider the two confirmed shootings in the 2026 cluster. Loureiro and Grillmair superficially strengthen a “scientists are being murdered” narrative because both were prominent researchers killed within months of one another. But once the investigative evidence is restored, the apparent similarity weakens. Loureiro's suspected killer had a documented connection to him from their earlier studies and was linked to the Brown University shootings; Grillmair's alleged killer was a local man charged in a sequence involving burglary and carjacking. Publicly available evidence has not established that either killing was motivated by secret research, UFO information or antigravity technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-06/brown-campus-shooter-said-he-plotted-attack-for-six-semesters?srnd=homepage-americas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bloomberg.com">[bloomberg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bloomberg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brown Campus Shooter Said He Plotted Attack for ‘Six Semesters’</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brown Campus Shooter Said He Plotted Attack for ‘Six Semesters’ - BloombergJanuary 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2026</span></span></span>

A disappearance demands a different standard again. The absence of a body leaves more possibilities open, which can make the event feel more mysterious, but uncertainty expands the range of explanations rather than proving the most dramatic one. Chavez's disappearance remained under active investigation, with police reporting searches of residences and trails, surveillance review and inter-agency [coordination]({{ 'coordination/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.losalamosnm.gov/Community/News-media/Search-Continues-Anthony-Chavez" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: losalamosnm.gov">[Los Alamos, NM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">losalamosnm.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Los Alamos, NMSearch Continues for Missing Person, Anthony Chavez</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Incorporated County of Los Alamos, NMMay 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 19, 2025</span></span></span> Reza disappeared during a hike and was formally treated as an at-risk missing person, prompting a multi-agency search.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.com/missing-woman-60-last-seen-hiking-in-california-forest-4-days-ago-concerns-mounting-11761046" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.com">[People.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on people.com.</span></span></span> Neither circumstance by itself establishes abduction, murder or a connection to research.

Cases can also move from one category to another as new evidence appears. Melissa Casias, who had been counted among missing people connected to Los Alamos, disappeared in June 2025. Her remains were discovered in Carson National Forest in May 2026, with a handgun nearby. New Mexico State Police said that the cause and manner of death had **not yet been determined** and that the investigation remained active.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://losalamosreporter.com/2026/05/30/new-mexico-state-police-identifies-remains-as-missing-taos-woman-melissa-casias/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: losalamosreporter.com">[Los Alamos Reporter]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">losalamosreporter.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on losalamosreporter.com.</span></span></span> The event therefore changed from “disappearance” to “death under investigation” without thereby becoming a demonstrated homicide.

This illustrates why a rigorous list needs more than a binary column marked “dead/missing”. At minimum, its evidential categories need to preserve whether a person is missing, confirmed dead, the established or provisional manner of death, whether foul play is suspected by investigators, whether a suspect has been identified, and whether there is evidence connecting the event to the person's research.

Otherwise, **uncertainty itself becomes the common denominator**.

## How one label makes unlike events look alike

Aggregation has a powerful visual effect. Imagine a timeline containing ten entries marked with the same red symbol and labelled “SCIENTIST DEAD OR MISSING”. A reader scanning it sees ten repetitions of essentially the same event.

Expand the entries, however, and the structure may look very different:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* one researcher was murdered by somebody with a documented personal connection;
* another was killed during an apparently local criminal episode;
* one death was officially classified as suicide but remains disputed by friends or supporters;
* another was classified as an accidental overdose;
* several people disappeared under different circumstances;
* one missing person was subsequently found dead, with the manner of death still undetermined;
* other deaths have no publicly reported cause.

</div>

That expanded version is not less truthful because it looks less coherent. It is **more informative precisely because it preserves differences that bear directly on causation**. The 14-person group investigated by PolitiFact contained this kind of heterogeneity, while claims circulating online presented the cases collectively as scientists who were “missing, dead or suicided”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.politifact.com/article/2026/apr/28/missing-dead-scientists-nuclear-weapons-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: politifact.com">[PolitiFact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">politifact.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PolitiFact &#124; Fact-checking claims about missing, dead scientists: Were they researching UFOs, nuclear weapons?April 28, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 28, 2026</span></span></span>

The terminology can itself push the reader towards a conclusion. “Suicided”, when used as a transitive verb, implies that an apparent suicide was actually an assassination. “Vanished” can imply intervention where “reported missing” does not. “Mysterious death” can be applied both to a genuinely undetermined death and to a death whose official explanation the compiler simply doubts. Once those terms are combined in a headline or infographic, the proposed explanation can become embedded in the category before the underlying evidence is considered.

This is especially important in [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) narratives because the connecting professional category can be broad as well. The 2026 compilation included people associated with NASA, Los Alamos, the US Air Force, Caltech, MIT, a pharmaceutical company and other government-related workplaces. PolitiFact found that some were scientists, while others held administrative, construction or other roles; it also found no evidence that “nearly all” had worked together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.politifact.com/article/2026/apr/28/missing-dead-scientists-nuclear-weapons-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: politifact.com">[PolitiFact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">politifact.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PolitiFact &#124; Fact-checking claims about missing, dead scientists: Were they researching UFOs, nuclear weapons?April 28, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 28, 2026</span></span></span> The category can therefore expand along **two axes simultaneously**: what counts as a relevant researcher, and what counts as a suspicious outcome.

## The Marconi deaths show the mechanism in an older form

The same problem predates the present UFO and antigravity debate. The much-discussed GEC-Marconi deaths of the 1980s became an enduring example of how a collection can accumulate force through aggregation. Lists associated with the controversy eventually encompassed scientists and engineers who died in circumstances officially described variously as suicides, accidents, misadventure or cases receiving open verdicts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GEC-Marconi_scientist_deaths_conspiracy_theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEC-Marconi scientist deaths conspiracy theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEC-Marconi scientist deaths conspiracy theory</span></span></span>

That history is useful here not because it proves or disproves any particular allegation about Marconi, but because it exposes the classification problem unusually clearly. If one person dies in a road accident, another apparently takes his own life and a third receives an open verdict, counting all three simply as “mysterious scientist deaths” discards precisely the information required to test whether they resulted from a common mechanism.

The effect becomes stronger as lists expand. A death does not have to resemble previous entries in *how* it occurred if it resembles them in some other respect: defence employment, technical expertise, premature death or an unusual circumstance may suffice. Conversely, someone whose profession only loosely fits the group can remain eligible if the death appears sufficiently strange. Different weaknesses can therefore compensate for one another.

This produces a category that is remarkably easy to enlarge. It also makes disconfirmation difficult. If suicide qualifies, a suicide adds to the pattern. If homicide qualifies, a homicide adds to it. If an accident qualifies, an accident adds to it. If no cause is known, uncertainty adds to it. And if the person has not been found, disappearance adds to it too.

The category has effectively become **outcome-insensitive**.

## Why category drift can exaggerate a suppression pattern

There is a statistical consequence as well as a semantic one. Before asking whether an apparent cluster is unusual, a researcher needs to know the relevant population and the event being counted. Ten homicides among 100 closely connected specialists would raise a very different question from ten instances of “death, disappearance, accident or suicide” selected from thousands of present and former employees across numerous scientific institutions over several years.

Base rates matter because unusual-looking observations cannot be evaluated sensibly without considering how frequently the underlying outcomes occur in the population from which cases are being selected. Neglecting those background rates can produce misleading judgements about how surprising a collection of events really is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/behavioral-decision-theory/base-rate-neglect/89A482E9542135608D08AA62E75F1205" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press Base rate neglect (Chapter 8</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press Base rate neglect (Chapter 8</span></span></span>

Category drift makes that problem harder because there is no longer one outcome whose background frequency can readily be compared. The compiler is effectively searching across several possible outcomes simultaneously. Murder can qualify; so can suicide, accident, unexplained death or disappearance. If the professional criterion is also flexible — aerospace, nuclear research, defence contracting, UFO involvement, unconventional propulsion or access to sensitive facilities — there are many possible combinations from which an apparently striking cluster can emerge.

This does not establish that the resulting pattern is coincidental. It establishes something narrower and more important: **the raw number of entries is not enough to show that it is anomalous**. The more ways a case is allowed to qualify, the larger the expected pool of qualifying cases becomes.

The same principle explains why adding cases after noticing a possible pattern requires caution. Statistical research on multiple comparisons addresses the broader problem that searching across many possible relationships increases the opportunity to find apparently noteworthy ones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.2478" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Why we (usually) don&#x27;t have to worry about multiple comparisons</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Why we (usually) don&#x27;t have to worry about multiple comparisons</span></span></span> In a death-list context, the issue is less a formal hypothesis test than a selection problem: the category can be drawn around the cases after the interesting cases have already been noticed.

## A list should preserve uncertainty rather than erase it

The strongest way to assess an alleged suppression pattern is therefore not to discard the list, but to **disaggregate it**. Instead of beginning with “How many researchers are dead or missing?”, begin with separate questions: How many were confirmed victims of homicide? How many deaths remain officially undetermined? How many were ruled suicide or accident? How many are genuinely missing? For each group, is there evidence connecting the event to the person's work?

That approach does not require accepting every official conclusion unquestioningly. Official findings can be incomplete or wrong, and unresolved disappearances deserve continued investigation. Amy Eskridge's case, for example, attracts attention partly because reported statements attributed to her before her death conflict sharply with the suicide interpretation accepted in public reporting. That discrepancy is a legitimate subject for examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hindustan TimesAmy Eskridge update: UFO scientist&#x27;s old texts cast doubt on cause of death; ‘would never kill myself’ &#124; Hindustan TimesAp...</span></span></span> But it should be examined as a specific evidential conflict within a specific case, rather than converted automatically into proof that every other death or disappearance belongs to the same mechanism.

The distinction also protects genuinely unresolved cases from being overstated. When investigators say a manner of death has not yet been determined, “undetermined” is meaningful information. Turning it immediately into “murdered scientist” destroys that uncertainty rather than resolving it. Likewise, an active missing-person investigation is evidence of an unresolved disappearance, not evidence that the missing person has been killed.

Even the 2026 congressional response preserved this distinction more carefully than many circulating lists. The House Oversight Committee sought information about reports concerning people who had “died or disappeared”, while the broader federal inquiry concerned whether connections or national-security implications actually existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/release/comer-burlison-seek-information-on-missing-nuclear-and-rocket-scientists/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span> An investigation into possible connections is not confirmation that a single cause has already been established.

## The pattern must survive disaggregation

For claims of researcher suppression, the decisive question is not whether a list contains disturbing events. Several plainly do. Murder is disturbing; unexplained disappearance is disturbing; an unexpected suicide or sudden death can leave serious unanswered questions. The analytical mistake occurs when that emotional similarity substitutes for causal similarity.

A credible common-cause hypothesis should become **stronger, not weaker, when the cases are separated into their real categories**. If researchers were deliberately being killed because of particular knowledge, evidence of that mechanism should emerge from the homicide investigations, disputed deaths, communications, threats, professional connections or other independent records. If disappearances form part of the same operation, evidence should connect those disappearances to it as well.

When the apparent pattern depends instead on collapsing homicide, suicide, accident, natural death, undetermined death and disappearance into one interchangeable outcome, much of its coherence has been created by the list itself. The count may remain numerically impressive, but it no longer represents a single observed phenomenon.

That is the central risk of category drift in UFO and antigravity researcher [death lists]({{ 'death-lists/' | relative_url }}): **very different events can be made to resemble repeated instances of one suppression pattern simply by giving them the same label**. Restoring the original classifications does not settle every mystery. It reveals which mysteries are actually alike — and which only looked alike while they were aggregated.
