## Introduction

When [police files]({{ 'police-files/' | relative_url }}), autopsy reports and other primary records are not publicly available, certainty has limits in both directions. In the disputed deaths associated with UFO or unconventional-propulsion research, the absence of accessible documentation does **not** prove homicide, suppression or a cover-up. But it can also make categorical public claims that every suspicious interpretation has been disproved harder to justify.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/UFO_and_antigravity_1a6484_missing_official_rec_eb2571-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Missing Records" width="1536" height="1024" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

Amy Eskridge's 2022 death in Huntsville, Alabama, is the clearest example. Her obituary confirms her death on 11 June 2022 and her role as president of the Institute for Exotic Science, but it gives no cause or manner of death. Later news reports describe a self-inflicted gunshot and a [suicide ruling]({{ 'suicide-ruling/' | relative_url }}), while also acknowledging that detailed official investigative records have not been publicly released.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LegacyAmy Eskridge Obituary (1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. A reported official conclusion is evidence and should not be disregarded. Yet without the underlying records, outsiders cannot independently inspect the forensic reasoning, scene evidence or investigative basis behind it. The defensible public position is therefore narrower than either "she was murdered" or "every question has been conclusively answered".

## Which records matter most?

A death investigation is not a single document. Different records answer different questions, and some are considerably more important than others when competing accounts circulate.

The **death certificate** ordinarily records the certified cause and manner of death, but in Alabama it is not an immediately public document. The Alabama Department of Public Health says [death certificates]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }}) remain confidential for 25 years and can normally be obtained during that period only by specified relatives, legal representatives and certain other authorised people. For a death in June 2022, unrestricted public access would therefore not arise until 2047 under the present rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Public HealthDeath Certificates &#124; Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH)March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

That restriction creates an important but easily misunderstood gap. A member of the public failing to locate Eskridge's death certificate online does not show that no certificate exists or that authorities are concealing one specially in her case. Alabama law itself explains why the document is not freely obtainable.

An **autopsy or forensic examination report**, where one was produced, would be more useful for evaluating disputed physical claims. Such a report can document injuries, wound characteristics, toxicology, internal examination and the pathologist's conclusion about cause and manner of death. The Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences (ADFS) states that its death-investigation service performs forensic examinations and that autopsy reports commonly take about 45 days to complete. It also provides a specific procedure for requesting laboratory and autopsy reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.adfs.alabama.gov/services/death-investigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adfs.alabama.gov">[adfs.alabama.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adfs.alabama.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences...</span></span></span>

Crucially, ADFS says final case reports from its investigations are public records once applicable restrictions no longer prevent release. A certified report cannot be issued while the ADFS case or a related criminal investigation remains pending, and ADFS requires confirmation from the relevant prosecutor or court that no such investigation remains open. Photographs, working notes and other case-specific materials receive more restricted treatment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adfs.alabama.gov/resources/attorneycorner/public-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adfs.alabama.gov">[adfs.alabama.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adfs.alabama.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences...</span></span></span>

A **police incident or investigative file** would address another set of questions: who discovered the body, whether there were signs of forced entry or struggle, what witnesses said, what physical evidence was recovered, and whether investigators examined possible third-party involvement. Alabama's public-records law generally favours access to public writings, but the state Supreme Court has long recognised exceptions, including records connected with pending criminal investigations and material received in confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/alabama/supreme-court/1981/404-so-2d-678-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawStone v. Consolidated Pub. Co.:: 1981:: Supreme Court of Alabama Decisions:: Alabama Case Law:: Alabama Law:: U.S. Law...</span></span></span>

Those legal rules are why "the records are not online" is not a sufficient conclusion. There are at least four very different possibilities: a record may be confidential by statute; it may exist but require a formal request; it may be temporarily withheld because an investigation remains open; or it may never have been produced in the form outsiders assume. Establishing which explanation applies is itself part of responsible verification.

## Why the Eskridge case remains difficult

Eskridge's public record contains a striking mismatch between the strength of later claims and the weakness of publicly accessible primary documentation.

What is firmly documented is comparatively limited. Her published obituary says she died on 11 June 2022 at the age of 34, identifies her as co-founder, chairwoman and president of the Institute for Exotic Science and notes her University of Alabama in Huntsville background. It does not state that she died by suicide, by gunshot or by homicide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LegacyAmy Eskridge Obituary (1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022</span></span></span>

The suicide account appears repeatedly in later reporting. In April 2026, for example, *The Sun* reported that Eskridge had died from a self-inflicted gunshot wound and that the death had been ruled a suicide. The same report, however, explicitly said that details of an official investigation had not been publicly released.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.the-sun.com/news/16236206/scientist-dead-after-danger-warning-trump-vows-answers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: the-sun.com">[The US Sun]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">the-sun.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">space scientist, Amy Eskridge, has been found dead under mysterious circumstances after expressing fears for her life, marking the eleven...</span></span></span> Other 2026 coverage likewise repeated a self-inflicted-gunshot ruling while discussing messages attributed to Eskridge in which she reportedly denied any intention to kill herself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hindustantimes.com.</span></span></span>

Those reported messages deserve to be treated as evidence of what Eskridge allegedly said or feared, not as forensic proof of what caused her death. Even an authentic message saying "I would never kill myself" cannot by itself establish homicide. Conversely, it would be poor reasoning simply to erase such statements from consideration because a death was later classified as suicide. Their proper significance depends on authentication, date, context, the recipient's account and comparison with the physical investigation.

The same problem applies to claims attributed to retired British military and intelligence figure Franc Milburn, who has said Eskridge complained of harassment and possible directed-energy attacks before she died. Public reporting establishes that such allegations have been made; it does not independently establish that the alleged attacks happened or were connected to her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.crbcnews.com/articles/69e9d358941f2f577a2091b7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crbcnews.com">[CRBC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crbcnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on crbcnews.com.</span></span></span>

More sensational formulations go considerably further. Material submitted in connection with congressional UAP discussions has repeated an allegation that Eskridge was killed by a private aerospace company. That is an allegation attributed to a source, not a finding by Congress or a law-enforcement agency. The evidentiary distinction is essential: putting a claim into testimony or a [congressional record]({{ 'congressional-record/' | relative_url }}) preserves the claim; it does not certify its truth.

This is precisely where missing primary records matter. If the public had a complete forensic report, scene report and final investigative summary, readers could compare such allegations against findings about wound trajectory, firearm evidence, toxicology, entry to the premises, fingerprints, DNA, contemporaneous communications and witness statements. Without those materials, much of the online argument proceeds by inference rather than by testing claims against the case file.

## Missing records are not evidence of a cover-up

One of the most common errors in this subject is to treat restricted access itself as suspicious. Alabama's record system provides a concrete reason to resist that inference.

Eskridge's death certificate falls within a statewide 25-year confidentiality rule that applies to recent Alabama deaths generally, not only to researchers involved in unusual technologies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Public HealthDeath Certificates &#124; Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH)March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span> ADFS, meanwhile, openly states that it accepts requests for final forensic reports and explains when investigative status can prevent release.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.adfs.alabama.gov/publicrecords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adfs.alabama.gov">[adfs.alabama.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adfs.alabama.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences...</span></span></span>

That means a lack of public documentation can arise from ordinary governance mechanisms. Privacy law, investigative exemptions, fragmented record custody and the absence of litigation can all reduce the amount of material that becomes readily searchable online.

A useful comparison is a criminal death that generates prosecution and court proceedings. In such a case, autopsy testimony, photographs, witness evidence and investigative details may enter an open court record and later become easy to reconstruct. A death classified as suicide may produce no trial at all. Consequently, there may be far less public documentation even when police and forensic agencies created substantial internal records.

The evidentiary mistake is therefore symmetrical. It is unsound to argue, "There is no public report, therefore officials must be hiding murder." It is equally unsound to argue, "I cannot see evidence of homicide, therefore every imaginable homicide scenario has been affirmatively excluded." The first turns an information gap into positive evidence of conspiracy; the second mistakes lack of public access for complete knowledge of the underlying investigation.

## A 2026 inquiry does not retroactively prove the allegations

Renewed federal interest in deaths and disappearances involving scientists has added another layer of confusion.

On 20 April 2026, the US House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform formally sought information from the FBI, NASA, the Department of Energy and the Department of War concerning public reports about scientists and other personnel connected with sensitive nuclear or rocket work who had died or disappeared. Significantly, the committee itself described the underlying reporting as **"unconfirmed"** and framed its action as an attempt to find out whether any broader national-security risk existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/release/comer-burlison-seek-information-on-missing-nuclear-and-rocket-scientists/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

The committee's letters also noted that the White House had said it would seek information from relevant agencies. That demonstrates genuine governmental interest in checking the reported pattern. It does not demonstrate that the deaths were connected, that Eskridge was murdered, or that any UFO or [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) research prompted violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/DOE-Missing-Scientists-Letter_4.20.26.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DOE Missing Scientists Letter 4.20.26</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOE Missing Scientists Letter 4.20.26</span></span></span>

This distinction is especially important because congressional scrutiny is sometimes presented online as though officials had validated the underlying conspiracy theory. In reality, asking agencies for a briefing is an investigative act, not a factual finding. The committee's own wording shows that the purpose was to assess uncertain public claims rather than endorse them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/release/comer-burlison-seek-information-on-missing-nuclear-and-rocket-scientists/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

The development nevertheless increases the value of primary records. If agencies later publish case-specific conclusions, those materials could narrow uncertainty. Until then, the existence of an inquiry should be counted as evidence that officials considered the reported pattern worth checking — no more and no less.

## How uncertainty should change conclusions

The Eskridge case is best understood by separating three levels of confidence that are often collapsed together.

**First, there are facts supported directly by public records.** Eskridge was a Huntsville researcher associated with unconventional propulsion and gravity-modification interests; she died on 11 June 2022 at 34; and her obituary documents her role in the Institute for Exotic Science.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LegacyAmy Eskridge Obituary (1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022</span></span></span>

**Second, there are conclusions reported as official but not readily independently inspectable through the primary case documents.** Multiple news reports say her death was ruled suicide by self-inflicted gunshot. That repeated reporting deserves meaningful evidential weight. But because the underlying death certificate, full forensic findings and police file are not all openly presented alongside those reports, an outside reader cannot reproduce the official reasoning from primary evidence alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.the-sun.com/news/16236206/scientist-dead-after-danger-warning-trump-vows-answers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: the-sun.com">[The US Sun]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">the-sun.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">space scientist, Amy Eskridge, has been found dead under mysterious circumstances after expressing fears for her life, marking the eleven...</span></span></span>

**Third, there are disputed allegations.** These include claims that Eskridge was threatened, subjected to exotic attacks or murdered because of her research. Some are attributed to identifiable people and therefore should not be described as invented from nothing. But no publicly available official finding located in the record establishes that those allegations caused or explain her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.crbcnews.com/articles/69e9d358941f2f577a2091b7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crbcnews.com">[CRBC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crbcnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on crbcnews.com.</span></span></span>

That produces a narrower conclusion than either side of the controversy often prefers. There is presently insufficient public evidence to justify saying that Eskridge was murdered because of antigravity or UFO-related work. There is also a genuine documentary limitation on how thoroughly independent outsiders can audit the reported suicide determination.

The most accurate language therefore distinguishes **the reported finding** from **the independently verifiable record**: Eskridge's death has been widely reported as an officially ruled suicide, while important underlying investigative documents have not been placed in a complete, easily accessible public case file.

## What would materially change the assessment?

Not every new document would be equally useful. The records most capable of moving the case away from speculation would be those that directly connect physical evidence to the official conclusion.

A certified ADFS autopsy or final death-investigation report, if one exists and is releasable, would be especially important because ADFS describes such reports as formal outputs of its forensic work and provides a public request process for completed cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adfs.alabama.gov/resources/attorneycorner/public-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adfs.alabama.gov">[adfs.alabama.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adfs.alabama.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences...</span></span></span> A police closing report could establish whether investigators considered and excluded third-party involvement. The death certificate could independently confirm the formally certified cause and manner, although Alabama's 25-year restriction makes ordinary public access difficult at present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Public HealthDeath Certificates &#124; Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH)March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

Authenticated copies of Eskridge's alleged pre-death messages would also help, but in a different way. They could clarify what she believed or feared before her death. They could not substitute for forensic evidence about who fired a weapon, how the scene was arranged or whether another person was present.

Likewise, evidence concerning her research should be evaluated separately from evidence concerning her death. Demonstrating that Eskridge genuinely pursued unconventional propulsion would establish motive only in the weakest preliminary sense: it would show that she worked in the field claimed by the theory. A homicide hypothesis would still require evidence linking a particular person or organisation, intent and action to the death itself.

That separation is the key safeguard against circular reasoning. "She researched antigravity, she feared suppression, and she later died" is a sequence of facts and allegations worth examining. It is not, without an evidential bridge, proof that the research caused the death.

## The limit of public certainty

Missing official records do not create a blank cheque for suspicion. They create an obligation to describe uncertainty accurately.

In Eskridge's case, the public record supports a reported suicide determination far more strongly than it supports a proven homicide. At the same time, the absence of a readily accessible, complete police-and-forensic record means the public cannot independently reconstruct every step behind that determination. Alabama's own rules show that at least some of this opacity can result from ordinary confidentiality and investigative procedures rather than extraordinary suppression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Public HealthDeath Certificates &#124; Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH)March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

The 2026 congressional inquiry reinforces the same discipline. Lawmakers did not announce that a [coordinated campaign]({{ 'coordination/' | relative_url }}) against scientists had been discovered; they asked agencies to investigate **unconfirmed** reports and explain whether any connection existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/release/comer-burlison-seek-information-on-missing-nuclear-and-rocket-scientists/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

For claims about suspicious deaths in [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) research, that is the appropriate evidential standard more broadly. Where primary records are available, conclusions should follow them. Where records are legally restricted, unreleased or incomplete, the gap should be identified rather than filled with assumptions. And where neither homicide nor a comprehensive rebuttal can be independently demonstrated from public evidence, the most responsible conclusion is not dramatic certainty but precisely bounded uncertainty.
