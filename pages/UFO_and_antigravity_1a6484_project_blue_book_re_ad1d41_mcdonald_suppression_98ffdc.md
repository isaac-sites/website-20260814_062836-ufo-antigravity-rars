## Introduction

The surviving [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) record supports a substantial part of atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald’s criticism of the US Air Force: the programme was small, its scientific treatment was contested even by scientists close to it, and some case files gave McDonald reasonable grounds to challenge official explanations. What the record does **not** supply is the additional evidence needed to show that the Air Force threatened, silenced or otherwise targeted McDonald personally because of his UFO research.

That distinction is especially important when McDonald is discussed in accounts of suspicious deaths among UFO or unconventional-aerospace researchers. Institutional defensiveness, poor investigation and even efforts to minimise an embarrassing subject are not equivalent to a campaign against an individual. McDonald himself made essentially this distinction. In March 1968, after extensive investigation, he wrote that he saw no convincing evidence of a US conspiracy concealing the true nature of UFOs. His preferred explanation was a less dramatic one: superficial investigation, limited scientific expertise and a strong institutional bias towards explaining reports away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/JamesMcDonald-UFOs-InternationScientificProblem.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents2.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents2.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUFOs - An International Scientific ProblemMarch 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2023</span></span></span>

## What the Blue Book records document about McDonald’s dispute

Project Blue Book was the Air Force's principal UFO-investigation programme from 1952 until its termination in December 1969. The surviving collection is substantial: the US National Archives describes approximately 37 cubic feet of chronological sighting files, two cubic feet of administrative or project material and three cubic feet associated with the Office of Special Investigations. The textual collection was reproduced on 94 rolls of microfilm. The Air Force's final accounting listed 12,618 reports from 1947 to 1969, with 701 remaining classified as unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

Those records matter to the McDonald question because his criticism was based partly on direct access rather than merely on public UFO literature. Accounts from both sides of the dispute agree that he was admitted to the Blue Book operation at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in 1966 and spent considerable time examining its files. Former Blue Book chief Hector Quintanilla, who was openly hostile to McDonald's conclusions, nevertheless recalled that McDonald had access to Blue Book information and spent hours examining the records. A contemporary sceptical historical account likewise describes three visits in June and July 1966 and nine days spent reading case files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/414482004/Hector-Quintanilla-UFOs-An-Air-Force-Dilemma-1975-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hector Quintanilla-UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hector Quintanilla-UFOs - An Air Force Dilemma (1975) PDF &#124; PDF &#124; Unidentified Flying Object &#124; Ufology...</span></span></span>

McDonald subsequently said that he had examined roughly 150 cases during three visits and had spoken directly with Blue Book's military and scientific personnel. His objection was not simply that some cases were labelled unidentified. He argued that supposedly resolved files sometimes contained observations inconsistent with the explanations placed upon them and that follow-up investigation was often inadequate. He told audiences that the discrepancy between the impressive official assurances surrounding Blue Book and the quality he believed he found in its working files was itself the central problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saturdaynightuforia.com/html/articles/articlehtml/ost-macdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saturdaynightuforia.com">[Saturday Night Uforia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saturdaynightuforia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on saturdaynightuforia.com.</span></span></span>

A revealing example was the 1952 Davis-Monthan Air Force Base B-36 incident. J. Allen Hynek later described McDonald's efforts to reconstruct the case after McDonald discovered that a major report he expected to find was missing from the Blue Book material. A surviving July 1966 McDonald letter recorded unsuccessful attempts to locate the original documentation and his effort to obtain recollections from one of the officers involved. Whatever the ultimate explanation of that sighting, the episode illustrates the type of archival deficiency McDonald meant when he complained about Blue Book: incomplete records and questionable classifications could make retrospective scientific evaluation extremely difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/books/The_Hynek_UFO_Report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hynek UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesSPHEREDr. J. This is stronger evidence for **investigative weakness** than for suppression of McDonald. A missing report can have many causes — poor records management, transfer between organisations, incomplete submission, loss or deliberate withholding. Establishing the last explanation requires evidence about who removed or withheld the material, when, and for what purpose. The mere absence of a document does not establish that chain of events.

The broader institutional record also makes McDonald's complaints about Blue Book's scientific standing difficult to dismiss as wholly idiosyncratic. The Air Force itself moved towards an outside university review in the mid-1960s, ultimately sponsoring the University of Colorado study led by Edward Condon. Blue Book was then terminated after consideration of the Colorado report, a National Academy of Sciences review, previous UFO studies and the Air Force's own experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

Yet even that institutional criticism should not be stretched beyond what the documents establish. Blue Book's final position remained that no investigated UFO had been shown to threaten national security, embody technology beyond contemporary scientific knowledge or constitute an extraterrestrial vehicle. Its 701 unidentified cases therefore represented unresolved reports, not official confirmations of exotic craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## McDonald was opposed, but opposition is not suppression

There is clear documentary evidence that McDonald encountered institutional resistance and personal antagonism. Quintanilla's retrospective manuscript is unusually useful precisely because its author plainly disliked McDonald's methods and conclusions. He portrayed McDonald as arriving with a preconceived view, criticised his investigative practices and rejected his charges against Blue Book. This establishes a contentious relationship between the programme's chief and one of its most prominent scientific critics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/414482004/Hector-Quintanilla-UFOs-An-Air-Force-Dilemma-1975-pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hector Quintanilla-UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hector Quintanilla-UFOs - An Air Force Dilemma (1975) PDF &#124; PDF &#124; Unidentified Flying Object &#124; Ufology...</span></span></span>

But the same record complicates the idea that Blue Book was trying to silence McDonald. The Air Force allowed him into the project files. McDonald later recalled that, after he complained directly to Brigadier General Arthur Cruikshank about Blue Book in June 1966, Cruikshank assigned three officers to conduct a rapid review. McDonald regarded the eventual result as inadequate, but access, discussion with senior officers and an internal review are difficult to reconcile with a simple picture in which the institution's overriding objective was to prevent him from examining or voicing criticism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saturdaynightuforia.com/html/articles/articlehtml/ost-macdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saturdaynightuforia.com">[Saturday Night Uforia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saturdaynightuforia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on saturdaynightuforia.com.</span></span></span>

McDonald also remained conspicuously able to voice that criticism in public. In July 1968 he appeared before the House Committee on Science and Astronautics alongside J. Allen Hynek, Carl Sagan and other scientists. The published [congressional record]({{ 'congressional-record/' | relative_url }}) identifies him by his University of Arizona position and preserves his lengthy arguments about UFO evidence and the deficiencies of previous investigation. Committee chairman George P. Miller explicitly framed the symposium as a scientific inquiry rather than an institutional attack on the Air Force, but McDonald's dissent nevertheless entered the public congressional record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/1968-07-29-house-symposium-on-ufos-committee-print-7-house-symposium-on-ufos-1968" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufotransparency.com.</span></span></span>

Nor was he excluded entirely from the Air Force-sponsored Colorado study. The final [Condon Report]({{ 'condon-report/' | relative_url }}) acknowledged that McDonald was conducting a concurrent investigation and stated that his assistance had been valuable in bringing cases to the project's attention. That did not mean Condon accepted McDonald's conclusions — the two ended up far apart — but it is significant when testing a strong suppression hypothesis. A government-sponsored report openly acknowledged assistance from the very scientist supposedly being erased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-1960s-condon-report-1968-ad0680975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">decade 1960s condon report 1968 ad0680975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyThe Condon Report (DTIC AD0680975), University of Colorado / U.S. Air Force (sponsor) · 1969 · UFO Transparency...</span></span></span>

There are, nevertheless, episodes that demonstrate [hostility]({{ 'hostility/' | relative_url }}) towards McDonald and the broader UFO research community. Historical material on the disposition of Blue Book files after the programme ended records astronomer Donald Menzel advocating restrictions that would keep McDonald, Hynek and civilian UFO organisations away from the material for a period. Such proposals are evidence of gatekeeping and distrust. They deserve to be described as such. They still do not establish a covert programme to threaten McDonald personally, much less connect official hostility to his later death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/chap06.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ncas.org.</span></span></span>

This is the central evidential distinction: **documented opposition is not missing from the record; documented personal suppression is**.

## McDonald himself rejected the strongest cover-up interpretation

The most important contemporary evidence against reading every Blue Book deficiency as deliberate suppression comes from McDonald.

In his March 1968 paper *UFOs — An International Scientific Problem*, he explicitly separated two explanations for the Air Force's poor performance. One was a deliberate conspiracy concealing what officials knew about UFOs. The other was institutional failure: weak scientific investigation combined with a longstanding tendency to dispose of cases through conventional explanations. McDonald favoured the second. He wrote that he did not see convincing evidence for a US cover-up conspiracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/JamesMcDonald-UFOs-InternationScientificProblem.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents2.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents2.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUFOs - An International Scientific ProblemMarch 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2023</span></span></span>

Later in the paper he sharpened the distinction. McDonald argued that advocates of conspiracy might have mistaken Blue Book's scientific inadequacy for secret knowledge: in his formulation, they could have confused **incompetence with inscrutability**. He called his alternative the "grand foul-up" hypothesis, while leaving open the possibility that new evidence could change his assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/mcdonaldca.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: James E. McDonald at the Canadian Aeronautics and Space Institute Astronautics Symposium, Montreal, Canada, March 12...</span></span></span>

That qualification matters. McDonald was not saying that every official action had been transparent or that secrecy played no role in Cold War UFO policy. He was saying that the evidence available to him did not justify the larger inference that authorities possessed the answer and were systematically hiding it. His own position therefore provides an unusually relevant evidential test for later claims that *he himself* was suppressed because he was uncovering such a secret.

There is an important difference between McDonald's argument and the Air Force's. McDonald believed some UFO cases were extraordinarily significant and favoured an extraterrestrial hypothesis; the Air Force ultimately said its investigations provided no evidence that unidentified reports were extraterrestrial vehicles or technologically beyond contemporary knowledge. They disagreed sharply about what the unexplained cases might mean. But disagreement about the phenomenon did not prevent McDonald from concluding that institutional failure was better supported than a grand conspiracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/JamesMcDonald-UFOs-InternationScientificProblem.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents2.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents2.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUFOs - An International Scientific ProblemMarch 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2023</span></span></span>

## What proof of personal suppression would actually look like

A claim that Blue Book mishandled UFO investigations requires one kind of evidence. A claim that officials deliberately targeted McDonald requires another. To move from the first proposition to the second, the documentary record would need some identifiable link between McDonald, an authorised actor and an intentional effort to punish or silence him.

Useful evidence could include an order to restrict McDonald's access specifically because of what he was discovering; correspondence directing officials to damage his employment or scientific standing; records of threats tied to his UFO work; instructions to prevent his testimony or publication; surveillance explicitly undertaken to obstruct his Blue Book criticism; or communications linking adverse action against him to a need to conceal particular information.

The surviving Blue Book material located in the National Archives is extensive enough to make that distinction meaningful. Its holdings include administrative files as well as sighting cases and Office of Special Investigations material, and large portions of the collection are now available digitally or through bulk-download resources. The National Archives also lists Blue Book administrative files and both original and sanitised case-file series among its UAP-related holdings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

None of this means that an absent document can be proved never to have existed. Historical archives are incomplete by nature, and the absence of evidence is not automatically evidence of absence. But a positive allegation of targeting needs affirmative support. An incomplete archive cannot logically do double duty as proof that the incriminating records were deliberately removed.

There is also evidence outside Blue Book that the US government took an interest in McDonald. Released FBI material shows that the Bureau maintained records on him and considered interviewing people concerning him. The surviving material touches his UFO activities but also his contacts, peace-related activities and the political environment surrounding the University of Arizona. The records therefore demonstrate government scrutiny, something hardly surprising in the domestic-security practices of the late 1960s, but they do not by themselves establish a Blue Book-directed programme to silence him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/fbimcdon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Computer UFO Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cufon.org.</span></span></span>

That distinction prevents two opposite errors. It would be wrong to claim that McDonald faced no official scrutiny simply because a suppression case has not been proved. It would be equally wrong to take every instance of scrutiny, professional disagreement or missing paperwork and combine them into evidence of a single covert operation without documents establishing that connection.

## Why investigative failure is not proof of targeting

Blue Book presents an unusually tempting environment for retrospective conspiracy arguments because several real features can be assembled into a compelling narrative: unexplained cases existed; some official explanations were controversial; files could be incomplete; the programme operated inside the Cold War national-security establishment; officials worried about public reaction to UFO reports; and McDonald became one of their most persistent scientific critics. Each point can be documented to varying degrees. Their coexistence, however, does not establish a causal chain from McDonald's criticism to deliberate action against him.

The records instead support a more mundane institutional mechanism. Blue Book had to process reports while also serving an Air Force concerned with national security and public relations. Its final conclusions were consistently reassuring, while critics such as McDonald believed that this orientation encouraged weak conventional explanations. Even National Archives material describing Blue Book's history notes the Air Force's concern with security threats and public interest, while the surviving collection demonstrates the enormous disparity between thousands of accumulated reports and the modest administrative structure responsible for handling them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/project-blue-book-50th-anniversary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

That institutional environment can produce bias without requiring a secret order. Once an organisation has concluded that a category of reports poses no security threat and is unlikely to reveal extraordinary technology, ambiguous evidence may receive less attention; investigators may preferentially seek ordinary explanations; embarrassing cases may become public-relations problems; and critics may be treated as nuisances. Those mechanisms are compatible with McDonald's description of Blue Book and with Quintanilla's openly defensive response to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/JamesMcDonald-UFOs-InternationScientificProblem.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents2.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents2.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUFOs - An International Scientific ProblemMarch 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2023</span></span></span>

The distinction also explains why the Blue Book archive can simultaneously strengthen McDonald's historical credibility as a **critic of the investigation** while weakening attempts to use him as straightforward evidence of **researcher suppression**. His access to the files gave him concrete grounds for challenging particular explanations and procedures. But his own reading of those institutional failures stopped short of alleging the conspiracy that later narratives sometimes imply.

## The evidential limit

For the narrow question of whether Blue Book records show that James McDonald was personally targeted, the answer is **no on the surviving evidence**. The archive documents a real and sometimes bitter dispute over how UFO reports were investigated. It supports the conclusion that McDonald's complaints cannot simply be dismissed as the imaginings of an outsider: he examined Air Force files, challenged identifiable cases, dealt directly with Blue Book officials and publicly confronted the programme's scientific standards. Independent developments, including the decision to commission an outside university study, show that concern over Blue Book's adequacy was not confined to McDonald.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saturdaynightuforia.com/html/articles/articlehtml/ost-macdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saturdaynightuforia.com">[Saturday Night Uforia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saturdaynightuforia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on saturdaynightuforia.com.</span></span></span>

What is missing is the evidential bridge from that dispute to personal suppression. No Blue Book record identified here orders McDonald to be threatened, professionally destroyed, prevented from speaking or physically harmed. Indeed, the record also contains awkward facts for a simple silencing narrative: he received access to Blue Book files, communicated his criticism to senior Air Force personnel, testified publicly before Congress, continued giving lectures and was acknowledged as a useful source of cases by the Air Force-sponsored Colorado project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2022/10/05162521/SUN-67-Mar-2001-copy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com">[amazonaws.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for Inquiry.·</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">By Philip J. Klass 404 &#x27;N&#x27;. St SW Washh?g!on...</span></span></span>

The strongest historically defensible conclusion is therefore narrower than either extreme. Blue Book's surviving record does not prove that its investigations were scientifically adequate, and it does not erase evidence of bureaucratic defensiveness, stigma or hostility towards UFO advocates. But those facts cannot substitute for evidence that McDonald was personally subjected to a coordinated suppression operation.

For assessing later claims surrounding his fate, that missing link is decisive. The documents substantiate **institutional controversy and investigative failure**. They do not substantiate **a Blue Book campaign against McDonald himself**. McDonald's own 1968 assessment remains an apt warning against collapsing the two: serious institutional failure can look suspicious without demonstrating the more consequential allegation of deliberate personal targeting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/JamesMcDonald-UFOs-InternationScientificProblem.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents2.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents2.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultUFOs - An International Scientific ProblemMarch 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2023</span></span></span>
