## Introduction

A missing [death record]({{ 'death-record/' | relative_url }}) is not the same thing as a hidden death record. In disputed deaths associated with UFO, aerospace or unconventional-propulsion research, that distinction is especially important because an unsuccessful internet search can easily be mistaken for evidence that documentation was suppressed. In practice, an apparently absent record usually falls into one of four categories: **it exists but public access is restricted; it exists but must be requested rather than downloaded; it is temporarily withheld because a case remains open; or the database being searched was never supposed to contain the record in the first place**.

There is also a less dramatic possibility: a particular secondary database genuinely failed to receive or correctly index the death. US government audits have documented such gaps. The right question is therefore not simply, “Where is the [death certificate]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }})?” It is: **which document should exist, which authority should hold it, and what does that authority say about its status?**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/handbooks-and-guides.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[cdc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NVSS - Handbooks and Guides for Use with U.S. Standard Certificates and Reports...</span></span></span>

## Four reasons a record can appear to be missing

The most useful first step is to separate four mechanisms that produce superficially similar search results. Only after establishing which one applies is it sensible to draw conclusions from the absence.

**1. The record exists, but it is confidential or restricted.** Death certificates are legal vital records, and access rules differ substantially between jurisdictions. They are not necessarily public documents immediately after death. Alabama provides a particularly relevant example because of the continuing controversy around [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researcher Amy Eskridge, who died in Huntsville on 11 June 2022. Her published obituary establishes the date of death and identifies her as president and co-founder of the Institute for Exotic Science, but it does not state a cause of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022</span></span></span>

That omission should not be confused with the absence of a state death certificate. The Alabama Department of Public Health says the state has filed death certificates since 1908 and restricts certificates for **25 years after death**. During that period, copies are available only to specified relatives and other authorised people, including legal representatives and the informant named on the certificate. Identification is required for restricted records. Thus an ordinary researcher being unable to obtain Eskridge's 2022 certificate in 2026 is precisely what Alabama's normal access rules predict; unrestricted access would not arise until 2047 under the current rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Public HealthDeath Certificates &#124; Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH)March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

**2. The record exists, but it is request-only rather than published online.** “Public record” does not necessarily mean “searchable on Google”. An agency may require a formal application, proof of eligibility, identification, a fee or a sufficiently specific records request. Alabama's forensic agency, for example, provides dedicated procedures for requesting its final case reports and other public records rather than maintaining a public search engine containing every case file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adfs.alabama.gov/resources/attorneycorner/public-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adfs.alabama.gov">[Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adfs.alabama.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Sciences Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Forensic SciencesAlabama Department of Forensic Sciences...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters well beyond Alabama. The US National Archives explicitly warns that ordinary state and local vital records are not federal records and therefore are not held in its catalogue. Death certificates are normally created and maintained by state or local authorities. Searching a large national archive and finding nothing can therefore establish only that the archive does not hold the certificate, not that the certificate does not exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/vital-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Vital Records &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Vital Records &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

**3. A record has been created but cannot yet be released.** Investigative status can produce a different kind of absence. The Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences (ADFS) says final case reports from its investigations are public records, but a certified report cannot be issued until the ADFS case is complete **and all pending criminal investigations are complete**. Before release, the agency requires written confirmation from the relevant prosecutor or court that no criminal investigation remains pending. Photographs, notes, worksheets and other case-specific material are subject to still different rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adfs.alabama.gov/resources/attorneycorner/public-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adfs.alabama.gov">[Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adfs.alabama.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Sciences Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Forensic SciencesAlabama Department of Forensic Sciences...</span></span></span>

That means “the agency has not released the autopsy file” has several possible interpretations. The examination may be unfinished; the forensic case may be complete while a related investigation remains open; the final report may be releasable while photographs or working papers are not; or the requester may simply have used the wrong procedure. At federal level the same broad principle appears in the Freedom of Information Act: law-enforcement material can be withheld where disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings, among other protected circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/oip/freedom-information-act-5-usc-552" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Justice.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">freedom information act 5 usc 552</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of Information Policy &#124; The Freedom of Information Act, 5 U.S.C. § 552January 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2022</span></span></span>

**4. The expected record was never in the database being searched.** This is perhaps the easiest trap for online research. A database that looks like a national death register may actually be an administrative extract with important exclusions.

The Social Security Administration's Death Master File (DMF) is a good example. SSA says it compiles death information from its records of Social Security number holders, but the public version supplied through the National Technical Information Service excludes state death records. Federal law and data-sharing restrictions limit what can appear in the publicly distributed file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0203315015" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: secure.ssa.gov">[Social Security Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">secure.ssa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Social Security Administration SSA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Social Security Administration SSA</span></span></span>

Historically, the DMF has also been incomplete. The Social Security Administration's Office of Inspector General has explicitly cautioned that SSA does not receive death information for every deceased person and that **absence from the DMF does not mean the person is alive**. An earlier audit identified approximately 1.4 million deceased beneficiaries whose deaths appeared in SSA payment records but had not been entered on the database from which the DMF was produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oig.ssa.gov/congressional-testimony/2015-03-17-newsroom-congressional-testimony-march16-hsgac/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oig.ssa.gov">[Office of the Inspector General]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oig.ssa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ssa.gov.</span></span></span>

That is a concrete demonstration of why a missing database hit cannot, by itself, establish suppression.

## Which agency should have the document?

The phrase “official death record” can obscure the fact that a death generates several records maintained by different institutions. US death registration is decentralised. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) describes a process involving funeral directors, medical certifiers, medical examiners or coroners and state or local registrars. Modern Electronic Death Registration Systems allow those parties to exchange information while registering a death, but the resulting certificate is still a vital record belonging to the appropriate jurisdiction rather than a universal federal case file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/handbooks-and-guides.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NVSS - Handbooks and Guides for Use with U.S. Standard Certificates and Reports...</span></span></span>

For disputed deaths, the document sought should therefore determine where the search begins:

* **Death certificate:** the state, territorial or local vital-records authority. It establishes the registered death and normally contains certified cause-of-death information, subject to local access rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/misc/hb_fun.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Funeral Directors&#x27; Handbook on Death Registration and Fetal Death Reporting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Funeral Directors&#x27; Handbook on Death Registration and Fetal Death Reporting</span></span></span>
* **Autopsy or forensic examination report:** the medical examiner, coroner or forensic-sciences agency that had jurisdiction over the body. Its access rules may differ completely from those governing the death certificate.
* **Police incident or investigative report:** the relevant police or sheriff's department, with possible additional records held by a prosecutor. Investigative exemptions may affect disclosure.
* **Federal investigative file:** the relevant federal agency, where federal jurisdiction actually existed. A federal FOIA search is not a substitute for requesting a state death certificate.
* **Death-database entry:** the organisation operating that particular database. A Social Security death entry, for example, is an administrative record assembled from reports received by SSA, not the underlying state certificate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssa.gov/dataexchange/request_dmf.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssa.gov">[Social Security Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Social Security Administration</span></span></span>
* **Death abroad:** jurisdiction becomes still more important. For US citizens who died overseas, consular death reports can exist separately from the local country's civil death record; historical US consular reports are divided between the National Archives and Department of State according to date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/foreign-policy/state-dept/genealogy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

This produces a useful diagnostic rule: **before treating a search failure as evidence, establish that the repository searched was actually responsible for the record at the relevant date and place**.

A request returning “no responsive records” from the wrong agency says little. A state registrar issuing a formal “failure to find” response to a correctly identified death is much more significant. Even then, researchers should check spelling, aliases, jurisdiction, date and whether the death occurred outside the area searched before interpreting the result.

## “Not online” and “does not exist” are different findings

Online research creates a strong visibility bias. Digitised records feel as though they constitute the archive, when they are often only a public-facing layer over much larger administrative systems. The National Archives makes this limitation unusually explicit: its catalogue contains federal archival descriptions and digitised federal material, but not the ordinary records of state, county and municipal governments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

The same problem affects commercial genealogy indexes, newspaper archives and Social Security-derived databases. They can be valuable corroborating tools, but they are not interchangeable with a registrar's holdings. A person's absence from one of them may reflect collection boundaries, redisclosure restrictions, transcription errors, delayed reporting or incomplete historical coverage.

Even government databases can contain errors in both directions. SSA's Inspector General has documented deceased people omitted from its death data, while another investigation found living people who had erroneously been placed in the DMF. SSA itself acknowledges that erroneous death reports sometimes occur and describes procedures for correcting them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oig-html.ssa.gov/audits/summary/html/A-06-09-29095.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oig-html.ssa.gov">[ssa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oig-html.ssa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ssa.gov.</span></span></span>

Consequently, researchers investigating an allegedly suspicious death should assign different evidential weight to three statements:

**“I cannot find the record online”** is weak evidence. It describes the researcher's search result.

**“The responsible agency says the record exists but is restricted or unavailable while a case remains pending”** establishes the document's status but not its contents.

**“The responsible agency conducted a search and formally says it has no responsive record”** is potentially significant, particularly when ordinary procedure indicates that such a record should have been created.

Those propositions should never be collapsed into one another.

## How to establish the record's actual status

For controversial deaths, a short documentary trail is more useful than repeated web searches. Start with the person's full legal name, date and place of death. Then identify the jurisdiction's vital-records authority and its rules for the year concerned. Determine whether the office began registration before that date, whether the certificate is public, and whether members of the general public are eligible to order it.

Next, separate the certificate from the death investigation. If an autopsy is alleged, identify the coroner, medical examiner or forensic agency with jurisdiction and look for that office's actual disclosure policy. If a police investigation is relevant, establish which law-enforcement agency handled it and whether its records rules distinguish closed investigations from active ones.

Finally, record the agency's response precisely. “Restricted to immediate family”, “case incomplete”, “criminal investigation pending”, “no responsive records”, “wrong agency” and “record located but exempt from disclosure” are fundamentally different findings. In a contentious UFO-related case, replacing all of them with “authorities refused to release the records” removes exactly the information needed to judge whether the absence is routine or unusual.

## How record status changes what can be concluded


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

An absent public record can become genuinely informative, but only after ordinary explanations have been tested. If the correct agency confirms that a document exists but state law makes it confidential, the absence from the internet provides **no independent evidence of concealment specific to that person**. The same restriction would apply to comparable deaths.

</div>

If the agency confirms that a report is temporarily withheld because an investigation remains pending, the status does tell researchers something: some official process has not yet reached the point at which the document can be released under that agency's rules. It does **not**, by itself, identify why the investigation remains open or support a particular theory of the death.

A formal “no record found” result deserves closer attention where the document should ordinarily exist. CDC guidance describes the death certificate as a permanent legal record and lays out defined responsibilities for completing and filing deaths in the US vital-registration system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/misc/hb_fun.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Funeral Directors&#x27; Handbook on Death Registration and Fetal Death Reporting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Funeral Directors&#x27; Handbook on Death Registration and Fetal Death Reporting</span></span></span> Even then, an apparent anomaly should prompt checks for jurisdictional transfer, incorrect identifying details, amendments, delayed registration or an overseas death before stronger conclusions are drawn.

The most evidentially interesting situation is not simply a blank search page. It is a **documented mismatch between expected procedure and the responsible authority's account**: for example, an agency says no record exists even though another primary document identifies that agency as having performed the examination; incompatible agencies give contradictory case-status responses; or a document previously acknowledged by an authority can no longer be accounted for. Such contradictions can justify further investigation because they concern the custody or handling of an identifiable record rather than an inference from internet silence.

For claims about suspicious deaths among UFO or antigravity researchers, this distinction provides an essential evidential safeguard. Restricted access should not be presented as proof of a cover-up, just as inaccessible primary records should not be replaced with unwarranted certainty about what those records contain. The strongest assessment is the one that preserves the difference between **known contents, reported contents, known restrictions and genuinely unexplained documentary gaps**.
