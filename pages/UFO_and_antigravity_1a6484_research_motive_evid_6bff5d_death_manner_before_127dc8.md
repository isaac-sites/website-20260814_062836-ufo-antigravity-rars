## Introduction

Before asking who might have wanted a UFO, [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) or unconventional-propulsion researcher dead, investigators have to establish a more basic proposition: **was the person killed at all?** An unusual death, an unresolved death or a death surrounded by secrecy does not by itself establish homicide. Medicolegal death investigation works in the opposite order. Investigators reconstruct the circumstances, examine the scene and body, review medical history and laboratory findings, and determine the cause and, where possible, the manner of death. Only then does a homicide-specific motive theory have a secure factual foundation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/death-investigation-guide-scene-investigator-2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">death investigation guide scene investigator 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This document provides a breakdown of the various tasks involved in conducting medicolegal death...Read more...</span></span></span>

That ordering is especially important when researching alleged suspicious deaths of scientists. A compelling suppression motive can make ambiguous facts look sinister after the event. But motive cannot substitute for evidence that another person's actions caused the death. The disciplined question is therefore not initially, "Who benefited?" It is: **"What does the death evidence establish happened?"**

## Cause of death and manner of death answer different questions

"Cause of death" and "manner of death" are related but distinct findings. Cause identifies the disease or injury responsible for the fatal sequence: examples might include a gunshot wound, blunt-force injuries, poisoning or coronary disease. Manner places the death into a broader category reflecting its circumstances. In the standard US system these categories are natural, accident, suicide, homicide and undetermined; a case can also remain pending while necessary investigation or testing is completed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00001318.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[cdc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Current Trends Operational Criteria for Determining Suicide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Current Trends Operational Criteria for Determining SuicideDecember 23, 1988 — Editorial Note: For each death certificate filed in the...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 1988</span></span></span>

That distinction matters because knowing the physical cause does not necessarily reveal how it came about. A forensic pathologist might establish that somebody died from blunt-force injuries while the available evidence remains insufficient to decide whether those injuries resulted from an accident, suicide or another person's actions. A published forensic-pathology series of deaths classified as undetermined found precisely this problem: manner could remain unresolved despite an established cause of death, with accident appearing the most probable explanation in many cases while homicide could not be excluded in some.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26234640/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Undetermined Manner of Death: An Autopsy Series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undetermined Manner of Death: An Autopsy Series - PubMedJanuary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2016</span></span></span>

Conversely, the word **homicide** in a medical examiner's classification should not automatically be translated into "murder". In medicolegal usage it can describe a death resulting from another person's actions; questions of criminal culpability, justification and the precise offence belong to the legal investigation. The distinction is particularly visible when comparing jurisdictions. England and Wales uses the coroner's conclusion of "unlawful killing", for which the elements of murder, manslaughter or another applicable unlawful killing must actually be established to the required standard. The Chief Coroner's guidance separately recognises "lawful killing", demonstrating why the bare fact that human action caused death and the legal conclusion that a crime occurred are not interchangeable propositions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Chapter-15-Conclusions-140725.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: judiciary.uk">[Courts and Tribunals Judiciary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">judiciary.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chapter 15 Conclusions 140725</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 15 Conclusions 140725</span></span></span>

For claims involving unconventional researchers, therefore, several questions should remain separate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **What killed the person?** The medical cause.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/151721/cdc_151721_DS1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stacks.cdc.gov">[stacks.cdc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stacks.cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cdc 151721 DS1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cdc 151721 DS1</span></span></span>
* **Under what circumstances did that fatal injury or condition arise?** The manner or equivalent legal finding.
* **Did another person's conduct cause it?** The threshold question for a homicide theory.
* **Was that conduct criminal?** A legal question requiring additional evidence.
* **Who was responsible, and why?** Attribution and motive questions that come later.

</div>

Collapsing those stages is one of the easiest ways for a suspicious-death narrative to outrun its evidence.

## What the scene and forensic findings can establish

A post-mortem examination is only part of a death investigation. The US National Institute of Justice's current *Death Investigation: A Guide for the Scene Investigator* treats medicolegal investigation as a structured process involving the scene, body, evidence, witnesses and relevant records. Earlier NIJ guidance makes the underlying principle explicit: physical evidence from all relevant locations may bear on the cause, manner and circumstances of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/death-investigation-guide-scene-investigator-2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">death investigation guide scene investigator 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This document provides a breakdown of the various tasks involved in conducting medicolegal death...Read more...</span></span></span>

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the Royal College of Pathologists likewise describes forensic pathology in suspicious deaths as a process extending from the initial police contact through examination and ultimately, where necessary, evidence in court. Its professional code establishes standards for each stage rather than treating the post-mortem as an isolated medical exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rcpath.org/static/5617496b-cd1a-4ce3-9ec8eabfb0db8f3a/Code-of-practice-and-performance-standards-for-forensic-pathology-in-England-Wales-and-Northern-Ireland.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcpath.org">[Royal College of Pathologists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcpath.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal College of PathologistsCode-of-practice-and-performance-standards-for-forensic-...This code sets out what is expected of the patho...</span></span></span>

That broader reconstruction matters because individual findings often admit more than one explanation. Investigators may need to consider the position and condition of the body; injury patterns; blood or other biological material; weapons or possible instruments; signs of disturbance; medications and toxicology; medical records; witness accounts; electronic or documentary evidence; and whether the physical evidence is consistent with the reported circumstances. A claimed homicide mechanism becomes materially stronger when independent findings converge on it rather than merely failing to rule it out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/234457.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

For example, toxicology identifying a lethal substance can help establish *what* caused death without automatically establishing *who administered it*. A fatal fall can explain extensive blunt-force trauma without by itself determining whether the person jumped, slipped, was pushed or entered the fall by some other route. Conversely, injury distribution, defensive injuries, scene reconstruction or evidence inconsistent with an alleged accident may make third-party involvement substantially more plausible.

This is why isolated autopsy details can be misleading when circulated without the complete report and scene investigation. A particular fracture, concentration of a drug or unusual wound may be real while still being non-specific. Its significance depends on the complete pattern and [competing explanations]({{ 'competing-explanations/' | relative_url }}). ## Why "undetermined" does not mean "probably murdered"

One of the most consequential errors in suspicious-death research is treating uncertainty as positive evidence of homicide. **Undetermined means that the available evidence does not support assigning another manner with sufficient confidence.** It preserves uncertainty rather than resolving it in favour of the most dramatic possibility.

The distinction is empirically important. In the published autopsy series of undetermined deaths, homicide remained possible in a minority of cases, but accident was the most frequent probable manner. The authors nevertheless emphasised that homicide can sometimes be underestimated. Both observations matter: an undetermined classification should neither be converted into homicide nor treated as proof that homicide has been excluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26234640/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Undetermined Manner of Death: An Autopsy Series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undetermined Manner of Death: An Autopsy Series - PubMedJanuary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2016</span></span></span>

The US standard [death certificate]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }}) similarly instructs certifiers to use "Could not be Determined" when manner cannot be determined. That is fundamentally different from selecting homicide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/NCHS/DATA/DVS/DEATH11-03FINAL-ACC.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DEATH11 03FINAL ACC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. STANDARD CERTIFICATE OF DEATH -- REV. 11/2003Indicate “Could not be Determined” ONLY when it is impossible to determine the manne...</span></span></span>

The same evidential discipline applies when the *cause* itself is uncertain. Decomposition, incomplete remains, unavailable toxicology, ambiguous injuries or missing scene information can leave investigators unable to reconstruct exactly what happened. Those deficiencies may justify further investigation. They do not create affirmative evidence for an assassination theory.

A useful way to think about an unresolved case is that it contains **competing hypotheses**, not an evidential vacuum that belongs to homicide by default. If accident, suicide, natural disease, homicide and some combination of contributing factors remain reasonably possible, evidence has to discriminate between them. A theory gains weight by explaining observations better than its alternatives, not merely because its alternatives have unanswered questions.

This problem is recognised beyond conventional death investigation. A Royal Statistical Society-associated analysis of suspected killings in healthcare stressed that such investigations can contain two distinct uncertainties: whether homicide occurred at all and, conditional on homicide having occurred, whether the suspected person was responsible. Treating suspicion about a person as evidence that the underlying deaths were murders risks reversing those questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.00962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The same logic applies directly to claims that unconventional researchers were eliminated because of their work.

## Suspicious circumstances are a reason to investigate, not a manner of death

A case can quite reasonably attract investigation before homicide is established. Sudden, violent, unusual or unexplained deaths are precisely the cases medicolegal systems are designed to scrutinise. The National Institute of Justice describes medicolegal death investigation as determining identity, cause, manner and surrounding circumstances when deaths are unexpected or unnatural. The Royal College of Pathologists similarly places suspected murder and other complex suspicious deaths within forensic pathology's remit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/taxonomy/term/medicolegal-death-investigations?page=5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

The key distinction is between an **investigative trigger** and an **investigative conclusion**. A locked laboratory, missing research papers, intelligence contacts, an unexplained absence, contradictory witnesses or an unexpected death shortly before a claimed breakthrough could all justify asking more questions. None independently establishes that the death was inflicted by another person.

This distinction protects an investigation in both directions. It prevents premature homicide claims, but it also prevents authorities or researchers from dismissing genuinely troubling evidence merely because an initial explanation appears mundane. Scene evidence and forensic conclusions can be revised when significant new evidence emerges. The appropriate response to uncertainty is therefore preservation of evidence, documented reasoning and continued investigation where warranted—not an automatic assumption either of assassination or innocence.

The importance of independent, evidence-led manner determinations is not merely theoretical. Recent scrutiny of US deaths involving police restraint has shown that manner-of-death decisions can themselves become contested and consequential. An independent Maryland review concluded that numerous cases previously given other classifications should have been classified as homicides, illustrating both that forensic classifications can be revisited and that institutional context can affect disputed cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/71e52a4cd2cc4103eab9648a127f1835" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span> An Associated Press investigation likewise documented controversy over external pressure on medical examiners and cited professional concerns that cause-and-manner decisions should remain independent of police and prosecutors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/a401d382bd0ceb3b35d48cfaccab4275" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that disputed rulings should therefore be presumed corrupt. It is that **the underlying forensic record matters more than institutional prestige or institutional suspicion**. A credible challenge identifies specific deficiencies—missed injuries, inconsistent reconstruction, overlooked toxicology, compromised evidence, incomplete records, unsupported assumptions or credible new evidence—and shows how correcting them changes the death analysis.

## Motive can distort the reading of ambiguous evidence

The ordering problem becomes acute in stories about [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers because the alleged motive is often unusually compelling. A scientist may have worked for defence contractors, sought patents, claimed a revolutionary propulsion effect, communicated with military agencies or stopped publishing after entering [classified work]({{ 'classified-work/' | relative_url }}). Those facts can make later misfortune feel connected even before evidence of a killing exists.

But this creates a classic direction-of-reasoning problem. Once a researcher is imagined as possessing dangerous knowledge, almost any unusual subsequent event can be assimilated into the theory: a traffic collision becomes a possible targeted attack; suicide becomes a possible staged killing; missing records become evidence of suppression; an inconclusive post-mortem becomes evidence of sophisticated concealment. The theory becomes difficult to falsify because ambiguity itself is repeatedly counted in its favour.

Forensic reasoning imposes a useful brake. Start without assuming the motive theory is true and ask what conclusions the death evidence independently supports. If the injuries and scene strongly establish assault, then questions of perpetrator and motive become central. If the findings establish an ordinary accident, a suppression theory must produce strong contradictory evidence before displacing that explanation. If manner remains genuinely undetermined, homicide remains one hypothesis among the possibilities supported by the case—not the default winner.

That approach also prevents the reverse mistake. A seemingly persuasive motive should not cause investigators to force ambiguous pathology into a homicide classification. Manner-of-death classification has genuine areas of professional judgement and disagreement, which makes independence particularly important. Professional standards and government death-investigation guidance emphasise systematic investigation precisely because a conclusion should emerge from the totality of the evidence rather than from the preferred investigative narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thename.org/death-certification" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thename.org">[The Name]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thename.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thename.org.</span></span></span>

## A homicide claim needs a bridge from death evidence to motive

For alleged suspicious deaths among unconventional researchers, the most reliable evaluation therefore proceeds in a deliberate evidential sequence. First establish identity, chronology and the reliability of the basic [death record]({{ 'death-record/' | relative_url }}). Then establish the medical cause as far as the evidence permits. Reconstruct the circumstances and determine whether the manner is natural, accidental, suicidal, homicidal or genuinely unresolved. Only if the evidence supports another person's causal involvement does a homicide-specific investigation move securely towards attribution, opportunity and motive.

That sequence does not mean [motive evidence]({{ 'motive-evidence/' | relative_url }}) is irrelevant during an investigation. A documented death threat, surveillance record or attempt to interfere with a researcher's work could legitimately prompt investigators to preserve evidence, revisit a scene or test a particular hypothesis. But such information should **direct inquiry rather than predetermine the forensic conclusion**.

This creates a useful standard for historical UFO and antigravity death claims. Before asking whether a government programme, corporation, intelligence service or commercial rival had reason to silence a researcher, ask what evidence establishes the foundational event that the proposed motive is supposed to explain. If another person's lethal action has not been established, the accurate description is an unusual, disputed or unresolved death—not an assassination established by motive.

That distinction may sound cautious, but it is actually what makes a strong suspicious-death case stronger. Where scene reconstruction, pathology, toxicology, witness evidence and other records independently point to homicide, motive evidence can then help explain **why** it happened and narrow **who** might have been responsible. Where those foundations are absent, an impressive motive remains a hypothesis about a killing that has yet to be demonstrated.
