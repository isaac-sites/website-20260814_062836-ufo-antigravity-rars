## Introduction

If separate deaths of UFO, unconventional-propulsion or [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers were genuinely part of one operation, the most persuasive evidence would not be that the victims studied similar subjects. It would be a **repeatable operational signature**: the same firearm, the same unknown DNA contributor, the same intermediary or payment route, overlapping surveillance personnel, shared communications infrastructure, or travel and location records that place the same actors around more than one victim.

Modern forensic intelligence is designed precisely to find such cross-case connections. The US National Institute of Justice describes the discipline as using forensic data across incidents to identify links between cases, while systems for DNA and ballistic evidence can connect crime scenes even before investigators know who the offender is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/understanding-forensic-intelligence-nij-framework" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeUnderstanding Forensic Intelligence: The NIJ Framework &#124; National Institute of JusticeOctober 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 25, 2023</span></span></span>

For the [researcher deaths]({{ 'missing-denominator/' | relative_url }}) commonly grouped in [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) narratives, however, the publicly documented record does not presently show that kind of repeated signature. That distinction is crucial: an unusual death may justify investigation, but **coordination requires evidence that travels from one case to another**.

## What would a genuine forensic link look like?

A useful way to evaluate claims of coordinated researcher killings is to ask whether investigators could construct a cross-case evidence table in which distinctive features recur independently. Some signatures would be substantially more probative than others.

**A shared weapon or ammunition signature** could be particularly important in shooting cases. Firearms can leave microscopic marks on bullets and cartridge cases. The US Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) uses the National Integrated Ballistic Information Network, or NIBIN, to compare such evidence across crime scenes. A computer-generated similarity is treated initially as an investigative lead; when necessary, an examiner subsequently compares the physical evidence microscopically to confirm a match.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atf.gov/resource-center/fact-sheet/national-integrated-ballistic-information-network" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atf.gov">[ATF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fact Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fact Sheet - National Integrated Ballistic Information Network &#124; ATFJune 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2026</span></span></span>

This is not merely theoretical. ATF describes NIBIN as capable of linking ballistic evidence from crimes in different places, while documented investigations have connected multiple shootings through the same firearm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atf.gov/firearms/tools-services-law-enforcement/national-integrated-ballistic-information-network-nibin" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atf.gov">[ATF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">national integrated ballistic information network nibin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Integrated Ballistic Information Network (NIBIN) &#124; ATFJanuary 28, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 28, 2026</span></span></span> In a hypothetical series of researcher deaths, therefore, cartridge cases independently recovered from two supposed suicides or homicides and confirmed as having been fired by the **same weapon** would be dramatically more significant than the observation that both victims died from gunshot wounds.

**Repeated unidentified DNA** could perform a similar function. The FBI's Combined DNA Index System, CODIS, searches forensic DNA profiles not only against known offenders but against profiles recovered at other crime scenes. A confirmed forensic-to-forensic match can therefore link two crimes even when the person who left the DNA remains unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/dna-fingerprint-act-of-2005-expungement-policy/codis-and-ndis-fact-sheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CODIS and NDIS Fact Sheet — FBI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CODIS and NDIS Fact Sheet — FBI...</span></span></span>

Again, the important feature is specificity. “Unknown DNA was present” is weak because many scenes contain biological material from innocent visitors, relatives, emergency personnel or previous occupants. “The same well-supported unknown profile attributable to the perpetrator appeared at two geographically and socially separate killing scenes” is an entirely different proposition.

Other physical traces could acquire similar significance if sufficiently distinctive: matching footwear impressions, an unusual tool mark, a particular ligature construction, uncommon chemical residues or repeated evidence of the same scene-manipulation technique. Pattern and impression evidence encompasses fingerprints, footwear, tool marks and tyre tracks, among other traces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/document/webinar-forensic-science-center-excellence-finalpdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forensic Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forensic ScienceApril 13, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2026</span></span></span> But such evidence would still need proper validation and contextual interpretation; resemblance alone is not automatically identity.

## Digital traces could expose the operation behind the deaths

For allegations involving an organised programme rather than a lone repeat offender, the most revealing signature might be logistical rather than biological.

Digital evidence can preserve communications, location information and financial activity. FBI guidance notes that investigators can encounter GPS information showing where people travelled, calls, emails and chats bearing on intent, and financial records revealing transactions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/featured-articles/executing-search-warrants-in-the-cloud" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leb.fbi.gov">[FBI: Law Enforcement Bulletin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leb.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executing search warrants in the cloud</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Law Enforcement BulletinExecuting Search Warrants in the Cloud — LEBOctober 7, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 7, 2014</span></span></span> Those categories suggest several concrete tests that would matter in alleged researcher killings.

A genuinely compelling cross-case investigation might find that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the same previously unidentified telephone repeatedly contacted different researchers shortly before their deaths;
* separate surveillance teams used devices communicating through the same accounts or infrastructure;
* the same vehicle, hire account, hotel booking or travel itinerary appeared near multiple incidents;
* a recurring intermediary received payments shortly before each death;
* financial transfers originated from the same organisation or concealed funding chain;
* devices belonging to one suspect generated location records near more than one victim at relevant times;
* authenticated messages identified several researchers as targets or discussed recovering their documents, prototypes or data.

</div>

No single category necessarily proves murder. A telephone number could belong to a mutual colleague; a shared hotel could reflect attendance at the same conference; researchers working in a small technical community may naturally communicate with the same people. The evidential force comes from **convergence**.

A useful real-world illustration comes from an unrelated ATF homicide investigation in Wisconsin. Ballistic analysis connected a recovered pistol to cartridge cases from an earlier homicide; investigators then developed additional evidence including enhanced video and mapping of the defendant's mobile-phone usage, which placed him at the scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atf.gov/firearms/docs/report/nfcta-volume-iv-part-vi-%E2%80%93-nibin-updates-and-new-analysis/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atf.gov">[ATF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PART VI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Integrated Ballistic InformatJanuary 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2026</span></span></span> That is the model relevant here: one forensic connection generates a hypothesis, while independent evidence tests and strengthens it.

For a supposed campaign against unconventional-propulsion researchers, a similarly persuasive structure might be: matching DNA connects two scenes; communications records connect the DNA source to an intermediary; financial records connect that intermediary to a common organisation; location data independently places associated personnel near both victims. Such a chain would be qualitatively different from assembling coincidences from biographies.

## Crime-linkage methods guard against false matches

The central danger in searching retrospectively for a hidden campaign is that investigators already know which cases they hope to connect. That makes almost any repeated detail look potentially meaningful.

Formal crime-linkage research tries to control this problem through two concepts: **consistency** and **distinctiveness**. An offender must show enough consistency between crimes for a recurring feature to be useful, but the feature must also be sufficiently distinctive from what other offenders commonly do. A 2026 meta-analysis of statistical crime-linkage research describes these as the two fundamental assumptions underlying linkage analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11896-026-09828-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springer.com.</span></span></span>

That distinction has immediate consequences for claims about researcher deaths. Suppose two researchers were found with computers missing. That resemblance sounds interesting until investigators establish how commonly computers disappear in burglaries and whether anything else connects the incidents. Conversely, the discovery that both computers had been accessed beforehand from the same obscure account or that the same device identifier appeared in authenticated network logs would be much harder to dismiss as generic similarity.

The same caution applies even to sophisticated forensic technology. A database result may generate a lead rather than a final conclusion. ATF explicitly distinguishes a **NIBIN lead**, an unconfirmed potential association derived from image correlation, from a confirmed **hit** established through examination of the physical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atf.gov/resource-center/fact-sheet/national-integrated-ballistic-information-network" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atf.gov">[ATF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fact Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fact Sheet - National Integrated Ballistic Information Network &#124; ATFJune 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2026</span></span></span> CODIS likewise has confirmation procedures after candidate matches are generated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/dna-fingerprint-act-of-2005-expungement-policy/codis-and-ndis-fact-sheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CODIS and NDIS Fact Sheet — FBI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CODIS and NDIS Fact Sheet — FBI...</span></span></span>

DNA statistics also require careful interpretation. NIST explains that mixed DNA samples can contain material from several people and that probabilistic genotyping estimates the strength of the evidence under competing propositions rather than simply declaring whose DNA it is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/feature-stories/dna-mixtures-forensic-science-explainer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dna mixtures forensic science explainer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dna mixtures forensic science explainer</span></span></span> An apparent trace connection should therefore not be converted casually into “the same assassin was present”.

The strongest linkage would consequently survive several tests at once: the signature is unusual, technically validated, temporally relevant, attributable to the offence rather than innocent activity, and corroborated by evidence produced through a different investigative route.

## Why the named researcher cases do not presently form a forensic series

The publicly available facts surrounding researchers frequently grouped into these narratives illustrate why [cross-case linkage]({{ 'cross-case-links/' | relative_url }}) is harder than compiling a list of suspicious-looking outcomes.

Ning Li, whose work on superconductors and gravity attracted considerable later speculation, died in 2021 aged 79. Her published obituary states that she “passed peacefully away”; the accessible public record located for this question does not identify homicide evidence, a suspicious weapon or another forensic signature capable of comparison with a separate researcher death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/guestbook/ning-li" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span></span></span>

Amy Eskridge, associated with the Institute for Exotic Science in Huntsville, died in June 2022 aged 34. Her published obituary confirms her death and scientific interests but does not itself establish homicide or provide the kind of scene evidence — weapon, perpetrator DNA, toxicological signature or identified assailant — from which a cross-case forensic match could responsibly be drawn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">amy eskridge obituary</span></span></span> Her young age and research interests may prompt questions, but neither is an operational signature.

James E. McDonald presents another fundamentally different evidential situation. The atmospheric physicist became one of the most prominent scientific advocates for serious UFO investigation in the 1960s and died in Tucson in June 1971. Contemporary and archival biographical material records his death as suicide; later summaries report that a revolver and apparent suicide note were found nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://handwiki.org/wiki/Biography%3AJames_E._McDonald" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: handwiki.org">[HandWiki]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">handwiki.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hand Wiki Biography:James E. Mc Donald</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hand Wiki Biography:James E. Mc Donald</span></span></span> Whatever broader arguments are made about pressures on McDonald, a forensic claim connecting his death to another researcher would need evidence extending beyond the fact that he investigated UFOs.

Eugene Mallove is different again because his 2004 death unquestionably was a homicide. Yet that makes the subsequent criminal investigation especially informative. Connecticut court records recount that Mallove was killed at his Norwich rental property after recently evicting tenants; Mozzelle Brown was ultimately convicted of murder and conspiracy to commit murder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on justia.com.</span></span></span> Contemporary police material also recorded property taken during the killing, including Mallove's wallet, telephone, camera and van.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eugenemallove.com/em_investigation.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eugenemallove.com">[Eugene Mallove]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eugenemallove.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eugene Mallove Press Releases on Mallove Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eugene Mallove Press Releases on Mallove Investigation</span></span></span>

The Mallove investigation is particularly instructive because an apparent forensic connection once led investigators in the wrong direction. Reporting on the case describes how a hair sample thought to have come from Mallove's van contributed to suspicion of an earlier defendant; defence lawyers later established that the state forensic laboratory had made an error about the hair's provenance, and murder charges against those earlier suspects were dropped in 2008.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://foreignpolicy.com/2016/07/07/the-coldest-case-cold-fusion-eugene-mallove-mit-infinite-energy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foreignpolicy.com">[Foreign Policy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foreignpolicy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Foreign Policy The Coldest Case – Foreign Policy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Foreign Policy The Coldest Case – Foreign Policy</span></span></span>

That episode is almost a miniature version of the problem confronting any proposed researcher “death pattern”. A forensic-looking association can be extremely persuasive until provenance, chain of custody and alternative explanations are checked. The proper response to an apparent match is therefore not to declare a common operation, but to try aggressively to falsify the connection.

Most importantly, the accessible evidence in these named cases does not reveal a recurring weapon, DNA profile, offender, vehicle, payment mechanism, communications account or other distinctive operational trace connecting Mallove's homicide with McDonald, Li or Eskridge. The absence of such public evidence does not prove that no undisclosed connection could exist. It means that a forensic claim of [coordination]({{ 'coordination/' | relative_url }}) cannot presently be made from the available record.

## Ordinary similarities are weak attribution

Several features frequently presented as suspicious in UFO and unconventional-propulsion narratives have little crime-linkage value by themselves.

Researchers may have government contacts because advanced aerospace research naturally intersects with defence agencies. Scientists in Huntsville may share employers, contractors, laboratories or acquaintances because Huntsville has a concentrated aerospace community. Researchers advocating unconventional theories may report professional [hostility]({{ 'hostility/' | relative_url }}) because controversial research generates disputes. People working in adjacent technical fields may attend the same conferences, correspond with the same specialists or seek funding from the same institutions.

Even similarities in the circumstances of death require a baseline. Two gunshot deaths are not automatically linked because firearms are common mechanisms of death. Two apparent burglaries are not linked merely because property disappeared. Two computers being missing may be more interesting, but investigators would still need to determine whether the machines were deliberately targeted, whether other valuables were ignored, who removed them and whether the same actor appears elsewhere.

The logic changes as features become more specific. Consider the difference between four hypothetical observations:

**Both victims were scientists.** Almost no linkage value.

**Both worked on unconventional propulsion.** Relevant to a possible motive, but still no perpetrator link.

**Both received threats using highly similar language.** Potentially significant, but authorship and independence must be established.

**The threats originated from the same authenticated account, and the same person's DNA was independently recovered from both killing scenes.** Now there is an operational connection requiring explanation.

This progression is what separates thematic pattern-finding from forensic linkage.

## A common operation should produce converging traces

A coordinated programme would also be expected to generate organisational evidence that a lone offender might not. Repeated operations require people to communicate, travel, obtain access, transfer money, acquire equipment and report results. Each activity can create records.

The evidential threshold should therefore rise, not fall, as the alleged conspiracy becomes larger. If a hypothesis proposes that an organisation killed multiple researchers across different states and decades, it must explain why investigators cannot identify any recurring perpetrators, logistical systems or distinctive forensic signatures across those events.

This does not mean every genuine conspiracy leaves a convenient written order. It means that the claim becomes substantially stronger when independent evidence converges. Forensic intelligence is valuable precisely because evidence collected in separate investigations can expose relationships that were initially invisible. NIJ describes this cross-case use of forensic information as a means of finding connections among evidence, suspects and crime scenes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/understanding-forensic-intelligence-nij-framework" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeUnderstanding Forensic Intelligence: The NIJ Framework &#124; National Institute of JusticeOctober 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 25, 2023</span></span></span> The FBI's DNA system and ATF's ballistic network demonstrate that the principle works operationally: unknown crime scenes can be connected before the responsible person has been identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/dna-fingerprint-act-of-2005-expungement-policy/codis-and-ndis-fact-sheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CODIS and NDIS Fact Sheet — FBI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CODIS and NDIS Fact Sheet — FBI...</span></span></span>

That provides a clear benchmark for the UFO and antigravity researcher question. A credible common-operation hypothesis would become markedly stronger if independent case files produced the same rare physical trace, the same unknown person, the same communications node or the same financial and travel infrastructure. Several of those occurring together would be stronger still.

Without such evidence, the fact that separate researchers died, disappeared from public work or experienced unusual circumstances remains a reason to examine each case carefully — not a forensic demonstration that the cases belong to the same operation.
