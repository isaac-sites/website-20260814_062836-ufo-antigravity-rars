## Introduction

In alleged assassination cases involving UFO, propulsion or [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers, the fact that a death was officially called suicide does not make the ruling infallible. But neither do professional conflict, secretive work, threats, missing files or distrust of authorities establish that a suicide was staged. A credible staging hypothesis needs something more concrete: **physical or chronological evidence that the death could not reasonably have occurred in the way the [suicide ruling]({{ 'suicide-ruling/' | relative_url }}) says it did**.

Forensic literature contains genuine examples of homicides initially made to resemble suicides. What changed those cases was not speculation about motive. Investigators found such things as wounds incompatible with self-infliction, blood evidence contradicting a witness's account, objects deposited after death, or body positions that could only have been arranged by somebody else. The central test for controversial [researcher deaths]({{ 'missing-denominator/' | relative_url }}) should therefore be the same: does the available evidence reveal a demonstrable contradiction between the body, the scene and the proposed sequence of events?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7691936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides - PMCNovember 25, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 2020</span></span></span>

## Forensic contradictions that could challenge self-infliction

The strongest evidence for a staged suicide would ordinarily come from the body itself. A forensic pathologist does not determine manner of death merely from the presence of a gun, ligature or apparent suicide note. The post-mortem examination establishes injuries and cause of death and is interpreted alongside the death scene and wider investigation. UK guidance expressly describes the post-mortem as a means of documenting injuries, collecting evidence and helping establish what may have happened and how and when death occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sudden-unexpected-death-medical-investigation/practice-advice-the-medical-investigation-of-suspected-homicide-accessible--2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span></span></span>

For an alleged researcher assassination, several types of contradiction would therefore carry substantial weight.

**An injury physically incompatible with self-infliction.** Gunshot location or trajectory can contribute to the assessment, although trajectory alone is not a reliable homicide detector. A study of 3,491 firearm deaths found statistically different trajectory patterns between suicides and homicides, but its authors stressed that trajectories should be interpreted with all the other evidence. A truly significant finding would be a wound whose range, angle, position or required firearm placement could not realistically be produced by the deceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1752928X21000202" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anatomical distribution and autopsy features of gunshot injuries to support the manner of death - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

A particularly instructive example appears in a 2020 forensic-pathology paper describing three homicides initially suspected to be suicides. In one case, investigators reconstructed the distance between the muzzle and the victim's face from gunpowder stippling. Measurements showed that producing the observed pattern through self-infliction would have required an implausible position and unconventional use of the victim's non-dominant hand. That finding became more compelling because other scene evidence independently contradicted the purported sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7691936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides - PMCNovember 25, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 2020</span></span></span>

**Multiple injuries that could not have been self-inflicted in sequence.** The same paper describes a woman with two contact gunshot wounds to the head. Both projectiles passed through deep central brain structures. The forensic conclusion was that she could not realistically have fired both shots herself. Blood evidence on her husband's clothing also contradicted his account of where he was when the shots were fired. He was subsequently convicted of murder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7691936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides - PMCNovember 25, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 2020</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that multiple wounds automatically mean homicide. Complex suicides exist, and unusual injuries can occur. What matters is whether medical evidence demonstrates that the proposed sequence is physiologically or mechanically impossible, rather than merely unusual. A recent forensic case involving hanging and stab wounds, for example, was still concluded to be suicide after scene investigation, interviews, autopsy, histology, toxicology and reconstruction were considered together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39054979/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Homicidal or suicidal death? Evidence-based approach to assessing cause of death in a peculiar forensic case - PubMed...</span></span></span>

**Evidence of assault independent of the fatal injury.** Defensive wounds, unexplained blunt-force trauma, restraint injuries, manual-strangulation injuries or other injuries inconsistent with the supposed suicide mechanism could materially change an assessment. Their significance depends on whether they can be dated and distinguished from resuscitation, previous injuries, [accidents]({{ 'accidents/' | relative_url }}) or the mechanics of the fatal event. UK suspicious-death guidance specifically requires forensic examination to document the extent and nature of injuries rather than assuming the apparent mechanism explains everything.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sudden-unexpected-death-medical-investigation/practice-advice-the-medical-investigation-of-suspected-homicide-accessible--2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span></span></span>

**Evidence that the apparent suicide mechanism was applied after death.** This is especially important in alleged staged hangings. Royal College of Pathologists guidance states that one purpose of an autopsy following known or suspected hanging is to determine whether the ligature was placed before or after death. The pathologist should look for findings supporting or refuting self-suspension and third-party involvement; suspicious findings should trigger escalation to a forensic examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rcpath.org/static/7b8672cc-e074-4c32-86e1e18f4d65296e/G179-Deaths-following-known-or-suspected-hanging.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcpath.org">[Royal College of Pathologists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcpath.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal College of Pathologists</span></span></span>

That is much stronger evidence than simply arguing that a hanging position “looks impossible”. Partial suspension and unusual positions can occur in genuine suicides, so intuitive judgements about what a person supposedly could or could not have done are weak substitutes for pathology. The important question is whether the tissues show evidence that the relevant compression occurred while circulation was still active. Forensic research on hanging likewise treats differentiation between genuine hanging and suspension of a corpse as a medical-forensic problem requiring examination of the ligature mark and underlying tissues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9914265/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Diagnostic Methods in Forensic Pathology: A New Sign in Death from Hanging - PMCJanuary 30, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 30, 2023</span></span></span>

## When the scene tells a different story from the body

A staged-suicide case becomes substantially stronger when independent parts of the scene point towards a sequence different from the official one. Modern death investigation therefore treats the body and its surroundings as one evidential system rather than analysing the autopsy in isolation. The US National Institute of Justice's 2024 death-investigation guide emphasises systematic medicolegal scene investigation, while UK guidance similarly stresses continuity of the body, scene assessment and communication between investigators and forensic pathologists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/death-investigation-guide-scene-investigator-2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">death investigation guide scene investigator 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of JusticeDeath Investigation: A Guide for the Scene Investigator, 2024 &#124; National Institute of Justice...</span></span></span>

One of the clearest published examples shows what a meaningful chronological contradiction looks like. In the third case reported by Prahlow and colleagues, a woman claimed that shots had been fired through a door before her husband suffered his fatal gunshot wound. Investigators later found fragments of that door **on top of his body and on top of partially dried blood**. The physical scene therefore preserved an order of events inconsistent with her account: the relevant shot through the door had occurred after the victim was already dead or bleeding at the scene. She eventually confessed to killing him and staging the scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7691936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides - PMCNovember 25, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 2020</span></span></span>

That kind of evidence is qualitatively different from a suspicious coincidence. It creates a testable chronological problem.

Comparable evidence in an alleged researcher suicide could include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* bloodstain or biological evidence showing that the fatal injury occurred somewhere other than where the body was supposedly found;
* physical deposits showing that objects were moved or introduced after the fatal injury;
* reliable digital records placing another person at the scene despite a contrary account;
* injuries, firearm range or bullet trajectory incompatible with the body's documented position;
* evidence that the weapon or ligature could not have reached its final position through the proposed self-inflicted sequence;
* trace or DNA evidence establishing relevant third-party handling when an innocent explanation is implausible;
* or independently authenticated electronic records that contradict the reported discovery time or movements of witnesses.

</div>

None should normally be treated as decisive alone. Their value comes from **convergence**. Research into staged crime scenes has found that combining physical and digital evidence can expose staging where either category might remain ambiguous by itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29783179/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Staged crime scene determination by handling physical and digital evidence: Reports and review of the literature - PubMedMay 16, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 16, 2018</span></span></span>

### Body position can become a powerful chronological test

Post-mortem changes can sometimes reveal whether a body has been repositioned, although such evidence requires specialist interpretation. Likewise, blood patterns, environmental evidence and object placement can preserve information about sequence. What matters is not that a corpse appears awkwardly positioned but whether the position is objectively incompatible with the body's post-mortem findings or with the physical events required by the suicide hypothesis.

The most striking example from the published staged-suicide literature is remarkably simple. An 18-year-old man was found with a contact gunshot wound to his head and a firearm nearby. There was even a letter asking his girlfriend for forgiveness, superficially strengthening the suicide interpretation. But his hands were found clasped together. Given the catastrophic brain injury, investigators concluded that he could not have fired the shot and subsequently arranged his hands in that position. Another man later confessed to shooting him while he slept and placing the gun nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7691936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides - PMCNovember 25, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 2020</span></span></span>

That example also demonstrates why suicide-note-like material must not override physical evidence. A seemingly incriminating letter existed, but investigators eventually regarded it as coincidental because the body's position contradicted self-infliction.

## Toxicology matters when it changes what the victim could do

Toxicology can contribute to a staged-suicide assessment, but the mere presence of alcohol, prescription drugs or illicit substances proves very little. People can die by suicide while intoxicated. The US National Violent Death Reporting System explicitly includes suicides committed while under the influence of substances voluntarily consumed, and toxicological testing is routinely part of violent-death investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7405a1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdc.gov.</span></span></span>

The more probative question is whether toxicology creates a **functional contradiction**. If a staging hypothesis claims that somebody else incapacitated the victim before arranging the scene, useful evidence would include an unexpected drug capable of producing the necessary impairment, reliable evidence concerning how and when it entered the body, and other findings independently connecting that incapacitation to another actor.

Conversely, claiming that a concentration “seems too high” for someone to have performed a particular action is not enough. Post-mortem toxicology involves questions of tolerance, redistribution, metabolism, timing and interactions between substances. The result has to be interpreted in the circumstances of the individual death. The forensic case becomes compelling only when toxicology joins other evidence to make the proposed self-inflicted sequence medically or mechanically untenable.

This distinction is particularly important in controversial researcher deaths because retrospective conspiracy narratives can turn almost any drug finding into evidence of poisoning. A genuine poisoning-and-staging case would require positive evidence of poisoning, not simply the discovery that drugs were present.

## Digital evidence can test the death-scene chronology

Phones, computers, access logs, CCTV, vehicle records and authenticated communications can be particularly valuable because they may establish a chronology independently of witness recollection. A forensic review of staged scenes published in 2018 described cases in which the correlation of physical and digital evidence was crucial to determining the true manner of death, including a homicide in which a gunshot death had been staged as suicide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29783179/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Staged crime scene determination by handling physical and digital evidence: Reports and review of the literature - PubMedMay 16, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 16, 2018</span></span></span>

For an alleged assassination of a researcher, the strongest digital evidence would therefore not simply be an email saying the person felt threatened. It would be data tied directly to the fatal event: an authenticated record showing another person's presence, manipulation of devices after the estimated time of death, deletion or fabrication traceable to someone else, or communications establishing a false account of the victim's final movements.

Messages predicting one's own murder occupy a more complicated category. They can make a later suicide ruling legitimately worth scrutinising, particularly if authenticated and written before death. But they do not establish how the person ultimately died. People can fear assassination and later die by suicide; they can also accurately anticipate danger. The message becomes substantially stronger evidence only when the death scene independently supports the scenario the person predicted.

That distinction is directly relevant to the renewed discussion surrounding propulsion researcher Amy Eskridge. Reporting in 2026 publicised messages attributed to Eskridge in which she reportedly denied that she would kill herself and expressed fears about what might happen to her. Her 2022 death has been reported as a self-inflicted gunshot death. Those messages are relevant to evaluating the narrative, but publicly accessible reporting has not supplied the underlying police and forensic files needed to determine whether the physical evidence contradicts self-infliction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hindustantimes.com.</span></span></span>

In evidential terms, therefore, the crucial missing question is not whether Eskridge had expressed fear. It is whether her autopsy, firearm evidence, toxicology, scene photographs, blood evidence, device records and investigative chronology contain contradictions comparable to those that have exposed documented staged suicides. Without those records, the strongest conclusion available from the public evidence is uncertainty, not proof of staging.

## Why motive and institutional conflict are not enough

The [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) context makes motive arguments unusually tempting. A researcher may have worked on unconventional propulsion, challenged official explanations, claimed knowledge of classified technology or clashed with powerful organisations. If that person then dies by suicide, it is easy to construct a plausible reason why somebody might have wanted them silenced.

But motive answers a different question from manner of death.

A staged homicide requires an offender to alter or arrange evidence so that a killing resembles self-inflicted death. A study of detected staged scenes identified 16 staged suicides among 115 staging cases examined and found firearms, hanging and asphyxia among the recurring methods. The study demonstrates that staged suicides genuinely occur; it does not provide a licence to infer one whenever a victim had enemies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/getting-away-with-murder-an-examination-of-detected-homicides-sta/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.bond.edu.au">[Bond University Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.bond.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bond University Research PortalGetting Away With Murder: An Examination of Detected Homicides Staged as Suicides - Bond University Resear...</span></span></span>

This distinction is also built into formal violent-death investigation. The CDC's National Violent Death Reporting System integrates law-enforcement information, coroner or medical-examiner records, toxicology and [death certificates]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }}) precisely because determining circumstances requires several evidential streams. Suicide itself is classified on the basis of a preponderance of evidence indicating intentional self-inflicted force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nvdrs/faq/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdc.gov.</span></span></span>

Institutional secrecy is similarly ambiguous. Missing public records can prevent outsiders from verifying an official conclusion, which is a legitimate limitation. But **absence of publicly available evidence is not positive evidence that contradictory evidence exists**. There is a major difference between “the autopsy has not been published, so the suicide ruling cannot independently be checked” and “the autopsy demonstrates injuries incompatible with suicide”.

That distinction should govern assessment of disputed UFO-researcher deaths. Claims of threats, classified programmes or professional suppression may justify looking more closely. They cannot substitute for the evidence that closer examination is supposed to find.

## What would materially change the assessment?

For a researcher death already classified as suicide, the staging hypothesis would become substantially more credible if previously unavailable primary records produced a cluster of mutually reinforcing contradictions. The most persuasive pattern would be an autopsy finding inconsistent with self-infliction, supported by scene reconstruction and an independent chronology pointing to another person's involvement.

A particularly strong evidential chain might therefore look like this: the post-mortem establishes that the fatal mechanism or wound could not reasonably have been self-produced; scene evidence shows that the body, weapon or other objects were repositioned; digital or witness evidence places another actor at the scene; and that person's account conflicts with physical evidence in ways that cannot plausibly be accidental. Documented staged-suicide cases show exactly why such convergence matters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7691936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Homicides Disguised as Staged Suicides - PMCNovember 25, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 2020</span></span></span>

By contrast, a researcher having enemies, saying they were being watched, planning future work, appearing cheerful shortly before death or stating that they would never kill themselves should be treated as contextual evidence. Those facts can justify scrutiny and can become important when combined with forensic contradictions, but they do not themselves demonstrate homicide.

This is also why competent initial death investigation matters so much. Updated UK guidance explicitly warns that if an unexpected death is incorrectly classified as non-suspicious at the outset, no forensic examination may occur and a potential homicide could consequently be missed. It calls for a systematic approach to unexpected deaths and forensic post-mortem examination when homicide is suspected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sudden-unexpected-death-medical-investigation/practice-advice-the-medical-investigation-of-suspected-homicide-accessible--2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span></span></span>

The appropriate evidential standard for alleged UFO or antigravity researcher assassinations is therefore demanding but straightforward: **look for evidence that the suicide scenario fails physically or chronologically**. A threat supplies a possible motive. A prediction supplies a reason for caution. Secrecy supplies unanswered questions. But a wound that could not have been self-inflicted, a body demonstrably moved after death, a scene arranged in the wrong sequence, or independent records placing another actor where they denied being are the kinds of findings that can turn suspicion of a staged suicide into a serious forensic hypothesis.
