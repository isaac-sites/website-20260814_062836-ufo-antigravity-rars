## Introduction

A researcher’s pre-death warning becomes genuinely stronger evidence only when facts **outside the warning itself** converge with it. An authenticated message can establish that the researcher expressed fear before dying. To corroborate the feared threat, however, investigators need separate evidence: original message data, independently recorded witness accounts, documented threats or incidents, physical or digital traces, and death-investigation findings that fit the warning better than ordinary alternatives.

That distinction is particularly important in disputed deaths connected with UFO, advanced-propulsion or alleged [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) research. Amy Eskridge’s case illustrates the problem. Messages attributed to her reportedly warned before her June 2022 death that a later report of suicide or overdose should not be believed. The existence and timing of such a message are potentially verifiable propositions. Whether somebody was actually threatening her, and whether that threat caused her death, are different propositions requiring independent proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hindustan TimesAmy Eskridge update: UFO scientist&#x27;s old texts cast doubt on cause of death; ‘would never kill myself’ &#124; Hindustan TimesAp...</span></span></span>

## Authenticating the warning and fixing its timeline

The first task is not deciding whether a warning sounds convincing. It is establishing **what the original evidence actually is**. A screenshot reproduced years later is materially weaker than the original conversation preserved on the sender’s or recipient’s device. Ideally, investigators would obtain the device or a forensic extraction containing the message, its surrounding conversation, account identifiers, timestamps and associated metadata. NIST’s mobile-forensics guidance treats preservation, acquisition, examination, analysis and reporting as distinct parts of a forensically sound process rather than treating an isolated screenshot as the finished evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/publications/guidelines-mobile-device-forensics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidelines mobile device forensics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidelines on Mobile Device Forensics &#124; NISTMay 15, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2014</span></span></span>

The same principle applies to email, cloud accounts, photographs, voice notes and social-media messages. The Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence recommends preserving the source of electronically produced material, creating a static copy, calculating and recording cryptographic hashes and maintaining chain-of-custody documentation. For cloud evidence it additionally recommends retaining acquisition logs and checking provider-supplied digital signatures or hash values where available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/18-f-002-2-0/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE - SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Best Practices for Digital Evidence Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDEBest Practices for Digital Evidence Collection - SWGDENovember 20, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2025</span></span></span>

That allows several different questions to be tested separately:

* **Did the message exist before the death?** A timestamp on an original device, contemporaneous email record, cloud record or recipient’s independently preserved copy is much stronger than a later transcription.
* **Did the researcher send it?** Account ownership, device data, surrounding correspondence, distinctive content and testimony from someone who received it can contribute to authentication. The general evidential principle is that an item must be supported sufficiently to show it is what its proponent claims it is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title28a-node232-article9-rule901" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code</span></span></span>

* **Has it changed?** Preserved originals and cryptographic hashes can help establish that a collected digital file has not subsequently been altered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swgde.org/documents/published-complete-listing/18-f-002-best-practices-for-digital-evidence-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swgde.org">[SWGDE - SWGDE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swgde.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Best Practices for Digital Evidence Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWGDEBest Practices for Digital Evidence Collection - SWGDEJune 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 30, 2025</span></span></span>
* **What surrounded the warning?** The messages immediately before and after it can materially change its meaning. A complete conversation may reveal whether a remark was literal, hypothetical, sarcastic, repetitive or tied to a specific incident.
* **Who possessed copies before the death?** If several recipients independently retained the same warning before knowing how the person would die, that is considerably stronger than several people later reproducing a copy obtained from one source.

In Eskridge’s case, reporting in 2026 reproduced a message attributed to 13 May 2022, less than a month before her death on 11 June. Reports also attribute other messages about alleged harassment and physical attacks to her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hindustan TimesAmy Eskridge update: UFO scientist&#x27;s old texts cast doubt on cause of death; ‘would never kill myself’ &#124; Hindustan TimesAp...</span></span></span> The next evidential advance would therefore not come from another article quoting those messages. It would come from authentication of the underlying records: original devices or exports, full conversation histories, dates, recipients and any corresponding provider records that remain obtainable.

This is an important distinction between **content corroboration** and **document authentication**. Proving that Eskridge really sent a particular warning would substantially establish what she said and when. It would not, without more, establish that her explanation for why she felt endangered was correct.

## What would independently corroborate the alleged threat?

Once the warning is authenticated, investigators can break it into claims capable of external testing. A vague statement such as “people are after me” offers few testable particulars. A statement naming a person, vehicle, location, communication account, break-in, injury or particular date creates opportunities for evidence independent of the researcher’s own account.

The strongest corroboration would come from evidence generated for reasons unrelated to the later controversy. If a researcher claimed before death that somebody repeatedly followed her to a laboratory, for example, contemporaneous security-camera footage, access logs, vehicle records or a colleague’s dated report could corroborate part of that claim without depending on the warning. Similarly, alleged threatening messages become more significant if investigators can recover the originating account, provider data or corresponding copy on another device rather than merely finding [screenshots]({{ 'screenshots/' | relative_url }}) supplied by the recipient.

Physical allegations need physical testing. A claimed break-in could potentially be compared with police reports, building-access records, damaged locks, alarms, CCTV or photographs taken at the time. An alleged injury might be checked against contemporaneous medical records, photographs and clinical findings. None necessarily proves who caused the event, but each can establish whether something described in the warning had an observable counterpart.

The key question is therefore not simply **“Is there evidence consistent with her story?”** It is **“Did evidence created independently of her story record the same specific event?”** That is a much harder standard, but it is what turns repetition into corroboration.

## Witnesses matter most when their accounts are genuinely independent

Witness testimony can provide powerful corroboration, but counting witnesses is misleading unless investigators establish where each person obtained the information.

Suppose three colleagues say after a researcher’s death that she told them she was receiving threats. That may initially look like three corroborating witnesses. But there are several very different possibilities. She may have separately told all three before her death; one colleague may have told the other two afterwards; or all three may simply have read the same circulated screenshot. Only the first arrangement provides three substantially independent observations of what the researcher was saying.

This problem is well recognised in evidence gathering. College of Policing guidance recommends separating witnesses when obtaining initial accounts because experimental research has repeatedly found that exposure to co-witness information can cause people to report details they did not themselves observe. It also recommends establishing the source of a witness’s information — effectively asking whether the person actually saw or heard something themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.college.police.uk/guidance/obtaining-initial-accounts/witness-separation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: college.police.uk">[College of Policing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">college.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College of Policing Witness separation &#124; College of Policing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">College of PolicingWitness separation &#124; College of PolicingNovember 24, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 24, 2020</span></span></span>

That principle becomes especially important in a UFO-related case once it has circulated through social media, podcasts and news reports. Exposure to later accounts can blur the boundary between personal memory and information learned afterwards. College of Policing guidance specifically warns that exposure to conversations, media and other narratives can affect recollection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.college.police.uk/guidance/obtaining-initial-accounts/advice-information-exposure" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: college.police.uk">[College of Policing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">college.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College of Policing Advice on information exposure &#124; College of Policing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">College of Policing Advice on information exposure &#124; College of Policing</span></span></span>

Accordingly, useful witness corroboration would preserve the earliest available account and ask concrete questions: When did the researcher speak to you? Was anyone else present? Did you write anything down at the time? Did she identify a particular threat? Did you personally observe any threatening incident? Who had you discussed the matter with before giving this account?

A contemporaneous email from a colleague saying, before the death, “Amy told me today that X threatened her at Y location” has a different evidential character from a 2026 interview in which the colleague recalls the same conversation after years of publicity. Neither should automatically be dismissed, but they do not deserve identical weight.

## The death scene must be tested against the warning, not interpreted through it

A particularly dangerous reasoning error is to start with the warning and reinterpret every ambiguous feature of the subsequent death as confirmation. Proper death investigation works in the opposite direction: scene evidence, injuries, toxicology, medical history, digital evidence and witness information are gathered systematically and then evaluated together.

The US National Institute of Justice’s 2024 death-investigation guide emphasises medicolegal death investigation as an independent but collaborative process alongside law enforcement. Modern reviews of the US system likewise describe cause-and-manner determinations as drawing on examination of the body, circumstances of death, medical records, toxicology and other laboratory testing, and evidence collected at the scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/death-investigation-guide-scene-investigator-2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">death investigation guide scene investigator 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">death investigation guide scene investigator 2024</span></span></span>

For an alleged pre-death warning, investigators would therefore ask whether the physical case contains anomalies that independently fit the threatened scenario. Depending on the circumstances, relevant material could include scene photographs and measurements, fingerprints or DNA, firearm examination, gunshot-related evidence, entry or exit records, surveillance footage, telephone activity, computer records, toxicology, injuries and the estimated chronology of death.

The methodological point is more important than any particular forensic technique: **the physical findings must be capable of challenging the warning as well as supporting it**. Crime-scene guidance stresses methodical preservation and collection because contamination or lost evidence can make later reconstruction impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/crime-scene-investigation-guides-law-enforcement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

Forensic pathology works similarly. Current UK suspected-homicide guidance, for example, requires investigators to communicate scene information, witness evidence, significant times, toxicology and other scientific results to the forensic pathologist, while recognising that the pathologist’s opinion may depend on the wider circumstances. It also envisages investigative material being used to assist or challenge pathological conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sudden-unexpected-death-medical-investigation/practice-advice-the-medical-investigation-of-suspected-homicide-accessible--2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Practice advice: The medical investigation of suspected homicide (accessible</span></span></span>

This is why a warning that specifically predicts “suicide” cannot logically overturn a suicide finding by itself. It instead creates a reason to examine whether the independent forensic evidence supporting that finding is robust. If scene reconstruction, pathology, toxicology, digital chronology and other evidence all independently support self-inflicted death and reveal no persuasive evidence of another participant, the warning remains evidence of prior fear rather than evidence of homicide. Conversely, unexplained physical findings inconsistent with the official reconstruction would materially increase its significance.

## Repeated sourcing is not independent corroboration

UFO cases are especially vulnerable to what can be called a **false source count**. Ten webpages may appear to confirm the same allegation while all ten ultimately derive it from one interview, screenshot, social-media thread or witness.

The Amy Eskridge reporting provides a useful illustration. Recent articles repeat similar claims attributed to Franc Milburn: that he corresponded with Eskridge, that she warned against accepting suicide or accident as an explanation, that she described harassment, and that he spoke with her shortly before her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://americanalmanac.com/disturbing-final-messages-from-dead-researcher-amy-eskridge-fuel-demands-for-fbi-investigation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: americanalmanac.com">[American Almanac]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">americanalmanac.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Almanac Amy Eskridge Death: Scientist&#x27;s Warning Before Suicide Ruling</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Almanac Amy Eskridge Death: Scientist&#x27;s Warning Before Suicide Ruling</span></span></span> Multiple publications repeating those claims increase their visibility; they do not automatically create multiple independent sources for the underlying events.

This is why verification practice distinguishes between corroborating a claim and reproducing somebody else’s reporting. The Associated Press describes verification as checking information against documents and independent reliable sources, while separately authenticating authorship, metadata, timing and location where relevant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ap.org/solutions/verification-at-ap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ap.org">[The Associated Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Associated Press Verification at AP &#124; The Associated Press</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Associated Press Verification at AP &#124; The Associated Press</span></span></span>

A useful way to audit a controversial warning is to trace every important assertion back to its **earliest observable source**. If five articles quote one screenshot supplied by one person, the evidence graph has one originating branch, not five. If the same message independently exists on the deceased’s device, the recipient’s device and a service-provider record, the situation is different: distinct evidence repositories are converging on the same underlying event.

The same rule applies to witnesses. Two people repeating what a third person told them are not three independent witnesses to the original event. Two colleagues who separately recorded the same unusual incident before speaking to each other are far more probative.

## What convergence would actually change the assessment?

No single corroborating item necessarily proves homicide. The strongest case would instead be cumulative, with evidence from sources that have different routes of origin.

Consider a hypothetical warning from an advanced-propulsion researcher: she writes that a named individual threatened her on Monday, reports the threat to a colleague on Tuesday and is later found dead. Investigators then discover that the colleague documented the disclosure on Tuesday; building CCTV independently places the named individual at the relevant location on Monday; the researcher’s phone contains the original warning; provider data supports its timing; and forensic evidence from the death scene contradicts a purely self-inflicted reconstruction. Those pieces do more than repeat one another. They connect **warning, external event and death through different evidence streams**.

By contrast, an authenticated warning plus ten later interviews discussing that warning establishes considerably less. It may prove beyond reasonable dispute that the researcher was frightened, but the evidence still originates with the researcher’s belief.

For cases involving UFO or alleged antigravity researchers, the most useful evidential hierarchy is therefore:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Authenticated warning:** establish the original communication, author, recipient and pre-death date.
2. **Contemporaneous independent records:** look for reports, medical records, access logs, photographs, CCTV, emails or other material created before the death that matches specific allegations.
3. **Independent witnesses:** identify what each person personally saw or heard before exposure to later narratives.
4. **Threat-source evidence:** determine whether alleged threatening communications, surveillance or intrusions can be traced beyond the deceased’s description of them.
5. **Death-scene and forensic evidence:** test whether pathology, toxicology, physical evidence and digital chronology support or conflict with the official reconstruction.
6. **Convergence:** ask whether these evidence streams independently point towards the same external actor or mechanism rather than merely sharing the deceased as their common source.

</div>

That final distinction sets the threshold that matters. A genuine pre-death warning can establish a real historical fact: **the researcher issued the warning**. Independent corroboration begins only when evidence that did not originate solely from that warning verifies its testable particulars. Evidence independently connecting those particulars to the subsequent death would be stronger still. Until that convergence exists, the responsible conclusion is narrower: the warning may justify further scrutiny, but it cannot by itself convert suspicion into proof of an external threat or homicide.
