## Introduction

When a UFO or [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researcher dies in circumstances officially classified as suicide, later claims of assassination cannot be assessed simply by asking whether the person had enemies, handled controversial ideas or had expressed fear. The central question is chronological: **what evidence of suicidal behaviour, [personal crisis]({{ 'personal-crisis/' | relative_url }}) and death-scene findings existed before the assassination theory arose?** A documented earlier suicide attempt is particularly important because previous attempts are recognised by both the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Institute of Mental Health as major suicide-risk factors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/suicide/risk-factors/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">risk factors</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Risk and Protective Factors for Suicide &#124; Suicide Prevention &#124; CDCMay 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2026</span></span></span>

Within the UFO-research literature, James E. McDonald provides the clearest example of why chronology matters. His death has occasionally been recast as the removal of an unusually credible UFO advocate, yet accounts of his [final months]({{ 'final-months/' | relative_url }}) describe an earlier gunshot suicide attempt, severe personal upheaval and a second fatal shooting accompanied by circumstances contemporaneously treated as suicide. Amy Eskridge presents a materially different evidential problem: her 2022 death was officially described as suicide, while messages publicised years later reportedly show her explicitly predicting that a future suicide report would be false. Those messages deserve examination, but without the underlying medical-examiner and [police files]({{ 'police-files/' | relative_url }}) in the public record, they cannot by themselves establish that the death was staged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newenglishreview.org/articles/james-e-mcdonald-ufos-and-nasa/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newenglishreview.org">[newenglishreview.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newenglishreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New English Review James E. ## Why prior behaviour changes the assessment

A staged-suicide hypothesis becomes substantially harder to sustain when there is independently documented suicidal behaviour before the fatal event. This is not because an earlier attempt makes later homicide impossible. It is because a staging theory must then explain two things rather than one: the suspicious final death and the earlier self-harm episode.

That distinction is especially important in the case of atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald. McDonald was not merely a peripheral UFO enthusiast. He was a University of Arizona atmospheric scientist who became an outspoken advocate for scientific investigation of unidentified flying objects, interviewed large numbers of witnesses and appeared before the US House Committee on Science and Astronautics in 1968. His American Meteorological Society obituary noted both his established atmospheric-science career and the conflicts his UFO work generated with other scientists and engineers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/obit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mc Carthy Dissertation: James E. Mc Donald Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mc Carthy Dissertation: James E. Mc Donald Obituary</span></span></span>

The assassination interpretation usually begins with those conflicts. McDonald attacked what he considered inadequate official investigation of UFO reports and criticised the conclusions of the University of Colorado UFO project. From a suppression narrative, he therefore had the kind of profile that might appear to supply motive: scientific standing, public advocacy and disputes with institutions responsible for examining the subject.

The evidence from his final months, however, points in another direction. Retrospective accounts describe his wife seeking a divorce amid serious marital problems and McDonald attempting suicide with a firearm weeks before his death. The first shooting did not kill him but severely damaged his vision. Atmospheric scientist Petr Chylek, writing in 2026 about information he had received from McDonald's colleague Walter Orr Roberts, described the marital crisis followed by the earlier suicide attempt and explicitly rejected a UFO-assassination explanation. Other biographical accounts likewise record an earlier self-inflicted gunshot before the fatal event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newenglishreview.org/articles/james-e-mcdonald-ufos-and-nasa/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newenglishreview.org">[New English Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newenglishreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New English Review James E. Mc Donald, UFOs, and NASA – New English Review</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New English Review James E. Mc Donald, UFOs, and NASA – New English Review</span></span></span>

That prior attempt is unusually consequential evidence. According to current CDC guidance, a previous suicide attempt is an individual suicide-risk factor; NIMH similarly lists a history of attempts among the main factors associated with later suicide risk. Neither agency treats such a history as proof of what caused any particular death, but it gives the McDonald chronology a documented behavioural continuity that a murder theory has to overcome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/suicide/risk-factors/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">risk factors</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Risk and Protective Factors for Suicide &#124; Suicide Prevention &#124; CDCMay 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2026</span></span></span>

The fatal event also did not first become a suicide only after decades of retrospective interpretation. Contemporary reporting described McDonald's June 1971 death as an apparent suicide, while later accounts report that a firearm and an apparent suicide note were found with him. His professional obituary confirms that he died on 13 June 1971, although it understandably concentrates on his scientific career rather than the manner of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_E._McDonald" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">James E. Mc Donald</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">James E. Mc Donald</span></span></span>

The resulting evidential picture is not “McDonald had problems, therefore assassination is impossible”. It is narrower and stronger: **a staged-death claim must account for a documented earlier attempt using the same broad method, major contemporaneous personal distress and a final scene interpreted as suicide at the time**. Merely showing that McDonald was controversial, criticised government policy or had an FBI file would not answer those points.

## Contemporary findings matter more than retrospective suspicion

The timing of evidence is often as important as its content. Records made before or immediately after a death generally have greater evidential value for reconstructing what happened than suspicions that emerge once the victim has become part of a wider conspiracy narrative. That does not make contemporary authorities infallible. It means that later claims need comparably strong evidence if they are to displace an earlier conclusion.

This distinction becomes clear when comparing McDonald with Amy Eskridge, the Huntsville researcher whose Institute for Exotic Science promoted unconventional propulsion and gravity-modification work. Eskridge died on 11 June 2022 at the age of 34. Her family obituary described her scientific interests and leadership of the institute but did not give a cause of death. Reporting that gained much wider attention in 2026 stated that authorities had classified the death as suicide by self-inflicted gunshot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">amy eskridge obituary</span></span></span>

The evidence later cited against that conclusion is striking. Former British intelligence officer Franc Milburn has said that Eskridge sent him a message in May 2022 saying that if reports ever claimed she had killed herself or died by overdose, those reports should not be believed. He has also described her as having reported harassment, threats and what she believed were attacks using directed energy. The reported messages and earlier recorded interviews therefore existed, if authentically dated and reproduced, before her death rather than being recollections invented years afterwards. That makes them relevant evidence rather than mere post-event folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yahoo.com/news/videos/fbi-probe-researcher-death-welcome-010757268.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yahoo.com">[Yahoo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yahoo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fbi probe researcher death welcome 010757268</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fbi probe researcher death welcome 010757268</span></span></span>

But their meaning is less straightforward than the most dramatic retellings imply. A statement such as “I would never kill myself” does not function as a forensic veto over a subsequent suicide determination. Suicide assessment is inherently probabilistic: NIMH cautions that most people who possess recognised risk factors do not attempt suicide, while stressful events, relationship difficulties and other circumstances can interact unpredictably. Conversely, a person's prior denial of suicidal intent cannot demonstrate that their later death was homicide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/suicide-faq" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nimh.nih.gov">[National Institute of Mental Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nimh.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Institute of Mental Health Frequently Asked Questions About Suicide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Institute of Mental HealthFrequently Asked Questions About Suicide - National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)...</span></span></span>

The Eskridge case therefore exposes a genuine public-record problem rather than proving staging. Recent reporting has repeatedly noted that the detailed police and medical-examiner materials needed to evaluate the scene independently have not been made broadly available in the sources reporting on the controversy. Public readers consequently lack the evidence needed to test basic questions: firearm positioning, wound characteristics, gunshot-residue results, fingerprints or DNA, toxicology, entry and exit evidence, scene photographs, the exact provenance of any weapon, witness timelines and the reasoning behind the official manner-of-death determination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://factually.co/fact-checks/justice/amy-eskridge-autopsy-police-report-how-to-obtain-7bb268" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: factually.co">[Factually]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">factually.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Is There an Official Autopsy or Police Report Availabl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Is There an Official Autopsy or Police Report Availabl</span></span></span>

That evidential gap cuts both ways. It is not sound to infer that unreleased records conceal murder. It is equally unsatisfactory to answer every question by simply repeating that a death was “officially ruled suicide”. Where primary records are unavailable, the responsible conclusion is narrower: the official ruling stands, potentially significant contrary statements have emerged, and outsiders currently lack enough primary forensic material to determine whether those statements expose a flaw in the ruling.

The renewed federal interest in a wider collection of scientist deaths and disappearances during 2026 does not itself change that standard. Reporting by the *Wall Street Journal* and *The Guardian* described federal scrutiny of possible connections while also stressing that no common plot had been established and that families or known circumstances offered ordinary explanations for several cases. Inclusion of a death in a broader review is therefore grounds for further investigation, not retroactive proof that its original suicide determination was false.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/how-a-fringe-conspiracy-theory-about-missing-scientists-got-the-fbis-attention-d61de97c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wsj.com">[The Wall Street Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wsj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how a fringe conspiracy theory about missing scientists got the fbis attention d61de97c</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The case of Melissa Casias, a New Mexico administrative assistant gone missing, became a flashpoint, with internet theorists linking her...</span></span></span>

## What would actually support a staged-death claim?

A persuasive staging case requires evidence capable of explaining the death scene better than suicide does. Motive is not enough. Neither is a researcher's controversial subject matter, alleged surveillance, professional conflict or statement that they feared being killed. Those facts may justify scrutiny, but they do not determine manner of death.

The most probative material would normally come from several independent categories that converge on the same conclusion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Physical incompatibility with self-infliction.** Wound trajectory, firearm position, trace evidence, toxicology, bloodstain patterns or other scene findings would need to conflict materially with the official reconstruction.
* **Third-party presence or intervention.** Reliable surveillance, access records, communications, eyewitness evidence, unidentified DNA or a defensible forensic timeline could establish another person's involvement.
* **Defects in the original investigation.** Missing evidence, contradictory reports, broken chain of custody, demonstrable alteration of records or an examiner changing conclusions without adequate forensic reason would matter far more than the mere fact that documents are difficult to obtain.
* **Authenticated contemporaneous threats tied to a capable actor.** A threat becomes much stronger when its source, timing, capability and connection to the eventual death can be established independently.
* **Evidence linking the supposed motive to the research itself.** Even proof that a suicide determination was wrong would not automatically prove a UFO- or antigravity-suppression motive. The second proposition requires its own evidence.

</div>

Retrospective “psychological autopsies” — attempts to reconstruct a deceased person's state of mind from interviews, records and communications — can add context but are not substitutes for physical evidence. A 2024 review by researchers at the University of Manchester identified recurring problems with validity, reliability, standardisation, informant bias and data handling in psychological-autopsy research. That matters especially in conspiracy-linked cases, where friends, relatives, investigators and online communities may interpret the same statements through very different assumptions after the death has acquired cultural significance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39470078/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The same caution applies in reverse. Evidence of depression, financial trouble, divorce or professional humiliation should never be treated as sufficient proof of suicide. The CDC specifically emphasises that suicide is rarely caused by one circumstance. Personal crises are therefore contextual evidence, not a complete reconstruction of a death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/suicide/risk-factors/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">risk factors</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Risk and Protective Factors for Suicide &#124; Suicide Prevention &#124; CDCMay 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2026</span></span></span>

## James McDonald shows why chronology can outweigh motive theories

Among UFO-related alleged assassination cases, McDonald's chronology is unusually damaging to a staged-suicide theory precisely because the potentially incriminating facts about his institutional conflicts are real. His public UFO advocacy, clashes with critics and willingness to confront official conclusions are not inventions. Yet those facts coexist with a sequence of personal events and suicidal behaviour that predates his death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/obit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mc Carthy Dissertation: James E. Mc Donald Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mc Carthy Dissertation: James E. Mc Donald Obituary</span></span></span>

A suppression argument that starts from motive — “powerful organisations disliked his work” — therefore skips the strongest contrary evidence. To remain viable, it would have to explain why McDonald apparently attempted to take his life before the fatal incident, why that attempt coincided with severe marital distress, and what forensic evidence shows that the later shooting was produced by somebody else. No comparable public evidence establishing such intervention has emerged in the sources reviewed here.

This is also why claims that institutional [hostility]({{ 'hostility/' | relative_url }}) “drove” a researcher to suicide should be kept separate from claims that the institution murdered them. McDonald plainly experienced professional controversy surrounding his UFO work, and hostile treatment could conceivably have contributed to distress. But social or professional pressure being one factor in a person's deterioration is an entirely different causal claim from an assassination disguised as suicide. The former can be plausible without supplying evidence for the latter.

## Amy Eskridge shows why a warning is important but not decisive

Eskridge presents almost the inverse evidential structure. Unlike McDonald, the most discussed public evidence is not a documented earlier suicide attempt but a reported pre-death declaration denying that she would take her own life. That deserves more weight than an acquaintance saying years later that “she would never have done it”, because the communication was reportedly made before her death and specifically anticipated a suicide narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hindustantimes.com.</span></span></span>

Its evidential force nevertheless depends on authentication and context. Investigators would want the original device or complete message export, verified timestamps, the surrounding conversation rather than selected screenshots, confirmation of the recipient and evidence that the message had not been edited. They would also need to determine what prompted the statement. Eskridge reportedly believed she was being harassed and attacked, which could support an actual-threat hypothesis, but those beliefs alone do not identify an assailant or establish that the alleged attacks occurred as she understood them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/resurfaced-video-amy-eskridge-death-questions-1793252" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">resurfaced video amy eskridge death questions 1793252</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">resurfaced video amy eskridge death questions 1793252</span></span></span>

This is where online retellings frequently commit an evidential leap. The reasoning becomes: she feared assassination; she predicted a false suicide narrative; she later died in a death called suicide; therefore the prediction came true. The first three propositions may all be reportable facts without logically establishing the fourth. To bridge the gap, investigators still need forensic evidence showing that another person caused the fatal injury.

The family's position also belongs in the evidential picture. Recent reporting on the broader 2026 scientist-death controversy says members of Eskridge's family have rejected interpretations presenting her death as part of a covert assassination programme, while press accounts emphasise that several supposedly connected cases have known personal or medical explanations. Family opinion cannot determine manner of death either, but it is relevant when conspiracy versions imply that those closest to a victim unanimously regarded the official account as impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/how-a-fringe-conspiracy-theory-about-missing-scientists-got-the-fbis-attention-d61de97c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wsj.com">[The Wall Street Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wsj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how a fringe conspiracy theory about missing scientists got the fbis attention d61de97c</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The case of Melissa Casias, a New Mexico administrative assistant gone missing, became a flashpoint, with internet theorists linking her...</span></span></span>

The strongest justified conclusion is therefore neither “the text proves murder” nor “the official ruling ends the matter”. It is that Eskridge's reported messages create a legitimate reason to seek and examine the underlying forensic records. Until those records are available or independently reviewed, the public evidence supports unresolved questions more strongly than it supports a demonstrated staged assassination.

## The evidential burden belongs with the staging claim

An official suicide determination should not be treated as sacred, especially where records are incomplete, investigative errors are documented or new physical evidence emerges. Medical-examiner decisions can be challenged and sometimes revised. But replacing one conclusion with a much more elaborate one — murder followed by deliberate staging and possibly institutional suppression — necessarily introduces additional claims that each require evidence.

For alleged researcher assassinations, that means distinguishing three levels that are often collapsed into one narrative:

1. **There are reasons to question the completeness of the public account.** Missing records, contradictory testimony or authenticated pre-death [warnings]({{ 'warnings/' | relative_url }}) can justify this conclusion.
2. **The suicide finding is forensically wrong.** This requires evidence about how the fatal injury actually occurred.
3. **The researcher was killed because of UFO, gravity or propulsion work.** This requires evidence identifying perpetrators or a chain of action connecting the death to that research.

Evidence sufficient for the first proposition is not automatically sufficient for the second, and evidence for the second does not establish the third.

That distinction substantially changes how the best-known cases should be read. McDonald's earlier suicide attempt and contemporaneous circumstances leave a staged-assassination interpretation with a heavy unresolved burden. Eskridge's pre-death warnings make her case less easily dismissed from public reporting alone, but missing primary forensic documentation prevents those warnings from proving homicide. In both cases, chronology and physical evidence are more discriminating than the researcher's controversial interests.

For readers evaluating claims that UFO or antigravity researchers were “suicided”, the most useful question is therefore not whether a death feels suspicious in retrospect. It is whether the assassination hypothesis explains **more of the contemporaneous evidence, with fewer unsupported assumptions, than the suicide finding it seeks to replace**.
