## Introduction

Cold fusion entered the story of allegedly suppressed [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers largely through one man: Eugene Mallove. Mallove was not primarily an [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researcher, and his murder in 2004 was ultimately prosecuted as a killing arising from circumstances around a rental property. What made him important to the broader narrative was his role as a prominent advocate for cold fusion and for other unconventional-energy research, combined with his long-running claim that scientific institutions had unfairly marginalised evidence for anomalous energy effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehour.com/news/article/Man-gets-16-years-in-prison-for-killing-scientist-8163651.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehour.com">[thehour.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehour.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Norwalk Hour Man gets 16 years in prison for killing scientist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Norwalk HourMan gets 16 years in prison for killing scientistJune 28, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 28, 2012</span></span></span>

## Mallove made suppression central to the cold-fusion story

The original cold-fusion controversy began in March 1989, when Fleischmann and Pons announced that electrochemical cells using palladium and heavy water had generated anomalous heat that they attributed to nuclear fusion. If correct, the implication would have been extraordinary: nuclear-scale energy from comparatively simple laboratory apparatus. But laboratories attempting rapid replication produced conflicting results, while expected nuclear signatures such as neutrons and tritium did not appear at levels consistent with the heat being claimed. A US Department of Energy panel concluded later that year that the evidence did not convincingly establish either useful energy production or a nuclear origin for the anomalous heat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/biblio/5144772" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cold fusion research (Technical Report) &#124; OSTI.GO V</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cold fusion research (Technical Report) &#124; OSTI.GOVNovember 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 1989</span></span></span>

Mallove's importance was that he interpreted the controversy very differently from most physicists. An MIT-trained engineer who was then chief science writer in the Massachusetts Institute of Technology news office, he became convinced that at least some cold-fusion results were genuine and that critics were prematurely shutting the subject down. His 1991 book *Fire from Ice* presented cold fusion not merely as an unresolved scientific anomaly but as a case in which institutional rivalry, funding interests and hostile scientific gatekeeping had obstructed recognition of a potentially revolutionary discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Fire_from_Ice.html?id=LkJwQgAACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books Fire from Ice: Searching for the Truth Behind the Cold Fusion Furor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books Fire from Ice: Searching for the Truth Behind the Cold Fusion Furor</span></span></span>

His dispute with MIT gave that argument a concrete institutional focus. Mallove objected to the handling of calorimetry results from an MIT Plasma Fusion Center replication attempt and alleged that presentation of the data understated a possible anomalous heat signal. Contemporary reporting recorded his accusations that researchers had manipulated or inadequately disclosed data; Ronald Parker, then director of the Plasma Fusion Center, rejected Mallove's allegations as unfounded. Mallove also objected to MIT's public treatment of cold fusion and to *Technology Review* declining an article he had prepared on the controversy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.deseret.com/1991/8/10/18935394/ex-mit-writer-accuses-researchers-of-altering-data-to-discredit-fusion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deseret.com">[Deseret News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deseret.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on deseret.com.</span></span></span>

This episode is important because the basic suppression narrative was already present more than a decade before Mallove's death. His argument was not originally that cold-fusion scientists were being assassinated. It was that mainstream institutions could suppress an unconventional field through control of prestige, publication, research funding and public interpretation. When he resigned from the MIT News Office in 1991, he described the institution's response to cold fusion as intolerant and obstructive; contemporary coverage also noted that he said he had not been personally pressured to resign because of his book.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.deseret.com/1991/6/9/18924935/mit-writer-will-resign-citing-unfair-criticism-of-fusion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deseret.com">[Deseret News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deseret.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">News MIT WRITER WILL RESIGN, CITING `UNFAIR&#x27; CRITICISM OF FUSION – Deseret News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deseret NewsMIT WRITER WILL RESIGN, CITING `UNFAIR&#x27; CRITICISM OF FUSION – Deseret News...</span></span></span>

Mallove subsequently helped build an alternative research infrastructure. He founded *Infinite Energy* magazine and the New Energy Foundation, promoted cold-fusion experiments and provided grants to researchers working on cold fusion and other unconventional-[energy claims]({{ 'energy-claims/' | relative_url }}). His own correspondence shows that by the early 2000s his interests had broadened beyond cold fusion into a much wider "new energy" world. That expansion is one reason his later image could be absorbed so easily into antigravity and UFO-adjacent narratives even though cold fusion itself concerns claimed nuclear or condensed-matter effects rather than gravity modification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eugenemallove.com/emwords_quest1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eugenemallove.com">[Eugene Mallove]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eugenemallove.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eugene Mallove Quest for Life and Knowledge 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eugene Mallove Quest for Life and Knowledge 1</span></span></span>

## Cold fusion is scientifically different from antigravity research

Cold fusion and antigravity are frequently grouped together in suppression literature because both can be presented as technologies that would overturn existing energy or aerospace systems. Scientifically, however, they are quite different categories.

Cold fusion — increasingly discussed by researchers under the broader label low-energy nuclear reactions, or LENR — grew out of specific electrochemical experiments involving deuterium and metals such as palladium. Its central experimental questions concern anomalous heat, reaction products, material loading and whether any observed effect has a nuclear origin. These are laboratory claims that can, at least in principle, be tested through calorimetry, particle detection, isotope measurements and reproducibility studies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ADOE_Report_of_the_Review_of_Low_Energy_Nuclear_Reactions.djvu/3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:DOE Report of the Review of Low Energy Nuclear Reactions.djvu/3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:DOE Report of the Review of Low Energy Nuclear Reactions.djvu/3</span></span></span>

Antigravity claims instead involve alleged manipulation, shielding or modification of gravitational effects, sometimes through superconductors, electromagnetic fields or more speculative mechanisms. UFO narratives add a further layer by linking such research to hypothetical propulsion systems supposedly associated with unidentified craft. None of those propositions follows from a cold-fusion result. A successful cold-fusion experiment could potentially affect energy generation without demonstrating anything about gravity control, inertial reduction or UFO propulsion.

The distinction is particularly important when assessing claims of motive. A theory that somebody wished to suppress a cheap energy source is not automatically evidence for suppression of breakthrough propulsion, and neither establishes a motive for murder. In conspiracy literature, however, the economic stakes are often treated as interchangeable: a technology said to threaten fossil fuels, centralised power generation, conventional nuclear fusion or aerospace monopolies can be placed within the same narrative even when the underlying physics and historical evidence are unrelated.

Cold fusion also has a much more visible record of formal institutional reassessment than a simple suppression story suggests. In 2004, at the request of researchers in the field, the Department of Energy convened another review. The reviewers remained divided over whether some experiments showed excess heat, but two-thirds of those addressing the nuclear-reaction evidence did not consider the case conclusive. The review nevertheless identified lines of investigation that could merit further work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/news/2004/041202/full/news041129-11.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">US review rekindles cold fusion debate: Nature News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">US review rekindles cold fusion debate: Nature News</span></span></span>

A major privately funded reassessment reached a similarly nuanced result in 2019. Google-backed researchers organised a multi-institution programme specifically because they thought cold fusion might have been dismissed too quickly. They reported no evidence of cold fusion, while also arguing that the work produced useful insights into highly hydrided metals and other underexplored physical regimes. *Nature* described the subject's post-1989 status as effectively taboo, but stressed that bringing it back under rigorous experimental examination was preferable to either uncritical belief or automatic rejection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-01683-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Google revives controversial cold-fusion experiments &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google revives controversial cold-fusion experiments &#124; Nature</span></span></span>

Government involvement has continued as well. In 2023, the US Advanced Research Projects Agency–Energy awarded $10 million across eight LENR projects at institutions including MIT, Stanford, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Texas Tech and the University of Michigan. ARPA-E framed the programme not as acceptance of cold fusion but as an attempt to obtain decisive, publishable evidence using modern diagnostics and hypothesis-driven experiments. As of 2026, several projects were still listed by ARPA-E as active.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arpa-e.energy.gov/news-and-events/news-and-insights/us-department-energy-announces-10-million-funding-projects-studying-low-energy-nuclear-reactions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arpa-e.energy.gov">[ARPA-E]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arpa-e.energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

That record does not prove that earlier cold-fusion researchers were always treated fairly. It does show why "suppressed science" requires careful definition. A field can experience ridicule, difficulty obtaining funding and barriers to publication while still remaining testable, periodically reviewed and even publicly funded. Those phenomena are materially different from evidence of a clandestine campaign to prevent research altogether.

## Mallove's murder transformed an institutional dispute into a death narrative

Mallove was killed on 14 May 2004 while cleaning a property in Norwich, Connecticut, that had recently been vacated by tenants. Because he was already one of the most outspoken proponents of supposedly suppressed energy technology, speculation that his research had provided a motive appeared quickly. Even early coverage from within the unconventional-energy community acknowledged such theories while reporting that investigators were pursuing a robbery or property-related explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sunjournal.com/2004/05/17/police-investigate-slaying/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sunjournal.com">[Lewiston Sun Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sunjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lewiston Sun Journal Police investigate slaying</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lewiston Sun Journal Police investigate slaying</span></span></span>

The later criminal record sharply weakens the idea that the murder was an attempt to silence cold fusion. Chad Schaffer, whose relatives had been evicted from Mallove's property, pleaded guilty to manslaughter and received a 16-year sentence in 2012. Mozelle Brown was later convicted of murder and conspiracy and sentenced to 58 years. Prosecutors and police tied the attack to the rental property and the eviction rather than Mallove's scientific advocacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehour.com/news/article/Man-gets-16-years-in-prison-for-killing-scientist-8163651.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehour.com">[Norwalk Hour]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehour.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Norwalk Hour Man gets 16 years in prison for killing scientist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Norwalk HourMan gets 16 years in prison for killing scientistJune 28, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 28, 2012</span></span></span>

A lengthy *Foreign Policy* reconstruction of the investigation shows why the case nevertheless remained fertile ground for speculation. The initial investigation was difficult, early suspects did not lead to a quick resolution, and Mallove's unusual professional world naturally prompted detectives and supporters to examine whether his work might have mattered. Yet the eventual evidential trail led back to people associated with the property. The extraordinary nature of Mallove's interests made an extraordinary motive imaginable; it did not make that motive evidentially stronger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://foreignpolicy.com/2016/07/07/the-coldest-case-cold-fusion-eugene-mallove-mit-infinite-energy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foreignpolicy.com">[Foreign Policy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foreignpolicy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Foreign Policy The Coldest Case – Foreign Policy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Foreign Policy The Coldest Case – Foreign Policy</span></span></span>

This is the key point for understanding his place in lists of allegedly suppressed scientists. Mallove's case combines three facts that are individually genuine: he publicly accused powerful scientific institutions of unfairly suppressing unconventional research; he became a prominent advocate for technologies that he believed could transform the energy system; and he was later murdered. Once those facts are placed side by side, it is easy to imply causation without demonstrating it.

The criminal case provides the missing test. Investigators had to establish who attacked Mallove and why, using witnesses, relationships, physical circumstances and other admissible evidence rather than the symbolic importance of his work. The resulting prosecutions supported a local, personal chain of events around the rental property. That does not logically prove that every possible research-related suspicion was impossible, but it leaves no comparable evidential foundation for the claim that cold fusion caused his death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehour.com/news/article/Man-gets-16-years-in-prison-for-killing-scientist-8163651.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehour.com">[Norwalk Hour]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehour.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Norwalk Hour Man gets 16 years in prison for killing scientist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Norwalk HourMan gets 16 years in prison for killing scientistJune 28, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 28, 2012</span></span></span>

## How cold fusion and UFO suppression narratives became merged

Mallove's inclusion broadens the alleged "UFO [researcher death]({{ 'missing-denominator/' | relative_url }})" story because his career provides a conceptual bridge from antigravity to a larger mythology of forbidden technology. Once the category becomes "scientists whose discoveries threatened established interests", the boundaries can expand almost indefinitely. Cold fusion researchers, alternative-energy inventors, gravity researchers and UFO investigators can all be grouped together even when they did not work on the same phenomena or belong to the same professional networks.

Several recurring ideas make this merger persuasive to believers. First is the **world-changing-technology motive**: if cold fusion promised extremely cheap energy, while antigravity promised revolutionary transport, existing industries would supposedly have enormous incentives to obstruct both. Second is **institutional continuity**: criticism by universities, journals or government panels can be interpreted as different expressions of the same suppressive system. Third is **retrospective reinterpretation of death**: once a researcher associated with unconventional science dies violently or unexpectedly, earlier professional conflicts can be recast as warning signs of physical danger.

Mallove's own rhetoric helped provide the first two elements. His criticisms of MIT and the scientific establishment supplied a documented history of conflict before his death. But the third element — turning those conflicts into an explanation for his murder — came principally from later interpretation rather than from the criminal evidence. That is precisely where a history of scientific controversy becomes a conspiracy narrative.

There is nevertheless a useful caution embedded in the story. Mainstream dismissal can become socially self-reinforcing. The Google-led cold-fusion project explicitly observed that repeated failures to reproduce the 1989 claims had effectively disqualified the subject from normal study, and *Nature* acknowledged the stigma surrounding it. Researchers may therefore have legitimate grounds to ask whether an unfashionable subject is receiving enough careful testing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Revisiting the cold case of cold fusion &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Revisiting the cold case of cold fusion &#124; NatureMay 27, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 27, 2019</span></span></span>

But the subsequent history also demonstrates the difference between stigma and secret suppression. Cold fusion has been reconsidered by the Department of Energy, investigated by a large privately funded collaboration, discussed in major scientific journals and funded again through ARPA-E. None of those programmes has so far supplied the decisive, reproducible demonstration that would vindicate the strongest cold-fusion claims, but neither does their existence fit a simple picture of authorities preventing the subject from being investigated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/news/2004/041202/full/news041129-11.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">US review rekindles cold fusion debate: Nature News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">US review rekindles cold fusion debate: Nature News</span></span></span>

For the broader UFO and antigravity death narrative, Mallove is therefore best understood as a boundary case rather than evidence of a common assassination pattern. His career genuinely documents how unconventional scientific claims can become entangled with arguments over reputation, funding, publication and institutional authority. His murder then gave those arguments a much darker symbolic meaning. The available evidence, however, supports keeping the two histories separate: one is a complicated scientific dispute over cold fusion and the treatment of marginal research; the other is a homicide for which the successful criminal cases pointed to a property-related conflict, not suppression of a revolutionary energy technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.middletownpress.com/news/article/Connecticut-man-gets-58-years-in-death-of-New-11785755.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: middletownpress.com">[CT Insider]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">middletownpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CT Insider Connecticut man gets 58 years in death of New Hampshire scientist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CT Insider Connecticut man gets 58 years in death of New Hampshire scientist</span></span></span>
