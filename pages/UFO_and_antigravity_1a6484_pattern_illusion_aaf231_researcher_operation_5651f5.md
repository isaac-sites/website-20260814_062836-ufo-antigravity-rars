## Introduction

Separate deaths or disappearances of people associated with UFOs, unconventional propulsion or gravity research do not become evidence of a coordinated suppression campaign simply because the people shared an unusual field. To link the cases, investigators would need **recurring operational signatures**: the same threatening actor, a repeated and distinctive method, common intermediaries, matching communications, shared financial or digital traces, or forensic evidence that independently connects one incident to another.

That distinction matters because a research label is thematic evidence, not linkage evidence. Crime-linkage research asks whether behaviour is both **consistent across incidents and distinctive enough to separate a genuine series from unrelated events**. Even then, behavioural similarity can generate false links and normally needs [corroboration]({{ 'corroboration/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0379073812002654" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Behavioural case linkage with solved and unsolved crimes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Behavioural case linkage with solved and unsolved crimes - ScienceDirectOctober 10, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 10, 2012</span></span></span>

Applied to claims about [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers, the useful question is therefore not “How many unusual researchers suffered tragedies?” It is: **what concrete feature recurs across otherwise independent cases that would be difficult to explain unless they shared a cause?**

## Thematic similarity is not operational linkage

A list of researchers can look remarkably coherent when its members are selected because they worked in aerospace, nuclear science, gravity modification, UFO investigation or other sensitive-looking fields. But that coherence is partly built into the selection rule. The same is true of labels such as “classified researcher” or “government scientist”: they tell us something about the people, but not necessarily anything about why they died.

Operational linkage works differently. In criminal investigation, the relevant comparison is between features of the events themselves. The US Office of Justice Programs describes linkage analysis as comparing the modus operandi — essentially how an offence was carried out — together with other distinctive behaviours to determine whether a recurring combination amounts to a meaningful signature. The process requires detailed, varied and multisource documentation rather than resemblance at headline level.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/linkage-analysis-modus-operandi-ritual-and-signature-serial-sexual" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

Research on behavioural case linkage makes the underlying test still clearer. Two conditions matter: **consistency**, meaning that an offender repeats relevant behaviour across incidents, and **distinctiveness**, meaning that the behaviour is unusual enough to distinguish those incidents from crimes committed by other people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.emerald.com/jcrpp/article/7/1/4/204686/Case-linkage-in-Australian-serial-stranger-rape" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: emerald.com">[Emerald Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">emerald.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emerald PublishingCase linkage in Australian serial stranger rape &#124; Journal of Criminological Research, Policy and Practice &#124; Emerald Pub...</span></span></span>

That distinction immediately exposes the weakness of a category such as “researcher connected with advanced technology”. If one person disappears while hiking, another is shot by a locally identified suspect, another dies from illness and another dies by suicide, the occupational similarity does not explain the sharply different event structures. A suppression hypothesis would have to account for those differences while supplying some independent evidence that nevertheless connects them.

The recent American “missing or dead scientists” controversy provides a useful real-world test. Reporting in 2026 found that the circulating set mixed researchers, laboratory personnel and others across different institutions and circumstances. The FBI and other authorities examined possible connections, but public reporting found no evidence establishing coordinated foul play.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/c046ce6d0a004e6a3e1971ff769244b5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">scientists gained traction, escalating from niche online forums to being addressed by the White House and U.S. Congress. ## What recurring signatures would actually matter?

No single type of evidence is mandatory. A [coordinated campaign]({{ 'coordination/' | relative_url }}) could theoretically use different methods. But the claim becomes substantially stronger only when multiple cases begin producing **independently discovered connections that were not created by the original decision to group the victims together**.

The most useful signatures would include:

* **Common threats.** Several researchers independently receiving messages from the same person, organisation, account, telephone number or communications infrastructure would be far more significant than each reporting vague professional [hostility]({{ 'hostility/' | relative_url }}). Similar wording could matter, but only if it were sufficiently distinctive and authenticated rather than a commonplace warning.
* **A recurring perpetrator or associate.** The same person appearing near multiple victims, contacting them shortly before their deaths, accessing their workplaces or being independently identified by witnesses would create a direct investigative bridge between cases.
* **Distinctive methods.** Repetition of an unusual method of attack, restraint, entry, surveillance, poisoning, scene manipulation or body disposal could justify comparison. A broad category such as “shot”, “missing” or “died unexpectedly” would normally be too common to carry much linkage weight on its own.
* **Shared intermediaries.** A recurring company, contractor, security employee, recruiter, financier, lawyer, intelligence contact or research collaborator becomes significant if records show that the intermediary actually interacted with several victims in circumstances relevant to the incidents.
* **Digital and communications traces.** Common email accounts, devices, IP infrastructure, telephone contacts, unusual login activity, malware or repeated approaches through the same online identity could turn an apparent thematic connection into a traceable operational one.
* **Forensic linkage.** DNA, fingerprints, ballistics, toolmarks or other physical traces connecting scenes would be considerably stronger than similarities inferred from biographies.
* **A repeated trigger event.** If several researchers were harmed shortly after independently reaching the same identifiable milestone — for example, contacting the same recipient with the same classified material — that sequence would deserve examination. Simply dying after having once researched an unconventional subject would not provide the same specificity.

The important word is **recurring**. One researcher receiving a threat before dying could make that individual case more concerning. Two people reporting different threats from unrelated sources still would not establish a common operation. But independently authenticated threats from the same source, followed by comparable acts against both recipients, would begin to supply the missing bridge.

This is also why investigators should compare combinations rather than hunt for one dramatic detail. Hazelwood and Warren's linkage framework treats a “signature” as a distinctive combination of behaviours, not merely any feature appearing twice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1359178902001064" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Linkage analysis: Modus operandi, ritual, and signature in serial sexual crime - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## The strongest links should survive case-by-case scrutiny

A proposed pattern should become clearer, not weaker, when each incident is investigated independently. That requirement is particularly important in [researcher-death]({{ 'missing-denominator/' | relative_url }}) narratives because solved or partly solved cases can provide negative tests of the proposed connection.

The murder of [cold-fusion]({{ 'cold-fusion/' | relative_url }}) advocate Eugene Mallove is instructive. Because Mallove promoted controversial alternative-energy research, his 2004 killing could superficially fit a suppression narrative. Yet the criminal investigation developed a much more specific chain of evidence. Connecticut records show that Mallove was killed at a rental property from which tenants had recently been evicted. Mozelle Brown was later convicted of murder and conspiracy; contemporary reporting also described Chad Schaffer, a relative of the evicted tenants, as having pleaded guilty to manslaughter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jud.ct.gov/external/supapp/Cases/AROap/AP230/AP230.93.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jud.ct.gov">[Connecticut Judicial Branch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jud.ct.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Connecticut Judicial Branch************************************************</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Connecticut Judicial Branch************************************************</span></span></span>

That does not make Mallove's scientific work irrelevant to every conceivable inquiry. It means that anyone proposing to link his death to other researcher killings must explain why the established participants and circumstances should be understood as part of the alleged wider operation. Merely adding Mallove to a list because cold fusion was controversial discards rather than incorporates the strongest case-specific evidence.

John Mack provides an even sharper example. The Harvard psychiatrist became famous for taking reported alien-abduction experiences seriously. He died in London in 2004 after being struck by a car. Harvard's memorial account states that the vehicle was being driven by a man under the influence of alcohol.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2005/12/john-e-mack/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.harvard.edu">[Harvard Gazette]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gazette John E. Mack — Harvard Gazette</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gazette John E. Mack — Harvard Gazette</span></span></span> If Mack is placed alongside murdered or missing researchers, therefore, the proposed linkage has to explain what operational evidence connects that documented road death with those fundamentally different events.

The principle works in the opposite direction as well. A genuinely unexplained disappearance should not be dismissed merely because some other cases on the same internet list have ordinary explanations. It should remain unresolved on its own evidence. But “unresolved” is not itself a common signature: several unrelated cases can remain unsolved for entirely different reasons.

## The 2026 cases show what cross-case comparison looks like

The 2026 US controversy is particularly revealing because authorities actually began doing what a serious linkage inquiry requires: comparing case facts across jurisdictions rather than relying on occupational resemblance.

Consider Caltech astrophysicist Carl Grillmair and JPL materials scientist Monica Reza. Grillmair was shot outside his home, and authorities arrested and charged Freddy Snyder with his murder. Reza disappeared during a hike in the San Gabriel Mountains. The Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department told the Los Angeles Times that investigators had spoken with other agencies and compared facts but had found no connection between Reza's disappearance and similar cases at that point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.latimes.com/politics/story/2026-04-21/fbi-probes-missing-or-dead-scientists-including-four-from-la-area" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: latimes.com">[Los Angeles Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">latimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on latimes.com.</span></span></span>

That comparison illustrates the evidential threshold better than either scepticism or suspicion alone. Investigators did not have to assume that a connection was impossible. They could compare locations, timelines, contacts, suspects and circumstances. If the same person, vehicle, communication account or distinctive method had emerged in both files, the hypothesis would have strengthened. According to the publicly reported findings, it did not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.latimes.com/politics/story/2026-04-21/fbi-probes-missing-or-dead-scientists-including-four-from-la-area" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: latimes.com">[Los Angeles Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">latimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on latimes.com.</span></span></span>

The murder of MIT physicist Nuno Loureiro supplies another useful control. MIT reported in December 2025 that authorities had identified the person responsible for Loureiro's killing and linked that individual to the Brown University mass shooting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.mit.edu/2025/statement-professor-nuno-loureiro" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.mit.edu">[MIT News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.mit.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mit.edu.</span></span></span> For a wider scientist-suppression theory to incorporate Loureiro credibly, it would therefore need evidence connecting that identified offender to the supposed campaign. His status as a physicist cannot perform that evidential work.

This is the central advantage of case-level analysis: **identified perpetrators, documented circumstances and forensic findings constrain the story**. A theory that can absorb every outcome — murder by an identified suspect, accidental death, suicide, unexplained disappearance and natural death — without requiring a common mechanism becomes extremely difficult to test.

## Similar methods are useful only when they are distinctive

Even genuine repetition can mislead. A method can recur simply because it is common.

Crime-linkage studies therefore do not ask only whether two offences resemble each other. They ask whether their similarities discriminate linked cases from the much larger population of unlinked ones. Research using solved and unsolved offences explicitly warns that both false-positive and false-negative linkages should be expected when behavioural evidence is used. Offenders can change their behaviour, while unrelated offenders can independently do similar things.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0379073812002654" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Behavioural case linkage with solved and unsolved crimes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Behavioural case linkage with solved and unsolved crimes - ScienceDirectOctober 10, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 10, 2012</span></span></span>

The same logic should govern researcher-death claims. “Both disappeared outdoors” is weaker than “both were approached beforehand by the same unidentified vehicle”. “Both were shot” is weaker than matching ballistics. “Both expressed fear” is weaker than independently preserved messages identifying the same person and the same threatened consequence. “Both worked on propulsion” is weaker still, because that characteristic describes the selected population rather than the incident.

Geography and timing can contribute, but again they need context. Empirical linkage research has found that spatial distance and temporal proximity can help distinguish some linked from unlinked crimes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/the-feasibility-of-using-crime-scene-behaviour-to-detect-versatil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.birmingham.ac.uk">[University of Birmingham]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.birmingham.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on birmingham.ac.uk.</span></span></span> Yet researchers living near the same aerospace hub or working for connected institutions already have reasons to occupy the same geographic network. Closeness is therefore more informative when it is unexpected given the background population.

The practical rule is simple: **the rarer and more independently verifiable the shared feature, the more evidential weight it can carry**.

## Threats need corroboration, not just repetition in retellings

Claims of warnings before death occupy a special place in UFO and antigravity narratives because they appear predictive: if somebody said they feared being killed and later died, the earlier statement can look like powerful evidence.

Amy Eskridge is a prominent example. Her obituary confirms that she co-founded Huntsville's Institute for Exotic Science and had an interdisciplinary scientific background; her work subsequently became part of speculation surrounding unconventional gravity research and her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obits.al.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: al.com">[Alabama Obituaries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">al.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">amy eskridge obituary</span></span></span> Online accounts have circulated much stronger allegations about intimidation and warnings, but those claims vary considerably in provenance and often depend on later retellings rather than publicly available investigative records.

For linkage purposes, the evidential question is narrower than whether a warning sounds disturbing. Investigators would want to establish when it was made, whether an original record exists, who received it, whether the alleged threatening party can be identified and whether equivalent evidence appears in another case.

Suppose, for example, that three researchers each left dated messages naming the same aerospace contractor, and independent telephone records showed contacts from the same numbers shortly before each death. That would constitute a repeated, externally checkable signature. By contrast, three people separately saying that powerful interests disliked their work would have much less discriminatory value. Researchers in contentious fields can reasonably anticipate criticism, professional conflict or secrecy restrictions without those experiences arising from a single hostile actor.

The distinction is between **corroborated convergence** and **retrospective resemblance**.

## Shared institutions can produce innocent connections too

A recurring employer, laboratory or defence contractor can be important, but it is not automatically suspicious. Large scientific institutions generate dense networks of colleagues, suppliers, security systems, conferences and research programmes. Two people sharing an institutional connection is therefore more likely than it would be among randomly selected members of the public.

This creates a base-rate problem for suppression claims. If thousands of people work in aerospace and national-laboratory environments, one should expect some deaths, accidents, crimes and disappearances among them without any common hostile cause. AP's reporting on the 2026 controversy noted that the circulating group included people with quite different roles and circumstances and that no definitive evidence had established coordinated foul play.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/c046ce6d0a004e6a3e1971ff769244b5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">scientists gained traction, escalating from niche online forums to being addressed by the White House and U.S. Congress. Institutional overlap becomes much more informative when it narrows to something operational: the same small project team, the same restricted compartment, the same security investigation, the same disputed technology transfer, the same external visitor or the same sequence of unauthorised access events. At that level, records could establish who knew whom, who had access to what, and whether the supposed connection existed before the tragedies rather than being constructed afterwards.

A useful test is therefore to ask: **Would investigators have noticed this connection without knowing that the victims were interested in UFOs or unconventional propulsion?** If the answer is yes — because, for example, their devices were contacted by the same account — the evidence is potentially independent. If the answer is no, because the only reason for comparing them is their research theme, the linkage remains largely circular.

## Independent signatures matter more than a perfect-looking story

There is a genuine danger at both extremes. Investigators can falsely connect unrelated events, but they can also fail to connect crimes committed by the same offender. Criminal-justice literature has long called the latter problem “linkage blindness”: relevant information can remain divided among jurisdictions, preventing investigators from recognising a serial pattern. The US Office of Justice Programs has specifically highlighted information sharing and comparison of modus operandi as tools for overcoming that problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/working-definition-serial-murder-and-reduction-linkage-blindness" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span> The UK's National Crime Agency likewise lists offence-linkage analysis among the specialist services provided by behavioural investigative advisers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalcrimeagency.gov.uk/what-we-do/how-we-work/providing-specialist-capabilities-for-law-enforcement/major-crime-investigative-support?highlight=WyJtaXNzaW5nIiwicGVyc29uIiwiYW5kIl0%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalcrimeagency.gov.uk">[National Crime Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalcrimeagency.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Crime Agency Major crime investigative support</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Crime Agency Major crime investigative support</span></span></span>

So the answer is not to assume that separate researcher deaths must be unrelated. It is to make the linkage hypothesis earn its status through evidence.

A convincing pattern would ideally emerge from records that were created **before anyone assembled a “mysterious researchers” list**: police reports, authenticated messages, access logs, financial transactions, forensic results, travel records, witness accounts and communications metadata. Independent investigators examining separate files should then discover the same actors or distinctive behaviours without needing the UFO or antigravity label to tell them what to look for.

That independence is crucial because behavioural linkage itself is imperfect. A review of crime-linkage practice reported incorrect links as well as cases in which suspected links could not be confirmed, illustrating why behavioural resemblance should guide investigation rather than substitute for corroborating evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1002/jip.1531" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The practice of crime linkage: A review of the literature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The practice of crime linkage: A review of the literature - Davies - 2019 - Journal of Investigative Psychology and Offender Profiling...</span></span></span>

## What would change the assessment?

The evidential picture surrounding alleged suppression of UFO or antigravity researchers would change substantially if several cases produced a recurring connection such as authenticated threats from one source, the same unexplained intermediary, matching forensic traces, a common perpetrator, or a distinctive operational sequence surrounding access to the same information.

Conversely, the coordinated-cause hypothesis weakens when detailed investigation produces different perpetrators, unrelated personal circumstances, ordinary medical explanations, accidents or disappearances with no cross-case contacts. The 2026 scientist controversy currently illustrates this fragmented picture: federal scrutiny was prompted by the accumulation of cases, but public reporting found no established coordinated connection, while some individual incidents already had identifiable suspects or circumstances pointing in different directions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/c046ce6d0a004e6a3e1971ff769244b5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[apnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">scientists gained traction, escalating from niche online forums to being addressed by the White House and U.S. Congress. Theories propose...</span></span></span>

This does not require every mystery to be solved before a pattern can be considered. It requires the proposed pattern to contain something more specific than the category used to create it. **Shared research interests explain why cases are placed on the same list; recurring operational signatures are what could show that they belong to the same investigation.**

Until those signatures appear and survive independent verification, a collection of tragedies involving UFO, aerospace or antigravity researchers remains evidence that tragedies occurred within an unusual professional community — not, by itself, evidence that a common actor caused them.
