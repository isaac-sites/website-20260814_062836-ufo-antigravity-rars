## Introduction

Harry Reid’s 2009 attempt to place a Pentagon-funded advanced-aerospace effort under **Restricted Special Access Program (SAP)** protection is important precisely because it failed. The then Senate Majority Leader asked Deputy Secretary of Defense William Lynn III to give unusually tight security protection to work formally managed by the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) as the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (AAWSAP), although Reid’s letter called it the Advanced Aerospace Threat and Identification Program (AATIP).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/161883/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: A government-funded aerospace research effort existed.">[Defense Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">A government-funded aerospace research effort existed.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dia.mil.</span></span></span>

## Why Reid wanted Special Access Program status

A Special Access Program is not simply another synonym for something being classified. Under the classification framework established by Executive Order 13526, a SAP imposes access and safeguarding requirements beyond those normally applied to information at the same classification level. Such programmes are supposed to be kept to an absolute minimum and created only where the information faces exceptional vulnerability and ordinary access controls are inadequate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/realitycheck/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security Information &#124; The White HouseDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

That made Reid’s request a consequential governance intervention. In his 24 June 2009 letter to Lynn, he argued that the aerospace study had made substantial progress and had identified highly sensitive and unconventional aerospace-related findings. He anticipated work touching areas such as advanced lift, propulsion and materials, and argued that the programme’s methods, personnel and relationships with industry required greater protection. He therefore sought a **Restricted SAP**, with access confined to a tightly controlled list of people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufoscans.com/20090624-reid-to-depsecdef-ref-aaitp-in-sap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Reid believed stronger protection was warranted.">[UFOScans.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Reid believed stronger protection was warranted.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufoscans.com.</span></span></span>

The distinction between Reid’s terminology and the programme’s contractual identity is also significant. Pentagon records explicitly state that the activity Reid called AATIP in his request was officially the DIA-managed **AAWSAP contract**. The later public debate has often treated AAWSAP and AATIP as separate or overlapping programmes in different ways, but for this particular policy decision the government paperwork is unusually direct: officials evaluating Reid’s request understood which DIA contract he wanted protected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/161883/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: A government-funded aerospace research effort existed.">[Defense Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">A government-funded aerospace research effort existed.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dia.mil.</span></span></span>

Reid was therefore asking the Department of Defense to do more than keep an already sensitive activity quiet. He wanted the department to establish an exceptional access regime around it. Under the rules governing SAPs, that required the department to find a defensible reason why ordinary classification and access controls were inadequate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/realitycheck/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security Information &#124; The White HouseDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

## Why defence officials declined the request

The most revealing document is DIA’s 13 November 2009 **Review of Special Access Program Request**. It records that DIA was asked to evaluate Reid’s proposal and states its conclusion at the outset: after examining deliverables produced so far and anticipated FY2010 work, the agency could not find adequate justification for establishing a restricted SAP.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/dia/AAWSAP/U-09-2660CE-IM-Review_of_Special_Access_Program_Request.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault UNCLASSIFIEDIJFtils Ti</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black Vault UNCLASSIFIEDIJFtils Ti</span></span></span>

The reasoning matters more than the simple fact of rejection. DIA found that the programme’s first-year documents were unclassified. Its review acknowledged that the research concerned unconventional aerospace technologies, but it said DIA was unaware of any report containing information sufficiently sensitive and vulnerable to warrant the additional protection of an alternative security measure or restricted SAP.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170058/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.mil">[Defense Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Intelligence Agency UNCLASSIFIED/SMS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Intelligence Agency UNCLASSIFIED/SMS</span></span></span>

The review also confronted a basic classification problem. Information cannot properly be classified merely because its subject sounds futuristic or strategically interesting. DIA noted that much of the material consisted of basic scientific research and publicly available information; under the applicable classification rules, basic scientific research could not simply be classified unless the necessary national-security criteria were satisfied. DIA concluded that this requirement had not been met.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170058/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.mil">[Defense Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Intelligence Agency UNCLASSIFIED/SMS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Intelligence Agency UNCLASSIFIED/SMS</span></span></span>

That creates a striking contrast between the two sides of the paper trail. Reid’s letter spoke prospectively about findings and technological advances that he believed would require extraordinary protection. DIA assessed what it actually had before it and found no material warranting such treatment. The agency even interpreted Reid’s strongest statements as apparently referring to **future phases** of the programme and associated security or counter-intelligence concerns, rather than to existing reports already demonstrating a breakthrough requiring SAP protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170058/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.mil">[Defense Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Intelligence Agency UNCLASSIFIED/SMS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Intelligence Agency UNCLASSIFIED/SMS</span></span></span>

This is an important constraint on interpretations of the episode. The rejection does not prove that every unusual claim associated with AAWSAP was false. Nor does it prove that no classified UFO-related information existed anywhere else in government. It establishes something narrower and firmer: when the responsible defence bureaucracy reviewed this particular request for exceptional secrecy, the material submitted through the programme did not meet the threshold officials considered necessary for a restricted SAP.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/dia/AAWSAP/U-09-2660CE-IM-Review_of_Special_Access_Program_Request.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault UNCLASSIFIEDIJFtils Ti</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black Vault UNCLASSIFIEDIJFtils Ti</span></span></span>

## The failed request versus a hidden operational programme

This distinction is easy to lose because a document requesting extraordinary secrecy can itself look like evidence of extraordinary secrets. Reid was a senior political figure; the request went to the Deputy Secretary of Defense; the programme concerned unconventional aerospace technologies; and the proposed access arrangements were unusually restrictive. All of those points are real.

Yet the documentary sequence changes their meaning:



<div class="youtube-embed-container youtube-embed-fallback"><div class="youtube-embed-card"><div class="youtube-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/-IhgiRI7-cg" title="Harry Reid UFO Program, parts 1-3 KLAS, Nov. 2017" loading="lazy" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><div class="youtube-embed-footer"><p class="youtube-embed-title"><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-IhgiRI7-cg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Harry Reid UFO Program, parts 1-3 KLAS, Nov. 2017</a></p><p class="youtube-embed-meta">Channel: Matthew Adams</p><p class="youtube-embed-actions"><a class="youtube-embed-watch-link" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-IhgiRI7-cg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" title="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-IhgiRI7-cg">Open on YouTube</a></p></div></div></div>

1. **A government-funded aerospace research effort existed.** DIA records identify AAWSAP and preserve its contracts, technical reports and administrative documents in the agency’s Freedom of Information Act reading room. [Defense Intelligence Agency](https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/161883/)

2. **Reid believed stronger protection was warranted.** His June 2009 letter sought restricted SAP status for the programme he called AATIP. [UFOScans.com](https://www.ufoscans.com/20090624-reid-to-depsecdef-ref-aaitp-in-sap/)

3. **The request triggered scrutiny rather than automatic acceptance.** DIA reviewed the deliverables against the criteria for additional protection. [The Black Vault](https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/dia/AAWSAP/U-09-2660CE-IM-Review_of_Special_Access_Program_Request.pdf)

4. **The reviewers found the justification insufficient.** They did not identify programme reports whose sensitivity and vulnerability justified the proposed restricted SAP. [Defense Intelligence Agency](https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170058/)

5. **The requested compartment therefore was not established.** The surviving record documents a proposed security architecture that failed to gain approval, not an operational SAP secretly created through Reid’s letter. [The Black Vault](https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/the-advanced-aerospace-weapon-system-applications-program-aawsap-documentation/)

That last point is especially important for narratives involving alleged antigravity or recovered-UFO technology. The fact that Reid sought a protected compartment is evidence that influential people believed the subject merited exceptional treatment. It is not equivalent to evidence that the technologies Reid hoped to protect had been demonstrated, acquired or reverse-engineered.

## How the failed bid became evidence in later UFO claims

The episode nevertheless acquired greater significance after the Pentagon’s UFO-related activities became publicly controversial. Reporting beginning in 2017 established Reid’s role in securing support for the advanced-aerospace effort, while subsequently released documents exposed the SAP request itself. Reid remained openly supportive of investigating UFO reports and defended having initiated the work. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2017/dec/17/pentagon-admits-running-secret-ufo-investigation-for-five-years)

Later comments from Reid help explain why the SAP request became so suggestive. He said that he had heard claims for years that aerospace contractor Lockheed Martin possessed retrieved material and that he had unsuccessfully sought Pentagon approval that would have allowed him to investigate further. Reid did not claim that he had personally seen such material. The distinction between **what Reid had been told** and **what he had verified** is therefore essential. [Esquire](https://www.esquire.com/news-politics/politics/a36320637/pentagon-confirms-ufo-program/)

The SAP episode can consequently support two very different narratives. In one, Reid’s extraordinary request is treated as circumstantial evidence that senior insiders knew a deeply concealed UFO programme existed. In the other, it demonstrates how extraordinary claims can circulate among credible officials and researchers and motivate genuine government action even when the subsequent institutional review does not validate the underlying premise.

The documents provide stronger support for the second, more limited proposition. Reid unquestionably tried to build a highly restricted security structure. What the records do **not** establish is that the Department of Defense recognised a hidden cache of recovered UFO technology and then placed it inside the compartment Reid requested. DIA’s recorded judgement was instead that the programme material it reviewed did not justify that additional secrecy. [The Black Vault](https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/dia/AAWSAP/U-09-2660CE-IM-Review_of_Special_Access_Program_Request.pdf)

## The later KONA BLUE attempt sharpens the distinction

A later episode reinforces why proposed secret programmes must be distinguished from programmes that actually became operational. After AAWSAP, supporters pursued another concept under the Department of Homeland Security that became known as **[KONA BLUE]({{ 'kona-blue/' | relative_url }})**. According to the Pentagon’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), the proposal envisaged a SAP structure capable of receiving alleged UAP technology and supporting investigations and possible reverse-engineering work. Reid and Senator Joseph Lieberman supported the prospective programme. [UFO Transparency](https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-2020s-aaro-historical-record-report-vol1-2024-aaro-historical-record-report-volume-1-2024)

But KONA BLUE was also rejected. AARO reports that a Prospective Special Access Program was formally requested but DHS leadership ultimately declined it for lack of merit. The proposed programme therefore provides another example of bureaucratic paperwork describing extraordinary intended capabilities without demonstrating that those capabilities, materials or activities actually existed operationally. [UFO Transparency](https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-2020s-aaro-historical-record-report-vol1-2024-aaro-historical-record-report-volume-1-2024)

That later history makes Reid’s 2009 bid easier to interpret. Government archives can preserve detailed proposals for compartments that officials hoped would acquire or investigate extraordinary technology. Those proposals are historically valuable because they document what influential participants believed, sought and expected. They cannot safely be read backwards as proof that the proposed secret programme was already functioning.

## What Reid's secret-program request actually shows

Harry Reid’s failed SAP bid is among the stronger documentary episodes in the modern history of US government UFO interest because there is little doubt about the central sequence. A senior senator requested extraordinary protection for a real DIA-funded programme; defence officials reviewed that request; and the available government documentation records their conclusion that the programme’s material did not justify restricted SAP status. [Defense Intelligence Agency](https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/161883/)

For claims about UFOs, antigravity research and extreme government secrecy, that makes the episode valuable in a less sensational but more concrete way. It shows that **secrecy itself was contested inside government**. Reid and programme supporters wanted a much narrower circle of access because they believed unconventional aerospace research could become exceptionally sensitive. The officials responsible for evaluating that proposition did not agree that the existing evidence crossed the necessary threshold. [Defense Intelligence Agency](https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170058/)

The resulting paper trail therefore documents belief, advocacy and bureaucratic resistance simultaneously. It supports the claim that serious officials sought unusually deep protection for UFO-adjacent aerospace research. It does not establish that the requested compartment concealed operational antigravity technology, recovered extraterrestrial vehicles or researchers whose work had to disappear from public view. In the broader history of alleged secret unconventional-propulsion programmes, the Reid episode is most useful as a warning against confusing **a request to create secrecy with evidence of the secret programme the requester hoped to create**.
