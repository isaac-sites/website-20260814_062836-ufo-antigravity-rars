## Introduction

Amy Eskridge unquestionably worked in the unusual world of gravity modification and unconventional propulsion. In 2018 she publicly represented the Institute for Exotic Science and HoloChron as organisations interested in gravity modification, superconductors and related advanced technologies. Her surviving presentation discussed proposed gravity-shielding experiments, reactionless propulsion and the recurring movement of promising research into classified programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/program-2018-12.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">program 2018 12.shtml</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 December 2018 Program...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2018</span></span></span>

What the public record does **not** establish is equally important. No accessible peer-reviewed paper, independently replicated experiment, patent or documented prototype shows that Eskridge herself had achieved controllable [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) or a propulsion breakthrough before her death. That missing technological link matters because the later claim that a private aerospace company murdered her to suppress or acquire transformative technology assumes that she possessed something extraordinarily valuable. The evidence establishes serious research ambitions and intriguing unpublished claims; it does not establish the breakthrough that would make such a motive self-evident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://factually.co/fact-checks/science/institute-for-exotic-science-antigravity-propulsion-evidence-907c38" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: factually.co">[Factually]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">factually.co</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Verifiable Evidence Exists for the Institute for...April 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

## What Eskridge actually presented about gravity modification

The strongest primary evidence for Eskridge's technical interests is her December 2018 talk to the Huntsville Alabama L5 Society, entitled *A Historical Perspective on Anti-Gravity Technology*. HAL5 introduced her as president and co-founder of the Institute for Exotic Science and chief executive of HoloChron Engineering. Its contemporary biography says she had a chemistry degree from the University of Alabama in Huntsville, was enrolled in its materials-science PhD programme and had experience ranging from microelectromechanical systems to polymeric drug delivery. Her later obituary says she graduated from UAH with a double major in chemistry and biology and was chairwoman and president of the Institute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/program-2018-12.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">program 2018 12.shtml</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 December 2018 Program...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2018</span></span></span>

The slides give a clearer picture than the much stronger descriptions of her work that appeared after her death. The Institute was described as specialising in quantum computing, gravity modification, metamaterial science and communications, while HoloChron was described as a Huntsville father-and-daughter company with expertise in gravity-modification research and development. Richard Eskridge, Amy's father and HoloChron's co-founder and chief technology officer, was identified as a retired NASA engineer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span>

Yet the actual 35-page presentation was principally a **survey of claimed antigravity research**, not a disclosure of a finished Eskridge device. It moved through historical and modern proposals including the Dean Drive, Eric Laithwaite's gyroscope claims, Jim Woodward's Mach-effect work, Ning Li's superconducting research, Eugene [Podkletnov]({{ 'podkletnov/' | relative_url }})'s claimed gravitational shielding and Claude Poher's superconducting-discharge experiments. Some entries were treated sceptically: the slides called the Wallace Machine's claimed effect "unsubstantiated", noted that ordinary mechanics explained Laithwaite's apparent weight changes and acknowledged that Podkletnov's claimed effect had been widely criticised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span>

That mixture is significant. Eskridge was not simply telling an audience that every antigravity claim was genuine. She was mapping a controversial research field and arguing that certain anomalous results deserved more systematic investigation.

### Superconductors were a particularly important thread

One of the recurring mechanisms in the presentation involved yttrium barium copper oxide, or YBCO, a high-temperature superconducting ceramic. Podkletnov had claimed that a rotating YBCO superconductor produced a small reduction in the apparent weight of objects above it. Eskridge's slides recorded the claimed two-per-cent reduction but also explicitly noted the scientific criticism surrounding it. They then discussed Poher's proposal that an electrical discharge through a YBCO disc generated a propulsive force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span>

This helps define what "antigravity" meant in Eskridge's public work. It did not necessarily mean a science-fiction vehicle cancelling Earth's gravity at will. Her presentation encompassed a broader family of proposed effects in which superconductors, electromagnetic fields, electrical discharges or changing inertial properties might produce anomalous forces that could eventually have propulsion applications.

Eskridge was also interested in why supposedly promising programmes seemed to disappear from the open literature. Her closing slide stated that research needed testable hypotheses and independent private funding, then asked how to prevent promising results from disappearing into the classified realm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span> This concern later became central to interpretations of her death, but in 2018 it appeared as part of her argument for doing unconventional propulsion research openly.

## The unpublished 2020 work is the crucial technology gap

The most consequential claim about Eskridge's own research appeared later. Material preserved from a 2020 discussion attributes to her a statement that the Institute had further developed "novel foundational work" originally produced by one of its team members while that person was a civil servant at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center. According to the statement, NASA's intellectual-property release procedure had to clear the foundational work before the Institute could publicly discuss its subsequent results. Eskridge said a paper was undergoing NASA review and expected publication soon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.ph/O3Pgg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.ph">[Archive.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AMY ESKRIDGE: The DEATH of a promising ANTI-GRAVITY researcher. Conducting anti-gravity research? You probably work in Huntsville, cease...</span></span></span>

This is potentially more important than the 2018 historical presentation because it suggests the Institute believed it possessed **new theory and supporting data**, rather than merely an interest in reproducing old antigravity experiments. The difficulty is provenance. The surviving quotation is preserved mainly through archived social-media and enthusiast material rather than an accessible NASA publication or Institute paper, and searches of the public record have not produced the promised paper. Later summaries likewise report that no independently verified experimental results from the Institute have surfaced publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://factually.co/fact-checks/science/institute-for-exotic-science-antigravity-propulsion-evidence-907c38" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: factually.co">[Factually]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">factually.co</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Verifiable Evidence Exists for the Institute for...April 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

The reasonable inference is therefore narrower than either extreme. The absence of a public paper does not prove there was no underlying research: Eskridge's reported statement specifically describes work awaiting release, and classified or intellectual-property restrictions can prevent publication for ordinary reasons. But the missing publication also cannot legitimately be treated as proof that NASA suppressed a successful antigravity system. Those are different propositions.

Most importantly for the murder theory, the public record lacks the kinds of artefacts that would allow outsiders to judge how advanced this work really was:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* no accessible technical paper presenting Eskridge's claimed theory and supporting data;
* no independently witnessed demonstration of an Eskridge or Institute device producing anomalous thrust or measurable gravity modification;
* no publicly documented replication by an independent laboratory;
* no disclosed performance measurements showing thrust, efficiency, weight reduction or another anomalous effect beyond experimental error;
* no public evidence demonstrating that a prototype could be scaled into a practical aerospace propulsion system.

</div>

This distinction is easy to lose because "worked on antigravity" and "possessed working antigravity technology" sound similar in compressed retellings. They are scientifically worlds apart. The former is well documented for Eskridge; the latter is not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/program-2018-12.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">program 2018 12.shtml</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 December 2018 Program...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2018</span></span></span>

## Her own presentation illustrates why replication matters

Eskridge's 2018 slides inadvertently provide a useful standard for evaluating later claims about her. Several of the historical cases she discussed involved precisely the problem now surrounding her own legacy: an intriguing result was claimed, other researchers struggled to reproduce it, documentation remained incomplete, and secrecy or institutional suppression was then proposed as an explanation for the missing evidence.

Podkletnov is a good example. Eskridge recorded his claim of gravitational shielding from rotating YBCO and mentioned attempted near-replications, but also acknowledged the widespread criticism of the effect. She similarly presented Morningstar Applied Physics as claiming a seven-per-cent weight reduction and described Boeing Phantom Works' reported interest in gravity research, before moving into much more speculative territory concerning alleged triangular craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span>

Her final prescription was scientifically important: the field needed "theories with a testable hypothesis".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span> Applied to Eskridge's own case, that means later accounts cannot substitute the existence of secrecy, threats or unpublished research for experimentally demonstrated performance. A breakthrough capable of transforming aerospace engineering would require exceptionally strong evidence precisely because the claimed effect would overturn or radically extend established propulsion physics.

That evidence might conceivably have existed privately. The public record simply does not currently contain it.

## How a research motive became attached to her death

The strongest version of the murder allegation comes not from the 2018 technical material but from retired British military figure Franc Milburn. Journalist Michael Shellenberger subsequently included the allegation in written material submitted to the US House Committee on Oversight and Accountability in November 2024. The congressional document records Milburn's claim that Eskridge was targeted with directed-energy weapons and murdered by a private aerospace company because of her involvement with UAP discussions and advanced propulsion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Written-Testimony-Shellenberger.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oversight Committee</span></span></span>

The status of that document needs careful handling. Its appearance in material submitted to Congress establishes that **the allegation was formally presented to Congress**. It does not mean Congress, the House Oversight Committee, NASA or another federal authority verified the allegation. The relevant entry itself describes it as Milburn's claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Written-Testimony-Shellenberger.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oversight Committee</span></span></span>

Recent reporting has repeated Milburn's account, including allegations that Eskridge experienced harassment and believed she was being attacked with directed electromagnetic energy. Those allegations may be relevant to the broader unresolved circumstances of her death, but they do not independently demonstrate a propulsion-related motive. The existence of directed-energy weapons in military arsenals does not establish that one was used against Eskridge, still less who might have used one or why.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nypost.com/2026/05/06/tech/pentagon-confirms-existence-of-energy-weapons-allegedly-used-against-ufo-researcher-amy-eskridge/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nypost.com">[New York Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nypost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DEWs utilize focused electromagnetic energy or subatomic particles to disable electronic systems or incapacitate targets. The disclosure...</span></span></span>

For the narrower question considered here, the alleged motive has two related versions. One is **suppression**: Eskridge supposedly possessed knowledge that threatened an existing classified programme and had to be prevented from releasing it. The other is **commercial or strategic competition**: a defence or aerospace interest supposedly regarded her independently developed technology as sufficiently disruptive to justify eliminating her.

Both hypotheses require considerably more than proof that she studied antigravity. They require evidence that somebody believed she possessed information or hardware important enough to justify an extraordinary criminal act.

## The public record does not establish technology worth killing for

There are facts that make the [motive claim]({{ 'motive-claim/' | relative_url }}) superficially plausible enough to investigate rather than dismiss out of hand. Eskridge operated in Huntsville, an important American aerospace and defence centre; her father had worked at NASA; her organisations explicitly pursued gravity modification; she publicly complained about promising work disappearing into classified programmes; and the reported 2020 statement suggests her Institute believed it had further developed NASA-originated foundational research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/program-2018-12.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">program 2018 12.shtml</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 December 2018 Program...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2018</span></span></span>

But these facts establish **access, interest and ambition**, not a breakthrough.

The most revealing comparison is between what survives from 2018 and what is missing from 2020–22. The 2018 presentation is remarkably accessible: it names researchers, mechanisms, historical experiments and claimed numerical effects. Yet when the story reaches the Institute's supposedly more important original work, the evidential trail becomes much thinner. There is a statement about theory and substantiating data, a reference to NASA review and subsequent claims that the work was strategically significant — but no accessible technical package allowing independent assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span>

That missing bridge is central to the murder-motive problem. If a surviving laboratory notebook, prototype, technical report or independently witnessed demonstration showed that Eskridge had produced repeatable anomalous thrust, the strategic-value hypothesis would become substantially more concrete. If the unpublished work instead proved to be an interesting theoretical model with inconclusive laboratory data, the proposition that an aerospace company would murder its researcher to suppress it would become considerably weaker.

At present, outsiders cannot make that distinction.

## Secrecy is not itself proof of success

Eskridge's case sits inside a broader belief that gravity-modification research repeatedly moves from academia into classified defence programmes. Her 2018 presentation explicitly advanced that concern, describing modern research as having migrated towards private-sector or "black budget" environments and ending with the observation that promising results seemed to disappear into [classified work]({{ 'classified-work/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/PDF/HAL5-Dec2018-Talk-AntiGravity.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 Dec2018 Talk AntiGravity</span></span></span>

There is a genuine aerospace context behind part of this picture: governments and defence contractors have historically investigated unconventional propulsion concepts, including ideas that ultimately proved unproductive. But classified interest is not equivalent to scientific validation. Defence agencies routinely investigate speculative technologies precisely because their potential payoff would be enormous **if** they worked.

The same caution applies to the missing 2020 paper. A NASA intellectual-property review, assuming Eskridge's reported description is accurate, would demonstrate an institutional connection to material originating at Marshall Space Flight Center. It would not by itself tell us whether NASA considered the physics revolutionary, merely proprietary, technically questionable or unsuitable for release for some other reason. The subsequent absence of the promised publication is an unanswered question, not evidence that the paper contained a successful antigravity formula.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.ph/O3Pgg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.ph">[Archive.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AMY ESKRIDGE: The DEATH of a promising ANTI-GRAVITY researcher. ## What would materially strengthen the murder-motive claim?

The unresolved evidence gap is therefore unusually specific. Establishing that Eskridge was a genuine unconventional-propulsion researcher is no longer the difficult part; contemporary HAL5 material does that.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/program-2018-12.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">program 2018 12.shtml</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 December 2018 Program...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2018</span></span></span> The decisive question is whether her work progressed from exploratory research to strategically valuable technology.

Several discoveries could substantially change the assessment: the unreleased paper or NASA review correspondence; dated Institute laboratory notebooks; raw experimental measurements; photographs and engineering specifications of a prototype; testimony from technically qualified collaborators who directly operated the apparatus; or an independent laboratory's replication of an Eskridge experiment. Documentary evidence that a company or government programme explicitly sought, acquired or attempted to suppress that particular technology would be even more significant.

Without such material, the argument risks becoming circular: Eskridge's breakthrough cannot be shown because it was suppressed, while the supposed suppression is inferred from the absence of the breakthrough evidence. That reasoning cannot distinguish successful secret technology from unfinished research that simply never reached publication.

## The motive gap remains the decisive weakness

Eskridge's gravity-modification interests should not be minimised. Contemporary sources establish an unusually serious engagement with unconventional propulsion: she created organisations around the subject, publicly surveyed experimental approaches, worked alongside a retired NASA engineer and argued for privately funded, openly conducted research into ideas that she believed were being lost to classified programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hal5.org/program-2018-12.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal5.org">[hal5.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal5.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">program 2018 12.shtml</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HAL5 December 2018 Program...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2018</span></span></span>

There is also an intriguing but unresolved second layer: the reported 2020 statement that the Institute had matured NASA-originated foundational work and possessed theory and supporting data that could not yet be discussed publicly. That is the strongest available indication that Eskridge's organisation believed it had progressed beyond historical review and into original research. The promised public paper, however, has not surfaced in the accessible record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.ph/O3Pgg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.ph">[Archive.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AMY ESKRIDGE: The DEATH of a promising ANTI-GRAVITY researcher. Conducting anti-gravity research? You probably work in Huntsville, cease...</span></span></span>

Consequently, the evidence does not presently support the confident statement that Eskridge was murdered **because she had working antigravity technology**. The [congressional record]({{ 'congressional-record/' | relative_url }}) confirms that Milburn's allegation of murder by a private aerospace company exists; it does not verify it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Written-Testimony-Shellenberger.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oversight Committee</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion is narrower and more useful. Amy Eskridge was pursuing gravity modification seriously enough that a research-related motive is a coherent hypothesis to examine. But the central premise needed to make that motive compelling — evidence that she actually possessed a reproducible, strategically valuable propulsion breakthrough — remains missing from the public record. Until the unpublished research, experimental data or comparable primary evidence emerges, the technological motive remains an allegation built around an unresolved gap rather than a demonstrated explanation for her death.
