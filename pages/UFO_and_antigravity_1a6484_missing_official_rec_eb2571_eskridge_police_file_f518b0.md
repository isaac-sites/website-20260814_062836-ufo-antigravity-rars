## Introduction

The most important unresolved issue in Amy Eskridge’s death is not whether public speculation can supply an alternative explanation. It cannot. It is whether the police file contains the ordinary scene evidence needed to test the explanation already reported: that Eskridge died in Huntsville, Alabama, on 11 June 2022 from a self-inflicted gunshot wound. Recent reporting continues to describe that as the official finding while acknowledging that only limited official detail has been made public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foxnews.com/politics/11th-scientist-death-emerges-string-missing-dead-officials-access-us-secrets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foxnews.com">[Fox News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foxnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fox NewsTrump vows answers on scientists dying under unusual circumstances &#124; Fox NewsApril 17, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 17, 2026</span></span></span>

That distinction is crucial. No publicly accessible comprehensive police file located for this report establishes who discovered Eskridge, the condition and security of the scene, the firearm’s location and ownership, what forensic testing was performed, what witnesses told investigators, or how investigators assessed possible third-party involvement. The absence of those details does not make homicide more likely. It means that outsiders cannot independently reconstruct the scene investigation from primary records.

The gap is also less anomalous than it can appear online. Huntsville Police Department (HPD) maintains criminal case reports, but its published access rules restrict ordinary copies to listed victims or reporting parties; someone listed in another capacity must subpoena a full report. More importantly, Alabama law expressly protects law-enforcement investigative reports, field notes, witness statements and related investigative writings or recordings from public disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.huntsvilleal.gov/residents/public-safety/huntsville-police/records/reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvilleal.gov">[City of Huntsville]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvilleal.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">City of Huntsville Records &amp; Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">City of HuntsvilleRecords &amp; Reports - City of Huntsville...</span></span></span>

## What the police file could establish

A police file matters because a cause-of-death label is the end of an investigative chain, not a substitute for the chain itself. In Eskridge’s case, public reporting gives the conclusion — suicide involving a self-inflicted gunshot — but comparatively little of the scene-level information from which investigators reached it. Fox News, for example, reported in April 2026 that her death had been described as a self-inflicted gunshot wound while “limited official details” had been released.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foxnews.com/politics/11th-scientist-death-emerges-string-missing-dead-officials-access-us-secrets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foxnews.com">[Fox News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foxnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fox NewsTrump vows answers on scientists dying under unusual circumstances &#124; Fox NewsApril 17, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 17, 2026</span></span></span>

A sufficiently complete HPD file could potentially answer several concrete questions that cannot presently be settled from news coverage alone:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Entry and scene security.** Officers’ narratives could record whether doors and windows were locked or open, whether there were indications of forced entry, who had access to the residence and whether anyone entered before police secured it.
* **Discovery of the body.** Dispatch records, initial officer reports and witness statements could identify who found Eskridge, when she was last known to be alive, when emergency services were contacted and what responders encountered on arrival.
* **The firearm.** Evidence logs could establish where the weapon was found, its ownership or provenance, how it was positioned in relation to Eskridge and whether it was collected for laboratory examination.
* **Forensic work.** Depending on what investigators considered necessary, the file could show whether officers requested fingerprint, DNA, firearms, gunshot-residue or other examinations, and whether photographs, measurements or trajectory observations were made.
* **Other physical evidence.** Evidence inventories could identify ammunition, cartridge cases, electronic devices, medications, written material or other objects investigators regarded as relevant.
* **Digital evidence.** If investigators examined a telephone, computer, messages, security footage or other electronic material, reports could show what was sought and whether it affected the investigative conclusion.
* **Investigative reasoning.** Supplemental reports could reveal whether detectives considered alternatives to suicide and what evidence caused them to accept or reject those alternatives.

</div>

These are not claims that any particular item exists in Eskridge’s file. They are the categories of information that make the unreleased police material consequential. HPD itself says its Records Division maintains criminal case reports, while Alabama law separately identifies reports, field notes, witness statements and investigative recordings as protected investigative materials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.huntsvilleal.gov/residents/public-safety/huntsville-police/records/reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvilleal.gov">[City of Huntsville]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvilleal.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">City of Huntsville Records &amp; Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">City of HuntsvilleRecords &amp; Reports - City of Huntsville...</span></span></span>

That last distinction prevents a common error in discussion of the case. Saying that a particular forensic result has not been published is not equivalent to saying that police never performed the examination. Conversely, repeatedly citing the reported suicide determination does not tell the public which tests were performed. Without the underlying documentation, both assertions go beyond what outsiders can demonstrate.

## Witnesses and possible third-party involvement

The police file has acquired greater importance because accounts that resurfaced after Eskridge’s death describe fears she expressed before June 2022. In 2026, reporting highlighted messages supplied by Franc Milburn, a former British military and intelligence figure who said Eskridge had told him she feared for her safety and had explicitly rejected the possibility that she would intentionally kill herself. Other reporting has described allegations by Eskridge of surveillance or harassment before her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hindustantimes.com.</span></span></span>

Those accounts are relevant leads, but they are not forensic proof of what happened on 11 June. A statement made before death that a person does not intend suicide can legitimately prompt questions; it cannot by itself establish that a later death was homicide. Likewise, allegations of stalking or harassment require [corroboration]({{ 'corroboration/' | relative_url }}) before they can be connected to a death.

This is precisely where the police file could move the discussion beyond competing narratives. The useful questions are narrower than the more dramatic claims circulating online. Did investigators know about Eskridge’s previous allegations? Did they interview people to whom she had described them? Were particular individuals or vehicles identified? Were relevant messages supplied to police? Did investigators examine security footage, access records or electronic communications? Was there evidence placing any other person at or near the death scene during the relevant period?

There is a further evidential distinction between **pre-death allegations** and **scene evidence**. Even if every published message attributed to Eskridge were authenticated, that would establish what she communicated and perhaps what she believed at the time. It would not establish that the person or organisation she feared existed as described, that an alleged stalker entered her home, or that such a person caused her death. Those propositions require independent evidence. Conversely, the absence of publicly presented evidence of an intruder is weaker than an investigative finding based on documented examination of entry points, cameras, witnesses and forensic traces.

The same caution applies in the other direction. Public reporting has stated that authorities found no basis to replace the suicide determination, and Eskridge’s death continues to be reported as suicide. There is presently no publicly established official finding that her [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) or UFO-related research caused her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foxnews.com/politics/11th-scientist-death-emerges-string-missing-dead-officials-access-us-secrets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foxnews.com">[Fox News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foxnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fox NewsTrump vows answers on scientists dying under unusual circumstances &#124; Fox NewsApril 17, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 17, 2026</span></span></span> But without the investigative file, the public cannot see exactly what HPD did to exclude third-party involvement or how thoroughly investigators explored the concerns now being cited.

## Firearm evidence is a major missing layer

Because the reported mechanism is a gunshot wound, the firearm evidence is particularly important. A meaningful reconstruction would normally depend on several pieces of information rather than the mere presence of a gun: the weapon’s location, its relationship to the body, ammunition and cartridge evidence, wound findings, scene photographs and whatever laboratory examinations investigators requested.

None of those details should be reverse-engineered from the phrase “self-inflicted gunshot wound”. That phrase communicates a reported conclusion, not the underlying dataset. Recent mainstream coverage still provides the conclusion without reproducing the primary police evidence behind it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foxnews.com/politics/11th-scientist-death-emerges-string-missing-dead-officials-access-us-secrets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foxnews.com">[Fox News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foxnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fox NewsTrump vows answers on scientists dying under unusual circumstances &#124; Fox NewsApril 17, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 17, 2026</span></span></span>

This gap also illustrates why an autopsy report and a police report would answer different questions. A forensic examination could document injuries and medical findings, whereas police records could connect those findings to the physical scene: where the firearm was recovered, who could access the location, what witnesses observed and what evidence officers collected. A strong independent assessment would ideally compare both sets of records rather than treating either one as complete by itself.

Alabama’s restrictions on death records further limit what an ordinary member of the public can check independently. The Alabama Department of Public Health states that [death certificates]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }}) remain confidential for 25 years after death, with access during that period limited to specified relatives, estate representatives and other authorised people. Eskridge’s 2022 certificate therefore is not simply an unrestricted public document that any researcher should expect to retrieve online.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/vitalrecords/death-certificates.html?iframed=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alabamapublichealth.gov">[Alabama Department of Public Health]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alabamapublichealth.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alabama Department of Public HealthDeath Certificates &#124; Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH)March 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why the police records may remain unavailable

The strongest mundane explanation for the missing public police file is Alabama law itself. Section 12-21-3.1 of the Alabama Code states that law-enforcement investigative reports and related investigative material “are not public records”. The protection expressly encompasses records, field notes, witness statements and other investigative writings or recordings. The statute also creates a court-supervised route by which non-criminal parties may, under specified circumstances, seek photographs, documents or tangible evidence, with protections for chain of custody and investigative work product.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-12/chapter-21/article-1/division-1/division-1/section-12-21-3-1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawAlabama Code § 12-21-3.1 (2025) - Subpoena of Law Enforcement Officers and Investigative Reports; Disposition of Criminal Matte...</span></span></span>

Importantly, this protection does not disappear automatically simply because an investigation is over. The Alabama Supreme Court addressed that issue in *Something Extra Publishing, Inc. v. Mack* in 2021, a dispute involving access to law-enforcement investigative material. The court record reflects reliance on Section 12-21-3.1’s rule that investigative files are not public records. The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press consequently describes Alabama investigative reports and related material as exempt and notes that the statutory privilege has been applied to a closed case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/alabama/supreme-court/2021/1190106.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawSomething Extra Publishing, Inc., d/b/a Lagniappe Weekly v. Huntsville’s own procedures reinforce the practical barrier. HPD says criminal case reports may be purchased by the listed victim or reporting party upon providing identification, a case number, incident details and a reason for the request. Its guidance adds that someone listed in another capacity must have the record subpoenaed to obtain a full copy. Separately, the City of Huntsville operates a public-records request process, but explicitly notes that records exempt from public disclosure need not be produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.huntsvilleal.gov/residents/public-safety/huntsville-police/records/reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvilleal.gov">[City of Huntsville]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvilleal.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">City of Huntsville Records &amp; Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">City of HuntsvilleRecords &amp; Reports - City of Huntsville...</span></span></span>

These rules make it unsafe to infer a cover-up merely from unsuccessful internet searches or public-record requests. Alabama has an unusually explicit statutory barrier protecting precisely the materials — witness statements, investigative reports, recordings and related evidence — that would be most useful for resolving the Eskridge debate.

At the same time, the legal explanation for non-disclosure does not make the evidential gap disappear. It explains **why the public may lack the evidence**; it does not turn unavailable evidence into verified evidence.

## What can and cannot be said with confidence

The Eskridge case is best understood by separating three different levels of certainty.

**First, there are well-supported public facts.** Eskridge died on 11 June 2022 in Huntsville at the age of 34. News organisations report her death as suicide involving a self-inflicted gunshot wound. She had publicly discussed unconventional propulsion research, and subsequent coverage has revived statements and messages in which she expressed fear about threats or harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foxnews.com/politics/11th-scientist-death-emerges-string-missing-dead-officials-access-us-secrets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foxnews.com">[Fox News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foxnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fox NewsTrump vows answers on scientists dying under unusual circumstances &#124; Fox NewsApril 17, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 17, 2026</span></span></span>

**Second, there are claims that remain unverified at the level required to explain her death.** Published accounts of stalking, targeting or threats may justify asking what investigators knew and what they checked, but the material currently in public view does not establish that a third party killed Eskridge. No official finding located for this report links her death to an aerospace company, government programme, UFO disclosure effort or antigravity research. Fox’s 2026 coverage explicitly noted that there was no publicly available evidence connecting her death to the other scientist cases then attracting attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foxnews.com/politics/11th-scientist-death-emerges-string-missing-dead-officials-access-us-secrets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foxnews.com">[Fox News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foxnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fox NewsTrump vows answers on scientists dying under unusual circumstances &#124; Fox NewsApril 17, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 17, 2026</span></span></span>

**Third, there are questions that cannot presently be answered responsibly from public reporting.** These include the exact condition of the scene; who entered it and when; whether entry points showed anything suspicious; the detailed position and provenance of the firearm; which forensic tests were conducted; what nearby cameras or digital devices showed; what witnesses told police; and what investigative steps were taken to exclude another person’s involvement.

That third category is easy to mishandle. An unanswered question is not affirmative evidence of foul play. But neither should an answer be invented merely because it seems likely. The intellectually useful position is to preserve the distinction between a reported official determination and the still-unseen evidence supporting it.

## What would materially change the assessment

Not every additional document would have equal value. The most informative release would be a coherent package linking the first response to the final investigative conclusion: initial officer narratives, incident and supplemental reports, scene and evidence logs, relevant witness statements, firearm or laboratory results, and records showing whether detectives investigated possible third-party involvement.

Such records could substantially strengthen the reported suicide determination if, for example, they documented a secured scene, evidence consistent with self-infliction, no credible indication of another person’s presence and investigative follow-up on the concerns Eskridge had previously expressed. Equally, documented inconsistencies — unexplained access, conflicting witness accounts, forensic findings incompatible with the reconstruction, or significant leads that investigators knowingly left unresolved — would justify a different level of scrutiny. The point is that those conclusions depend on records, not on the direction in which speculation happens to lean.

Until such material becomes accessible, Eskridge’s police file represents a genuine limit on public certainty rather than evidence for a particular alternative theory. Alabama law provides a straightforward reason why detailed investigative records may remain outside ordinary public access, while current reporting supplies a reported suicide determination but not enough scene documentation for outsiders to reproduce the investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-12/chapter-21/article-1/division-1/division-1/section-12-21-3-1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawAlabama Code § 12-21-3.1 (2025) - Subpoena of Law Enforcement Officers and Investigative Reports; Disposition of Criminal Matte...</span></span></span>

For the wider discussion of allegedly suspicious deaths connected with UFO or unconventional-propulsion research, that is the most defensible significance of the Eskridge records gap. It does not establish suppression. It does not overturn the reported official finding. It identifies exactly what evidence would be needed to move the case from competing public narratives towards independently checkable conclusions.
