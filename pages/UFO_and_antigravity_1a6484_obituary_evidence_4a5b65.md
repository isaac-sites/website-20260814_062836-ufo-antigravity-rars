## Introduction

An obituary can be strong evidence that a disputed researcher was a real person, worked in a particular field, belonged to named institutions and died on a stated date. It is usually much weaker evidence for *how* or *why* that person died. That distinction matters in claims about suspicious deaths among UFO, unconventional-propulsion and [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers, where a short memorial notice is sometimes treated either as definitive proof of an official account or, conversely, as suspicious because it omits a cause of death.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/UFO_and_antigravity_1a6484_obituary_evidence_4a5b65-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Obituaries" width="1024" height="559" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

Neither inference is sound on its own. Obituaries are primarily records of a life and announcements to a community, not forensic reports. Cause of death may be absent by family choice, because it was unknown when the notice was written, or simply because the writer considered it private or unnecessary. For contested cases, the obituary is therefore best used as an identification and chronology anchor. Questions about suicide, accident, homicide, medical causation or investigative findings require independent records such as [death certificates]({{ 'death-certificate/' | relative_url }}), coroner or medical-examiner findings, police material, court records and well-sourced contemporary reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/writing-cause-of-death-statements.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NVSS - Writing Cause-of-Death Statements...</span></span></span>

## What an obituary can establish reliably

The greatest value of an obituary is often mundane but indispensable: it establishes the basic framework against which more dramatic claims can be tested. A funeral-home notice may give a full name, age, date of death, relatives, location, funeral arrangements and selected career information. Newspaper and professional-society obituaries can add education, institutional affiliations and professional accomplishments. Genealogical guidance from FamilySearch accordingly recommends treating obituaries as useful clues that should still be verified, because the identity of the person supplying the information is often unknown and omissions are common.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cms-z-assets.familysearch.org/0d/28/ddb27e929fdc9cdc3b05c3b8fb3b/morton-finding-and-using-obituaries-more-effectively.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cms-z-assets.familysearch.org">[FamilySearch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cms-z-assets.familysearch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Family Search Finding and Using U.S. Obituaries More Effectively</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Family Search Finding and Using U.S. Obituaries More Effectively</span></span></span>

## Why silence about cause of death proves very little

A recurrent mistake in suspicious-death discussions is to treat the absence of a cause as evidence that somebody deliberately concealed it. In ordinary obituary practice, however, cause of death is optional. Guidance for preparing obituaries explicitly lists it as information that may be included rather than as a required field. Funeral notices commonly concentrate instead on relatives, career, character, memorial services and burial arrangements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.coopmemorial.org/uploads/1/0/2/5/10254188/writing_and_submitting_an_obituary_guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coopmemorial.org">[COOPERATIVE MEMORIAL SOCIETY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coopmemorial.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">COOPERATIVE MEMORIAL SOCIETYWriting and Submitting an Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 6, 2026</span></span></span>

This matters particularly in the case of Amy Eskridge. Her 2022 obituary records her death on 11 June at the age of 34, her University of Alabama in Huntsville education, her scientific interests and her role as co-founder of the Institute for Exotic Science. It does not state a medical cause of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2022</span></span></span>

That omission is a genuine evidential gap, but it is not affirmative evidence of homicide, poisoning or official suppression. A memorial notice does not normally function as a medical certificate, and families can choose how much information to disclose. The correct conclusion from an obituary that gives no cause is simply: **the obituary does not establish the cause**.

The same caution applies to euphemistic language. Ning Li's notice says that she "passed peacefully away", but this phrase does not amount to a forensic determination of the disease, injury or chain of events that caused death. In her case, later family reporting provided information about the serious injuries she had sustained years earlier. The memorial's wording neither establishes nor disproves that medical history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/obituary/ning-li?lud=4CF765EE88E7526FCBA619C30101F7E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral HomeNing Li Obituary July 27, 2021 - Berryhill Funeral Home &amp; CrematoryJuly 27, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2021</span></span></span>

This distinction becomes particularly important online, where memorial pages are often copied into databases stripped of context. A phrase such as "died suddenly", "passed peacefully" or simply "died" may look significant when isolated in a conspiracy timeline. In the conventions of obituary writing, however, these expressions can reflect tone and privacy rather than an investigative conclusion.

Even positive biographical statements deserve calibration. An obituary is designed to commemorate the deceased, so descriptions of scientific stature may be affectionate or promotional rather than independently evaluated assessments. Li's obituary, for example, describes her as one of the world's leading scientists in superconducting antigravity research. It is useful evidence that this was how her work was presented by those commemorating her; it is not, by itself, a scientific validation of the claimed antigravity effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/obituary/ning-li?lud=4CF765EE88E7526FCBA619C30101F7E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral HomeNing Li Obituary July 27, 2021 - Berryhill Funeral Home &amp; CrematoryJuly 27, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2021</span></span></span>

## Murder can be real while the proposed motive is wrong

Eugene Mallove provides perhaps the clearest illustration of why researchers should separate three different questions: whether a death was violent, whether it was homicide, and whether the homicide was connected to controversial research.

Mallove, a prominent advocate of [cold fusion]({{ 'cold-fusion/' | relative_url }}) and unconventional energy research, was unquestionably killed in 2004. Early tributes from within the alternative-energy community reported the homicide while the motive remained unresolved, creating an understandable opening for speculation that his work might have been involved. One contemporary tribute even stressed the timing of his death relative to developments in cold-fusion research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.enterprisemission.org/_articles/05-18-2004/eugene_mallove.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: enterprisemission.org">[Enterprise Mission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">enterprisemission.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enterprise Mission In Memory of Dr. Eugene Mallove</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enterprise Mission In Memory of Dr. Eugene Mallove</span></span></span>

But an obituary or memorial composed in the immediate aftermath could not possibly contain the results of a criminal process that unfolded over the following decade. Later court proceedings developed a very different evidential picture.

A Connecticut Appellate Court decision recounting the case says Mallove's body was found on 14 May 2004 at a Norwich rental property from which tenants had recently been evicted. The decision describes the police investigation into people connected with those former tenants and records the subsequent murder and conspiracy prosecution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jud.ct.gov/external/supapp/Cases/AROap/AP230/AP230.93.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jud.ct.gov">[Connecticut Judicial Branch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jud.ct.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Connecticut Judicial Branch************************************************</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Connecticut Judicial Branch************************************************</span></span></span>

Associated Press reporting on the 2015 sentencing of Mozzelle Brown likewise reported that prosecutors tied the attack to the property: Mallove had been preparing the house for rental after relatives of one participant had been evicted. Brown received a 58-year sentence following convictions for murder and conspiracy; another participant, Chad Schaffer, had previously received a prison term for manslaughter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pressherald.com/2015/01/07/man-convicted-of-killing-physicist-gets-58-years-in-prison/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pressherald.com">[The Portland Press Herald]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pressherald.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pressherald.com.</span></span></span>

The case therefore demonstrates a crucial limitation of contemporary death notices. A memorial can accurately establish that somebody was killed yet be incapable of identifying the eventual motive or perpetrators because those facts were still under investigation. Preserving the uncertainty of the earliest source is appropriate; freezing the whole case at that early stage is not.

Mallove is consequently a useful warning against a common evidential shortcut: "He was a controversial energy researcher, and he was murdered; therefore he was murdered *because* of the research." The first two propositions can both be true while the causal claim remains unsupported. Later investigative and judicial evidence must be allowed to change the assessment.

## Which record answers which question?

The simplest way to avoid over-reading obituaries is to match each disputed claim to the type of source capable of answering it. An obituary is one layer in an evidence stack, not the top of the hierarchy for every question.

**Identity and biography.** Professional-society obituaries, university memorials, funeral notices and contemporary newspaper obituaries are useful for names, dates, family relationships, employment, degrees and professional history. Cross-checking is still worthwhile where credentials or claimed achievements are central to the controversy. McDonald's professional obituary is particularly strong for this purpose because it was published by the American Meteorological Society and gives specific institutional and educational details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/obit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Mc Carthy Dissertation: James E. Mc Donald Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesMcCarthy Dissertation: James E. McDonald Obituary...</span></span></span>

**Date and place of death.** Obituaries can supply a strong initial anchor, particularly when funeral arrangements and surviving relatives corroborate the identity. Cemetery records, contemporaneous newspapers and civil death records can then provide independent confirmation.

**Medical cause of death.** This is primarily a death-certification question, not an obituary question. The US National Center for Health Statistics explains that the medical portion of a death certificate records the diseases, injuries or poisoning involved in the death and is certified, depending on jurisdiction, by a physician, coroner or medical examiner. The underlying cause is intended to identify the disease or injury that initiated the fatal sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/writing-cause-of-death-statements.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NVSS - Writing Cause-of-Death Statements...</span></span></span>

Even a death certificate should not be treated as supernatural certainty. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that certification represents a medical opinion based on the information available to the certifier, which may include history, testing and autopsy results. But it is still evidentially much better suited to a cause-of-death claim than a memorial notice written for family and friends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/writing-cod-statements/death_certification_problems.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdc.gov.</span></span></span>

**Manner and circumstances of a violent death.** Police reports, coroner or medical-examiner material, court filings and reliable contemporaneous journalism become central. McDonald's professional obituary does not give the reported suicide circumstances; local historical reporting does. Mallove's early memorials could report a homicide but not the later prosecution; court records can.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tucson.com/news/blogs/morgue-tales/tales-from-the-morgue-ufos-are-real/article_6593476e-745e-11e1-838d-001871e3ce6c.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tucson.com">[Arizona Daily Star]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tucson.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Arizona Daily Star Tales from the Morgue: UFOs are real</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Arizona Daily StarTales from the Morgue: UFOs are realMarch 23, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 23, 2012</span></span></span>

**Motive.** This is usually the hardest claim and requires still stronger evidence. Even proving homicide does not prove why it happened. Evidence might include communications, witness testimony, investigative findings, financial or interpersonal records, threats that can be authenticated, or findings tested in court. A researcher's controversial speciality is contextual information, not proof of motive.

## A practical test for disputed researcher deaths

For alleged UFO- or antigravity-related deaths, an obituary is most valuable when its claims are separated into what it actually says and what later commentators infer from it.

A useful reading method is:

1. **Confirm the person.** Check the full name, dates, locality, family and institutional connections to make sure the obituary concerns the intended researcher rather than somebody with a similar name.
2. **Extract only explicit facts.** Record what the notice actually establishes — for example, "died on 27 July 2021" — rather than upgrading "passed peacefully" into a specific medical diagnosis.
3. **Mark omissions as unknown, not suspicious.** If no cause appears, write "cause not stated in this obituary", not "cause withheld".
4. **Separate biography from scientific validation.** [Memorial praise]({{ 'memorial-praise/' | relative_url }}) can establish how somebody was remembered without proving that disputed experimental claims were correct.
5. **Move to the appropriate independent record.** For cause, consult death certification or medical-examiner evidence; for criminal circumstances, police and court records; for allegations of threats or suppression, seek independently authenticated contemporary documentation.
6. **Allow later evidence to supersede early uncertainty.** An obituary written days after a death may necessarily be less informative than a court judgment or investigation completed years later.

This method produces different but more defensible conclusions in the best-known cases. McDonald's obituary firmly establishes his scientific identity and date of death but requires other evidence to address suicide. Li's obituary demonstrates that the supposedly vanished physicist lived until 2021 but does not explain her years outside public view; later reporting based on her son provides that missing history. Eskridge's obituary confirms her death and scientific biography but leaves the cause unstated, so the obituary alone cannot adjudicate later suspicious-death allegations. Mallove's case shows most sharply that even a confirmed murder cannot be attributed to unconventional research merely because early memorials lacked the investigative evidence that emerged later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/obit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Mc Carthy Dissertation: James E. ## Why independent records remain necessary

Obituaries occupy an unusual evidential position. They are close to the deceased person's family and community and can preserve details that official records omit. The Huntsville-Madison County Public Library, for example, describes obituary collections as valuable sources of personal history precisely because they often contain information absent from government records. That makes them useful historical documents rather than disposable secondary sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hmcpl.org/databases/obituary-index" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hmcpl.org">[Huntsville-Madison County Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hmcpl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hmcpl.org.</span></span></span>

Their strengths, however, are almost the inverse of those required to prove a suspicious-death theory. Obituaries are normally written to announce, commemorate and remember. They are not designed to reconstruct a shooting, determine toxicology, test competing medical explanations, establish criminal intent or evaluate an intelligence operation.

In [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researcher cases, this means an obituary should usually occupy the **first evidential layer**: it confirms that the researcher existed, helps establish who they were and fixes important points in the chronology. The next layers must answer progressively harder questions. Death certification addresses medical causation; police and coroner material addresses circumstances; court records can test criminal allegations; independently corroborated documentary evidence is needed before tying a death to research, government activity or suppression.

The central rule is therefore simple: **an obituary can prove much about a life without proving the theory attached to a death**. Treating that boundary seriously does not dismiss suspicious circumstances. It prevents a gap in a memorial notice from becoming evidence for whatever explanation a reader already prefers, while leaving genuinely unresolved cases unresolved until better records emerge.
