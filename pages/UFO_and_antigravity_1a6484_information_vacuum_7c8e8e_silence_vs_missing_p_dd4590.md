## Introduction

In [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) lore, a scientist can be described as having “disappeared” when the underlying evidence establishes something much narrower: papers stopped appearing, interviews ceased, a laboratory closed, or outsiders could no longer obtain a reply. That is **publication silence**, not by itself a missing-person case. A genuine missing-person claim concerns a person's whereabouts: relatives, colleagues or authorities cannot establish where they are. Police guidance makes this distinction unusually clear. The College of Policing defines a missing person as someone “whose whereabouts cannot be established” until the person is located and their wellbeing confirmed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.college.police.uk/app/major-investigation-and-public-protection/missing-persons/missing-persons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: college.police.uk">[College of Policing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">college.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College of Policing Missing persons &#124; College of Policing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">College of PolicingMissing persons &#124; College of PolicingNovember 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 22, 2016</span></span></span>

This distinction matters especially in [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) stories because secrecy is already part of the narrative. Defence funding plus an abruptly thinning publication record can look suggestive without demonstrating that anyone physically vanished. Physicist Ning Li is the clearest example: her scientific visibility largely disappeared, but later evidence shows that Li herself did not.

## What the public record can actually establish

A publication record can establish that somebody was scientifically active at a particular time. It can show affiliations, co-authors, research topics and sometimes funding. It can also establish that the visible trail subsequently became sparse. What it cannot establish, simply by ending, is what happened to the author.

In 1991 she and Douglas Torr published *Effects of a Gravitomagnetic Field on Pure Superconductors* in *Physical Review D*, with both authors associated with the University of Alabama in Huntsville. A further Li–Torr paper appeared in *Physical Review B* in 1992. These are ordinary bibliographic facts preserved by the American Physical Society, PubMed and the US Department of Energy's Office of Scientific and Technical Information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10013404/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Effects of a gravitomagnetic field on pure superconductors</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of a gravitomagnetic field on pure superconductors - PubMedJanuary 15, 1991...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 15, 1991</span></span></span>

In 1997, Li and collaborators published a test of a proposed gravitational effect involving type-II YBCO superconductors. ## What turns silence into a missing-person case?

A useful way to test claims of a “[missing scientist]({{ 'missing-scientist/' | relative_url }})” is to ask what exactly is alleged to be missing: **the person's publications, their public accessibility, or the person themselves?**

Official missing-person systems are built around the third question. British policing guidance defines somebody as missing when their whereabouts cannot be established, while Police.uk tells families they do not have to wait 24 hours to make such a report. In the United States, the National Missing and Unidentified Persons System, or NamUs, is an official Department of Justice programme through which law enforcement, investigators and families work to resolve missing-person cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.police.uk/report-missing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: police.uk">[police.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UKReport a missing person &#124; Police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UKReport a missing person &#124; Police.uk</span></span></span>

There need not always be a publicly searchable database entry: reporting systems vary, some entries are voluntary, privacy matters, and not every case receives extensive media coverage. NamUs itself notes that entry requirements vary between jurisdictions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/namus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[National Institute of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span> Therefore, absence from a database cannot prove that somebody was never missing.

But the evidential direction still matters. A defensible claim that a scientist genuinely disappeared would normally benefit from evidence such as a police missing-person report, a family appeal, an employer reporting that the person's whereabouts were unknown, contemporary news coverage of a search, an official case file, or other direct testimony establishing that people who ordinarily knew where the person was could no longer locate them.

By contrast, searches for publications ending in a certain year, unanswered emails from journalists, disappearance from conference programmes, or a lack of public information about a defence-funded project demonstrate **loss of public visibility**. Without additional evidence, they cannot carry the stronger claim.

## Ning Li shows why the distinction matters

Li's story is particularly useful because the apparent disappearance can be watched forming inside the documentary gap.

The conventional publication trail does become thin in the early 2000s, but it does not end at a single mysterious point. A 2003 paper attributed to Ning Li and James C. Bradas, *Measurability of AC Gravity Fields*, survives in material from a MITRE-hosted conference. It discusses generating and measuring alternating gravitational fields. Whatever one thinks of the underlying theory, its existence means that the simple narrative “military funding in 2001, then she instantly vanished” is chronologically wrong.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://groups.google.com/g/sci.space/c/RKfc3i7Xc1g" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: groups.google.com">[Google Groups]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">groups.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

The later silence was nevertheless real. The *Huntsville Business Journal*, investigating the disappearance story in 2023, found that Li ceased producing the sort of publications by which people following her academic career had previously tracked her. AC Gravity remained on the corporate record, while publicly visible scientific results became scarce. That combination created precisely the sort of [information vacuum]({{ 'info-vacuums/' | relative_url }}) in which “I cannot find anything recent about this scientist” could mutate into “nobody can find this scientist”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntsvillebusinessjournal.com/news/2023/07/30/solving-the-mystery-of-huntsvilles-brilliant-scientist-disappearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvillebusinessjournal.com">[Huntsville Business Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvillebusinessjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on huntsvillebusinessjournal.com.</span></span></span>

Yet Li's eventual obituary gives a very different kind of record. Berryhill Funeral Home recorded that Dr Ning Li of Huntsville died on 27 July 2021, listed her son and grandchildren, and announced a visitation, funeral service and burial at Faith Memorial Park. This is not evidence about the success or failure of her research, but it is highly relevant to the disappearance claim: she had a documented family and local life in Huntsville at the end of it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/obituary/ning-li?lud=4CF765EE88E7526FCBA619C30101F7E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span></span></span>

The 2023 *Huntsville Business Journal* investigation went further by interviewing Li's son, George Men. Its reconstruction reported that Li's later years included a serious 2014 road accident that left her badly impaired. The article therefore replaced a long-running internet mystery with a much more ordinary — and sadder — explanation for at least part of her later invisibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntsvillebusinessjournal.com/news/2023/07/30/solving-the-mystery-of-huntsvilles-brilliant-scientist-disappearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvillebusinessjournal.com">[Huntsville Business Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvillebusinessjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on huntsvillebusinessjournal.com.</span></span></span>

None of this determines what happened inside every non-public research programme with which Li may have been associated. It does, however, undermine the specific claim that the thinning scientific record itself demonstrates that Li physically disappeared.

## Clues that point to professional withdrawal instead

Scientific careers leave several different trails, and they do not necessarily stop together. Journal articles are only one of them. A researcher may disappear from bibliographic databases while remaining visible through patents, conference proceedings, company registrations, professional directories, [local records]({{ 'local-records/' | relative_url }}) or family notices.

In a UFO or antigravity case, four patterns should therefore lower confidence in a literal-disappearance interpretation.

**The public trail fades gradually rather than terminating at a documented incident.** Li's 2003 conference material is important precisely because it complicates an apparently sharp boundary between “public scientist” and “missing scientist”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://groups.google.com/g/sci.space/c/RKfc3i7Xc1g" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: groups.google.com">[Google Groups]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">groups.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

**The researcher has moved from academia into private or government-sponsored work.** Academic science rewards publication; proprietary research often does not. Military involvement does not prove classification, but neither should a lack of subsequent journal papers be surprising enough to count as evidence of physical disappearance.

**Independent traces of ordinary life continue.** An address, business filing, family account, obituary or local event does not tell readers what somebody was researching, but it can directly contradict the idea that the person's whereabouts were wholly unknown. Li's funeral record is particularly difficult to reconcile with sweeping versions of the claim that she simply vanished without trace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/obituary/ning-li?lud=4CF765EE88E7526FCBA619C30101F7E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[Berryhill Funeral Home]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span></span></span>

**The disappearance claim comes from people trying to contact the researcher rather than people responsible for their welfare.** A journalist, UFO researcher or interested physicist receiving no reply is evidence that the person was inaccessible *to that correspondent*. It is not equivalent to a relative telling police that the person cannot be located.

These distinctions do not require assuming that every information gap is innocent. They simply keep the strength of the conclusion proportional to the strength of the evidence.

## Why UFO and antigravity stories blur the categories

Antigravity research is unusually fertile ground for this confusion because publication silence already fits the expectations of the surrounding story. Once military funding, advanced propulsion and unconventional physics appear together, an absence of information can easily be interpreted as information about secrecy.

Li's case contains an especially potent sequence: peer-reviewed gravity-related papers, experiments involving NASA-associated researchers, an Army-funded project and then a sharp reduction in publicly accessible work. Every component except the final inference is independently documentable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.43.457" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.aps.org">[aps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.aps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aps.org.</span></span></span>

The inference is nevertheless the decisive part. “The government funded her and then her publications stopped” can be verified without establishing “the government made her disappear”. The first statement describes a sequence in the documentary record. The second proposes a cause and a physical event for which additional evidence is required.

Older internet discussions illustrate how readily that distinction collapsed. Archived material concerning antigravity researchers explicitly labelled Li a “missing scientist” while simultaneously using the defence-funding record as an important part of the explanation for where she had supposedly gone. In other words, evidence that she had entered a less publicly visible research environment was being treated as evidence that she herself was missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.projectavalon.net/forum/showthread.php?t=1785" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projectavalon.net">[projectavalon.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projectavalon.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anti Gravity Research And The Missing Dr. Ning Li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anti Gravity Research And The Missing Dr. Ning Li</span></span></span>

There is also a self-reinforcing effect. Once an article calls somebody “missing”, later writers may search for evidence of *why* the person disappeared rather than first verifying *whether* a disappearance occurred. Defence grants, inaccessible records and unanswered correspondence then become supporting clues because the central premise has already been accepted. Repetition can make that premise appear independently corroborated even when many versions ultimately descend from the same thin public record.

## A better evidence threshold for “disappeared”

For claims about scientists associated with UFOs, exotic propulsion or antigravity, the word “disappeared” is most useful when its meaning is made explicit.

A researcher who has **stopped publishing** has a publication gap. Someone who has **stopped speaking publicly** has withdrawn from public view. Someone whose subsequent career cannot readily be reconstructed has an **uncertain public record**. A person becomes a credible missing-person case when there is evidence that their actual whereabouts could not be established by people or institutions positioned to know.

The distinction also prevents two opposite errors. It avoids turning mundane professional withdrawal into a conspiracy, but it does not require dismissing genuine missing-person evidence merely because a scientist worked in a controversial field. If a family appeal, police investigation or documented search exists, that evidence should be evaluated on its own merits. Official systems themselves recognise that missing-person cases vary greatly in risk and circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.college.police.uk/app/major-investigation-and-public-protection/missing-persons/missing-persons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: college.police.uk">[College of Policing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">college.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College of Policing Missing persons &#124; College of Policing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">College of PolicingMissing persons &#124; College of PolicingNovember 22, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 22, 2016</span></span></span>

Ning Li demonstrates why the threshold matters so much. The open record supports a genuine scientific mystery in the limited sense that outsiders still lack a complete account of her later research and of what resulted from the military-funded work. It does **not** follow that her physical fate is similarly mysterious. Her documented 2003 activity, later family account and 2021 Huntsville funeral record show how dramatically different those two questions can become.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://groups.google.com/g/sci.space/c/RKfc3i7Xc1g" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: groups.google.com">[google.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">groups.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

## The information vacuum is real; the disappearance may not be

The most defensible conclusion in cases of this kind is sometimes less dramatic but more informative: **the missing object is the record, not necessarily the researcher**.

That distinction leaves legitimate questions intact. Why did publication cease? What happened to an experimental programme? Were results negative, commercially sensitive, abandoned or unavailable for some other reason? Which claims were ever independently replicated? Those are worthwhile questions about science and documentation.

But they are different from asking whether somebody's family, employer or the authorities were unable to find them. Treating the two questions as interchangeable turns an information vacuum into evidence for the very event the vacuum is supposed to prove.

For UFO and antigravity research, where secrecy itself is often part of the proposed explanation, maintaining that separation is especially important. A sparse publication history can establish silence. A missing-person case requires evidence that a person was actually missing. Ning Li's history shows why confusing those standards can transform an incomplete bibliography into a disappearance story that persists long after ordinary records point elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.berryhillfh.com/obituary/ning-li?lud=4CF765EE88E7526FCBA619C30101F7E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: berryhillfh.com">[berryhillfh.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">berryhillfh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Berryhill Funeral Home Ning Li Obituary</span></span></span>
