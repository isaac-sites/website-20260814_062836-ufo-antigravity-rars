## Introduction

James E. McDonald’s charge against [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) was more specific than the familiar claim that the US Air Force simply “covered up UFOs”. He argued that Blue Book’s **method of resolving cases was biased towards conventional explanations**: once a possible balloon, star, aircraft, radar effect or atmospheric phenomenon could be suggested, officials were sometimes too willing to treat that possibility as an adequate solution even when important features of the report did not fit it.

That distinction is central to understanding the records. Blue Book unquestionably solved many ordinary misidentifications, and an unexplained case was not evidence of an extraterrestrial craft. McDonald’s criticism was instead methodological: **how strong did an explanation have to be before Blue Book called a case explained?** After examining hundreds of cases, interviewing witnesses and applying his own expertise in atmospheric physics, he concluded that the threshold was often too low.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.bluebookfiles.org/pdfs/unknown.00%20-%20NARA%20-%20FordUFOFiles%20-%20FordUFO.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.bluebookfiles.org">[Bluebook Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.bluebookfiles.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unknown.00 NARA Ford UFOFiles Ford UFO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bluebook FilesGERALD R. ## How McDonald judged Air Force case resolutions

McDonald approached Blue Book’s explanations by asking whether the proposed cause could reproduce the important characteristics actually reported. That meant more than finding an ordinary phenomenon somewhere in the vicinity. Direction, angular position, duration, motion, weather conditions, radar geometry and the observations of independent witnesses all mattered.

This is especially clear in his treatment of atmospheric explanations. At the 1968 House symposium on UFOs, McDonald discussed the 1952 Washington, DC radar-visual incidents. Rather than merely rejecting the Air Force’s weather-related interpretation, he said he had interviewed five Civil Aeronautics Administration personnel and four airline pilots and examined the relevant atmospheric data. He calculated the radar refractive-index gradient and argued that it was nowhere near that required for radar “ducting”, while the reported propagation angles were also inconsistent with that mechanism. He therefore regarded the conventional explanation as quantitatively inadequate rather than merely unpersuasive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/shg/symposium/mcdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947SHG - UFO Symposium 1968: McDonald Statement...</span></span></span>

The same test appeared in his criticism of meteorological optics. McDonald was himself an atmospheric physicist, so mirages and unusual propagation were squarely within his professional field. He argued that proposed atmospheric explanations had to obey the geometry and physics of those phenomena; invoking an inversion or optical effect by name was not enough. In his congressional testimony he said that, after checking case after case, he believed some meteorological explanations advanced in the UFO debate were inconsistent with established quantitative properties of atmospheric optics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/shg/symposium/mcdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947SHG - UFO Symposium 1968: McDonald Statement...</span></span></span>

That did not mean McDonald believed every UFO report was extraordinary. His research concentrated heavily on cases that had already survived preliminary screening, and he openly acknowledged this selection effect. At the House symposium he explained that he was working largely with “presifted” material supplied through groups such as NICAP and APRO rather than a random sample of raw reports, where ordinary misidentifications were much more common. That qualification matters: his work was primarily a challenge to the handling of the **difficult residue**, not evidence that most things reported as UFOs were genuinely anomalous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/shg/symposium/mcdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947SHG - UFO Symposium 1968: McDonald Statement...</span></span></span>

## Plausible explanations versus forced explanations

The heart of McDonald’s case was the difference between an explanation that was **possible in principle** and one that satisfactorily accounted for the evidence. A star can be mistaken for a UFO; a balloon can behave unexpectedly; unusual atmospheric refraction can produce strange appearances. But establishing that such phenomena exist does not establish that one of them caused a particular sighting.

A vivid example was the 17 April 1966 police pursuit in Ohio and Pennsylvania. Contemporary reporting of McDonald’s investigation described several police officers following or independently observing a luminous object over a prolonged distance. Blue Book’s explanation combined the Echo satellite with Venus: the officers supposedly first saw the satellite and then shifted their attention to the planet. McDonald objected that the reported direction of approach and the duration and movement described by the witnesses did not fit that sequence. His point was not that Venus or Echo could never generate UFO reports; it was that an explanation had to match **this** report before being counted as a solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/edmontonjournal5jun1967.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOS at close sight: the newspapers, Pr. James E. McDonald, 1697June 5, 1967...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 1967</span></span></span>

Another useful example comes from a Blue Book case that McDonald discussed from Kansas City in August 1961. Two witnesses described a large, silent object hovering at close range before departing rapidly. McDonald later interviewed both men and compared their recollections with the Air Force file. He found discrepancies even in how the file characterised the reported shape, while noting that the Air Force had conducted telephone interviews but apparently no substantial subsequent investigation. Blue Book nevertheless retained the case as unidentified. For McDonald, cases of this sort undermined the broader official reassurance that the unexplained residue contained nothing beyond conventional scientific or technological explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/articles/casia_68.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">casia 68</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: - UFOs - An International Scientific Problem Paper Presented at the Canadian Aeronautics and Space Institute As...</span></span></span>

His criticism could also run in the opposite direction: sometimes a case eventually remained officially unidentified only after conventional possibilities had been considered. In discussing one close-range police case, McDonald noted that Blue Book had assembled information about the star Antares and local temperature inversions as possible explanations before ultimately classifying the event as unidentified. That example is important because it shows that Blue Book did **not invariably force every case into an identified category**. The dispute concerns how consistently and rigorously that restraint was exercised across the programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saturdaynightuforia.com/html/articles/articlehtml/ost-macdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saturdaynightuforia.com">[Saturday Night Uforia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saturdaynightuforia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on saturdaynightuforia.com.</span></span></span>

McDonald consequently disputed the meaning of Blue Book’s low percentage of unidentified reports. The Air Force’s final accounting recorded 12,618 sightings, of which 701 remained unidentified. Its official position was that investigations had produced no evidence that the unidentified cases represented technology beyond contemporary scientific knowledge or extraterrestrial vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

McDonald accepted neither the low unexplained percentage nor the inference commonly drawn from it. In his March 1968 paper he argued that a more thorough and open-minded reassessment of the roughly 12,000 cases then in the files might raise the unidentified proportion to **30–40 per cent**. That was his estimate, not a demonstrated reclassification of the archive, and it should therefore not be treated as an established statistic. What it reveals is his underlying methodological argument: the published unidentified rate depended partly on **how readily investigators accepted proposed conventional identifications**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/mcdonaldca.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: James E. McDonald at the Canadian Aeronautics and Space Institute Astronautics Symposium, Montreal, Canada, March 12...</span></span></span>

## Why McDonald thought the process was biased

McDonald connected individual questionable explanations to a broader institutional problem. In *UFOs — An International Scientific Problem* in March 1968, he explicitly distinguished this from a grand conspiracy. He said he saw no convincing evidence of a US conspiracy concealing the true nature of UFOs. Instead, he described superficial investigation, limited scientific expertise and, particularly after 1953, a strong negative bias towards “explaining away” the problem. He believed Air Force personnel largely **sincerely regarded UFOs as misidentified conventional phenomena**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.bluebookfiles.org/pdfs/unknown.00%20-%20NARA%20-%20FordUFOFiles%20-%20FordUFO.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.bluebookfiles.org">[Bluebook Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.bluebookfiles.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unknown.00 NARA Ford UFOFiles Ford UFO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bluebook FilesGERALD R. FORD LIBRARYApril 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2026</span></span></span>

That distinction makes his accusation more credible but also more limited. Institutional bias does not require officials secretly to know that their conclusions are false. It can arise when investigators begin with a strong expectation that every case has an ordinary explanation, devote fewer resources to anomalies, or accept weaker evidence for a preferred conventional hypothesis than they would demand for an extraordinary one.

There was historical material that gave McDonald a reason to suspect such pressures. After the CIA-sponsored Robertson Panel met in 1953, its recommendations included reducing the special status and public fascination surrounding UFO reports. The surviving history of the period confirms that officials were concerned not simply with identifying objects but with the potential national-security consequences of mass public interest and overloaded reporting channels. A historical CIA account also records that McDonald encountered the Robertson Panel material at Wright-Patterson in 1966 and subsequently pressed for its release.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/ciaufo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[FAS Project on Government Secrecy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FAS Project on Government SecrecyCIA&#x27;s Role in the Study of UFOs, 1947-90...</span></span></span>

McDonald particularly emphasised Air Force Regulation 200-2, which included language directing Air Force activities to reduce the percentage of unidentified cases to a minimum. He interpreted that instruction as creating the wrong incentive: the administrative objective should have been **accurate classification**, even if the resulting number of unknowns increased, rather than minimising the unknown category itself. His argument was that an institutional target of this kind could subtly turn “find out what happened” into “find a conventional label that closes the case”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saturdaynightuforia.com/html/articles/articlehtml/ost-macdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saturdaynightuforia.com">[Saturday Night Uforia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saturdaynightuforia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on saturdaynightuforia.com.</span></span></span>

There is, however, an important limit to that inference. A direction to reduce unidentified cases can also be read innocently as an instruction to improve investigation so that solvable cases do not remain unresolved. The wording alone does not demonstrate that personnel were ordered to fabricate explanations. McDonald’s stronger case therefore depends on the combination of that institutional orientation with the **specific case resolutions he believed failed quantitative or observational tests**.

## The criticism was not McDonald’s alone

McDonald’s charge gains historical significance because Blue Book’s own long-serving scientific consultant, astronomer J. Allen Hynek, eventually made criticisms that overlapped with it even though the two men did not agree on everything.

Hynek had worked with the Air Force UFO programmes for roughly two decades. By the late 1960s he was criticising Blue Book’s scientific resources and procedures, describing its staffing and scientific training as grossly inadequate and objecting to its limited engagement with the wider scientific community. Accounts of his October 1968 recommendations also record complaints that important cases did not necessarily reach him promptly and that too much effort was spent on routine reports rather than the comparatively small number of scientifically interesting cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/j-allen-hynek-ufos-project-blue-book" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j allen hynek ufos project blue book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meet J. Allen Hynek, the Astronomer Who First Classified UFO &#x27;Close Encounters&#x27; &#124; HISTORYNovember 19, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 19, 2018</span></span></span>

This convergence matters because it separates two questions that are often collapsed in later UFO literature. One is whether McDonald was right about the **nature or origin** of difficult UFO reports. He favoured the extraterrestrial hypothesis, while acknowledging that he did not possess decisive physical proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/shg/symposium/mcdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947SHG - UFO Symposium 1968: McDonald Statement...</span></span></span> The other is whether Blue Book was organised well enough to resolve difficult reports reliably. Criticism of the latter does not establish the former.

The National Archives’ description of Blue Book likewise makes clear that the programme used three broad outcomes after investigation: identified, insufficient data and unidentified. Identified causes included aircraft, balloons, satellites, astronomical objects, atmospheric effects and hoaxes. Such categories are perfectly reasonable in themselves. The methodological dispute is about the evidential standard used to place particular cases inside them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2018/04/16/ufos-natural-explanations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prologue.blogs.archives.gov">[Pieces of History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prologue.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pieces of History UFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pieces of History UFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of History</span></span></span>

## What biased case handling would mean for suppression claims


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

For claims that UFO researchers such as McDonald were being “suppressed”, the Blue Book material supports a narrower conclusion than stories of deliberate personal persecution or suspicious death sometimes imply.

</div>

The records and McDonald’s own statements provide substantial evidence for a **dispute over institutional method and public presentation**. He believed that weak investigation, low scientific priority, prior expectations and overconfident conventional explanations had made the UFO problem appear more completely solved than the underlying case files justified. The documented post-1953 concern with reducing public interest provides relevant historical context for that criticism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.bluebookfiles.org/pdfs/unknown.00%20-%20NARA%20-%20FordUFOFiles%20-%20FordUFO.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.bluebookfiles.org">[Bluebook Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.bluebookfiles.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unknown.00 NARA Ford UFOFiles Ford UFO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bluebook FilesGERALD R. FORD LIBRARYApril 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2026</span></span></span>

But methodological bias and personal suppression are different propositions. A programme can discourage inquiry without secretly threatening researchers: if questionable explanations are repeatedly presented as settled, unexplained reports appear scientifically uninteresting; witnesses become less willing to report them; funding becomes harder to justify; and scientists risk reputational damage by studying a subject officially portrayed as already resolved. McDonald himself repeatedly stressed ridicule and scientific neglect as important mechanisms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/shg/symposium/mcdonald.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947SHG - UFO Symposium 1968: McDonald Statement...</span></span></span>

Nothing in the Blue Book explanation dispute, by itself, demonstrates that officials targeted McDonald for physical harm or that his later death was connected to his UFO research. Indeed, his 1968 formulation explicitly rejected convincing evidence for a US conspiracy concealing the true nature of UFOs and instead blamed a more ordinary institutional mechanism: sincere scepticism reinforced by inadequate investigation and a strong preference for conventional resolutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.bluebookfiles.org/pdfs/unknown.00%20-%20NARA%20-%20FordUFOFiles%20-%20FordUFO.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.bluebookfiles.org">[Bluebook Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.bluebookfiles.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unknown.00 NARA Ford UFOFiles Ford UFO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bluebook FilesGERALD R. FORD LIBRARYApril 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2026</span></span></span>

That is why the most defensible reading of McDonald’s Blue Book critique is both less sensational and more interesting than a simple “cover-up” narrative. **His accusation was fundamentally about confirmation bias.** Blue Book’s conventional hypotheses were often reasonable starting points; McDonald argued that they too readily became finishing points. The surviving record establishes a serious historical argument over whether the Air Force’s case-resolution process was scientifically robust. It does not establish that the unexplained reports were extraterrestrial, nor does it turn institutional bias into evidence for claims of violent researcher suppression.
