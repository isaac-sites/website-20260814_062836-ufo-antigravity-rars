## Introduction

A pre-death message saying, in effect, “if my death is reported as suicide, do not believe it” is important evidence in a disputed [researcher death]({{ 'missing-denominator/' | relative_url }}). It records the person’s stated expectation before the fatal event, can establish that they claimed to fear outside harm, and may give investigators names, incidents or threats to check. But it is **not a forensic veto over a later suicide finding**. Manner of death is determined from the whole evidential picture: the scene, injuries, weapon or toxicology findings, digital evidence, witness accounts, medical and behavioural history, and evidence of another person’s involvement. Modern forensic guidance stresses precisely this multi-source approach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK620159/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strengthening the U.S. Medicolegal Death Investigation System - NCBI BookshelfDecember 15, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2025</span></span></span>

That distinction is especially relevant to the 2022 death of Huntsville propulsion researcher Amy Eskridge. Messages attributed to her and publicised later reportedly anticipated a false suicide report. They therefore justify scrutiny of the ruling. What they do not do, without corroborating forensic records, is establish that somebody murdered her and staged the scene.

## Why pre-death warnings deserve evidential attention

The strongest feature of a predicted suicide denial is its **timing**. A statement demonstrably created before death cannot simply be a reaction by relatives or supporters who dislike the official conclusion. If authenticated, it establishes that the deceased had expressed a particular [state of mind]({{ 'state-of-mind/' | relative_url }}) while alive.

Her contemporary obituary confirms the date and describes her as chairwoman and president of the Institute for Exotic Science, which she had co-founded. It does not describe the manner of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1987 - 2022) - Huntsville, AL - AL.com (Huntsville)June 21, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2022</span></span></span> Later reporting has consistently described the official finding as suicide by a self-inflicted gunshot wound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hindustan TimesAmy Eskridge update: UFO scientist&#x27;s old texts cast doubt on cause of death; ‘would never kill myself’ &#124; Hindustan TimesAp...</span></span></span>

The element that makes her case relevant to this narrower question is a message reportedly sent to former British military officer Franc Milburn on 13 May 2022, less than a month before her death. Published accounts say Eskridge explicitly denied that any future report of her killing herself or deliberately overdosing should be accepted as true.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[hindustantimes.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hindustan TimesAmy Eskridge update: UFO scientist&#x27;s old texts cast doubt on cause of death; ‘would never kill myself’ &#124; Hindustan TimesAp...</span></span></span>

That is more significant than a vague remark such as “people are after me”. It contains a prospective claim about a specific future interpretation of her death. Reporting also attributes to Eskridge broader allegations of harassment and physical attacks, while Milburn has said that he spoke to her only hours before her death and perceived nothing unusual in her manner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/amy-eskridge-death-investigation-1794631" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Times UK...</span></span></span>

For an investigator, such material creates legitimate questions rather than answers. Was the message genuinely written by Eskridge? Is its displayed date reliable? Does the complete conversation alter its meaning? Who possessed the original device or account? Had she made similar statements to other people independently? Most importantly, did she identify a particular threatening person or organisation whose movements and communications can be checked?

The evidential distinction is crucial. A warning that produces independently verifiable facts can become powerful corroborative evidence. A warning that merely predicts murder remains principally evidence of **what the deceased said and believed**.

## Why stated intent is not a forensic veto

The intuitive argument is simple: someone who says “I will not kill myself” shortly before dying cannot have died by suicide. Suicide research shows why that inference is unsafe.

A 2021 review in *General Hospital Psychiatry* examined 22 studies concerning disclosure and denial of suicidal ideation. It found that roughly half of suicide decedents had denied suicidal ideation during the preceding week or month. The authors concluded that denial of suicidal thoughts by itself is an inadequate indicator of suicide risk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0163834321001080" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Common is Recent Denial of Suicidal Ideation among Ideators, Attempters, and Suicide Decedents? A Literature Review - Sc...</span></span></span>

That finding does **not** explain Eskridge’s death, nor does it mean her warning should be disregarded. There is an important qualitative difference between answering “no” when asked about suicidal thoughts and spontaneously predicting that other people may falsely report one’s future death as suicide. But the research demonstrates the narrower forensic point: a living person’s stated lack of suicidal intent cannot establish what that person subsequently did.

Human intentions can change rapidly. People can conceal suicidal thinking, experience an acute crisis after making an earlier statement, or communicate differently to different people. Conversely, a person who genuinely fears being killed may accurately report a real threat. Investigators therefore cannot safely infer either suicide or homicide from stated intent alone.

Forensic practice reflects this uncertainty. A recent US National Academies assessment emphasises that determining cause and manner of death requires information from numerous sources and can demand extensive forensic analysis and [coordination]({{ 'coordination/' | relative_url }}) between pathologists, investigators, medical personnel and other specialists. It also stresses that “manner of death” is a classification of circumstances rather than a legal verdict, and notes that forensic pathologists can reach different manner determinations from the same difficult case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK620159/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strengthening the U.S. Medicolegal Death Investigation System - NCBI BookshelfDecember 15, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2025</span></span></span>

Research on suicide classification similarly shows that suicidal communications are only one category of evidence. In a study of 100 deaths classified as suicide and 22 classified as undetermined, investigators found that suicidal communication, particular causes of death and other circumstantial indications of intent collectively explained most classifications. In other words, communications matter because they join a larger evidential structure; they do not replace it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0379073802001883" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Suicide classification—clues and their use: A study of 122 cases of suicide and undetermined manner of death - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

The converse principle matters just as much. A conventional suicide note does not automatically prove suicide, and an anti-suicide declaration does not automatically prove homicide. Either document can be misunderstood, incompletely reproduced, falsely attributed, written well before a change in circumstances or contradicted by stronger physical evidence.

## The Eskridge warning leaves a forensic gap, not a murder finding

The public discussion of Eskridge illustrates the danger of allowing an unusually striking piece of evidence to outrun the underlying case file. Her reported warning is memorable precisely because her death was subsequently described as the event she had denied in advance: suicide. That coincidence understandably raises the evidential stakes.

But publicly accessible reporting does not provide the complete forensic record needed to test the [competing explanations]({{ 'competing-explanations/' | relative_url }}). Searches for the underlying records have repeatedly found extensive secondary reporting of the [suicide ruling]({{ 'suicide-ruling/' | relative_url }}) but no readily accessible comprehensive autopsy and police-investigation file from which outsiders could independently reconstruct the scene and ruling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://factually.co/fact-checks/justice/amy-eskridge-autopsy-police-report-how-to-obtain-7bb268" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: factually.co">[Factually]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">factually.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Is There an Official Autopsy or Police Report Availabl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Is There an Official Autopsy or Police Report Availabl...April 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 26, 2026</span></span></span>

That absence matters more than the volume of online discussion. To evaluate the warning against the suicide finding, investigators would ideally need evidence addressing questions such as:

* whether the reported messages can be authenticated from original devices, account records or recipients rather than screenshots alone;

* the exact location, trajectory and characteristics of the gunshot wound;
* firearm ownership, accessibility and positioning;
* fingerprints, DNA, gunshot residue and other relevant trace evidence, where collected and scientifically informative;
* the physical condition of the scene and whether there were indications of disturbance, restraint, entry by another person or staging;
* toxicology findings and relevant medical information;
* phone, computer, access-control, location and surveillance records establishing who was present or communicating with Eskridge;
* the complete chronology of her communications rather than selected extracts;
* documented reports of the threats or harassment she described and whether any identified suspect can be independently linked to them.

</div>

None of those categories automatically resolves a death. Their importance is cumulative. A pre-death denial becomes much more consequential if independent evidence points in the same direction.

The opposite is also true. If the physical scene strongly demonstrates self-infliction, no credible evidence places another person at the scene, authenticated digital evidence establishes the deceased’s actions immediately before death, and a complete behavioural history supplies additional evidence of suicidal intent, an earlier denial becomes substantially less persuasive as an explanation of the fatal event.

The appropriate description of the publicly visible Eskridge evidence is therefore **an unresolved discrepancy**: an official suicide finding reported by multiple outlets exists alongside a striking earlier statement attributed to Eskridge denying that interpretation in advance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hindustan TimesAmy Eskridge update: UFO scientist&#x27;s old texts cast doubt on cause of death; ‘would never kill myself’ &#124; Hindustan TimesAp...</span></span></span> The discrepancy warrants examination. It does not, by itself, resolve the discrepancy in favour of assassination.

## What corroboration would make the warning more probative

The value of a predicted suicide denial rises sharply when it moves from a statement of fear to a source of testable evidence. Three forms of corroboration are particularly important.

**First, authenticate the warning itself.** Investigators would want the original message data where possible, not merely reposted screenshots. The identity of the sender, timestamp, surrounding conversation and chain of custody all matter. If several recipients independently retained contemporaneous messages expressing the same warning, fabrication or retrospective reinterpretation would become harder to sustain.

**Second, test the alleged threat independently.** A message naming a person, vehicle, company, telephone number, encounter or earlier assault creates investigative leads. Records showing that an identified person actually contacted, followed or threatened the researcher would transform the evidential picture. By contrast, repeating an allegation across later websites does not constitute independent corroboration of the underlying event.

**Third, look for convergence with physical and digital evidence at death.** Evidence of another person's presence, unexplained injuries, scene inconsistencies, communications suggesting an arranged meeting, surveillance footage or forensic findings inconsistent with self-infliction would give an earlier warning much greater weight. Conversely, evidence independently reconstructing a self-inflicted death would weaken the assassination interpretation even if the earlier message were unquestionably authentic.

This is also where an **undetermined** manner of death has an important conceptual role. Forensic medicine does not require every ambiguous death to be forced into suicide or homicide. A *Journal of Forensic Sciences* study of 48 deaths classified as undetermined found that even after complete investigations, more than one manner remained conceivable in most cases; the cause of death itself was known in nearly half.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1556-4029.12924" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Undetermined Manner of Death: An Autopsy Series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online Library Undetermined Manner of Death: An Autopsy Series</span></span></span> Knowing *what physically killed somebody* and knowing *whose intentional action produced that death* are different questions.

That principle is especially useful in alleged researcher-assassination cases. A gunshot wound can establish a physical cause of death while leaving the manner dependent on scene reconstruction and circumstantial evidence. An authenticated warning can legitimately increase doubt about suicide without supplying the missing physical evidence necessary to establish homicide.

## The strongest conclusion the warning can support

Predicted suicide denials should neither be treated as conspiracy folklore nor promoted into proof of assassination. Their proper evidential position lies between those extremes.

In the Eskridge case, the reported May 2022 message is relevant because it apparently preceded her death and specifically anticipated a suicide or overdose account. Her reported allegations of harassment make the warning more contextually significant than an isolated declaration of good spirits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.strangerthanfiction.org/2026/04/amy-eskridge-scientist-predicted-own-death/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: strangerthanfiction.org">[Stranger Than Fiction]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">strangerthanfiction.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stranger Than Fiction Amy Eskridge: The Scientist Who Predicted Her Own Death</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stranger Than Fiction Amy Eskridge: The Scientist Who Predicted Her Own Death</span></span></span> At the same time, suicide research demonstrates that stated absence of suicidal intent is not a reliable forensic exclusion of later suicide, while medicolegal practice requires manner-of-death conclusions to integrate physical, circumstantial, medical and investigative evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0163834321001080" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Common is Recent Denial of Suicidal Ideation among Ideators, Attempters, and Suicide Decedents? For the wider question of suspicious deaths among UFO and [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers, this produces a useful evidential rule: **a pre-death prediction of a false suicide should trigger verification, not determine the verdict**. Its strongest function is to tell investigators where to look — at the authenticity of the communication, the alleged threats behind it and any independent evidence contradicting the death scene — rather than to tell them in advance what they must find.

Until those strands converge, “she said she would never commit suicide” is evidence against accepting a suicide narrative uncritically. It is not evidence strong enough, standing alone, to prove murder.
