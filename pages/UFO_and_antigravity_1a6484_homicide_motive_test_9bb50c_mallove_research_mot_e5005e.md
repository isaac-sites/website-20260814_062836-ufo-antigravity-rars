## Introduction

Eugene Mallove’s death is a confirmed homicide, but proving that he was killed **because of his [cold-fusion]({{ 'cold-fusion/' | relative_url }}) or unconventional-energy work** would require a second, separate chain of evidence. The decisive material would not be the controversial nature of his research, the brutality of the killing, or its timing. It would be evidence connecting the perpetrators or anyone directing them to Mallove’s scientific activity.

That evidence could take several forms: documented threats referring to his research; communications identifying his work as the reason to target him; payments or contacts linking the killers to an interested third party; surveillance focused on his laboratory, publications or collaborators; selective theft of research material; or credible admissions explaining a research-related motive. The presently available court record instead supplies a concrete link to Mallove’s rental property and former occupants, while records from the homicide investigation do not publicly establish an equivalent link to his scientific work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[justia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawBrown v. ## What would actually connect the killing to Mallove’s research?

A persuasive research-motive case would need to establish a **causal bridge** between Mallove’s science and the people responsible for his death. In practical terms, investigators would want evidence answering three questions: who had a research-related reason to stop Mallove; how did that person or organisation become connected to the killers; and what shows that this objective influenced the attack?

The strongest evidence would be material created **before the killing**, because it would be difficult to explain as a story invented afterwards. Examples would include:

* **Specific threats.** Emails, letters, telephone records, recorded conversations or witness testimony showing that Mallove was threatened over a publication, experiment, patent, demonstration, disclosure or collaboration. A warning such as “stop this research” followed by independently verified contact with an assailant would be far more significant than general [hostility]({{ 'hostility/' | relative_url }}) towards cold fusion.
* **Communications involving the perpetrators.** Messages, call records, meetings or other evidence showing that a killer had been instructed to target Mallove because of his scientific work. The evidential value would rise sharply if communications contained knowledge about Mallove’s projects that the killer would otherwise have had no reason to possess.
* **A financial trail.** An unexplained payment, debt cancellation, cash transfer or other benefit connecting an assailant with someone who had an identifiable interest in suppressing Mallove’s work could establish a commissioning mechanism. A payment alone would not prove motive, but a payment combined with communications and timing could.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Targeted surveillance or preparation.** Evidence that somebody had monitored Mallove’s laboratory, office, scientific meetings, collaborators or research schedule before the attack could distinguish deliberate scientific targeting from a spontaneous local confrontation.
* **Selective removal or destruction of scientific material.** Missing notebooks, experimental records, computers, unpublished manuscripts or technical data would matter especially if ordinary valuables were left behind, or if the missing research later appeared in the possession of somebody connected to the perpetrators.
* **Admissions supported by independent evidence.** A perpetrator, organiser or knowledgeable accomplice explicitly stating that Mallove was targeted because of his research could be powerful evidence, particularly if the statement contained details that were not public and could be corroborated.

</div>

No single category is automatically conclusive. A threat might be unrelated to the eventual murderer; missing research could have an innocent explanation; an informant could lie. The compelling case would therefore involve **convergence**: independent evidence pointing from Mallove’s scientific activity to a person with a motive, from that person to the perpetrators, and from the perpetrators to the homicide.

## Why timing and occupation cannot carry the argument

Mallove unquestionably had a scientific career capable of generating controversy. He was an outspoken advocate of cold fusion, publisher of *Infinite Energy* and supporter of unconventional-energy research. Shortly before his death, the US Department of Energy agreed to reconsider cold-fusion evidence. Contemporary reporting quoted Mallove welcoming the development enthusiastically and saying that a major new window had opened in physics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asa3.org/archive/asa/200403/0244.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asa3.org">[American Scientific Affiliation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asa3.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Scientific Affiliation ASA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Scientific AffiliationASA - March 2004: Credibility for cold fusion?March 25, 2004...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 25, 2004</span></span></span>

People close to his research also regarded his death as a major setback. Statements subsequently collected on a Mallove family-associated investigation page emphasised how damaging his loss was to the new-energy community and drew attention to the timing around renewed consideration of cold fusion. Those statements establish something important about **Mallove’s significance to that community**, but they do not identify a scientific adversary who arranged his death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eugenemallove.com/em_investigation.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eugenemallove.com">[eugenemallove.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eugenemallove.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Press Releases on Mallove Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 24, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 24, 2005</span></span></span>

This is the central distinction. “Mallove was doing potentially disruptive work when he was murdered” establishes temporal proximity. It does not establish the mechanism required for a suppression killing.

Some later alternative-energy accounts go further, portraying his death against claims that revolutionary energy technologies face organised suppression. Such material is useful for understanding why the research-motive theory arose, but the claim itself cannot substitute for evidence connecting a suspected suppressor to the Norwich crime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projectcamelot.org/mallove.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projectcamelot.org">[projectcamelot.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projectcamelot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Camelot &#124; In Tribute &#124; Eugene Mallove</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Camelot &#124; In Tribute &#124; Eugene Mallove</span></span></span>

The same problem applies to the argument from brutality. The violence may indicate anger, determination or a personal confrontation, but the injuries themselves cannot tell investigators *what subject generated that anger*. A murder can be exceptionally violent without being professionally motivated.

## The crime scene points to a different evidential trail

The most important comparison is between the evidence a research-motive hypothesis would require and the evidence that actually emerged in the criminal investigation.

Mallove was killed at 119 Salem Turnpike in Norwich, Connecticut, a rental property where he had recently evicted tenants and which he had travelled from New Hampshire to clean. The 2025 Connecticut Appellate Court decision in *Brown v Commissioner of Correction* records that former tenants Roy and Patricia Anderson had been evicted the previous month. Patricia Anderson’s son, Chad Schaffer, and his girlfriend, Candace Foster, had also lived there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawBrown v. Commissioner of Correction:: 2025:: Connecticut Appellate Court Decisions:: Connecticut Case Law:: Connecticut Law...</span></span></span>

That is significant because it supplies something the research theory presently lacks: a documented, pre-existing connection between people implicated in the homicide and the **specific place and circumstances in which Mallove was killed**.

Later witnesses strengthened that connection. According to the appellate record, Jill Sebastian told investigators that she had seen Schaffer’s bloody clothing and that Foster had admitted lying about Schaffer’s whereabouts on the night of the murder. Another witness, Keishon Dullivan, told police that Schaffer said he had assaulted Mallove with Mozzelle Brown at the Salem Turnpike property and that Mallove’s possessions were taken to stage the attack as a robbery. Foster eventually admitted involvement and said that Mallove’s van was driven to Foxwoods while Schaffer took possessions from him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawBrown v. Commissioner of Correction:: 2025:: Connecticut Appellate Court Decisions:: Connecticut Case Law:: Connecticut Law...</span></span></span>

Contemporary police appeals are consistent with theft forming part of the crime scene. Investigators sought Mallove’s missing wallet, mobile telephone, digital camera and wedding ring, while his stolen van was recovered at Foxwoods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eugenemallove.com/em_investigation.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eugenemallove.com">[eugenemallove.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eugenemallove.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Press Releases on Mallove Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 24, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 24, 2005</span></span></span>

This matters for the research-motive question because the publicly identified missing property was largely **ordinary personal property**, not a conspicuous cache of cold-fusion notebooks, laboratory records or unpublished technical material. That does not logically rule out another motive, but it means the known theft evidence does not itself supply the research connection that a suppression theory needs.

## Staging a robbery is suspicious, but not proof of a scientific conspiracy

One potentially confusing feature deserves particular attention. Court-related reporting said Foster described the attackers returning to make the incident look like a robbery. A contemporary new-energy publication highlighted the same issue and questioned why Schaffer supposedly believed the scene needed to be staged that way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://newenergytimes.com/v2/news/2010/35/3524scienceenergynews.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newenergytimes.com">[New Energy Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newenergytimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New Energy TimesNew Energy Times - Issue #35: Science and Energy NewsJuly 30, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2010</span></span></span>

That is a legitimate evidential question. Staging can show consciousness of guilt and an attempt to conceal the true circumstances of a crime. But it does **not identify the concealed motive by itself**.

For staging to support a research-suppression theory specifically, investigators would need the next link: evidence that the reason for disguising the killing was to conceal its connection to Mallove’s scientific work. Without that link, staging is compatible with numerous alternatives — including hiding the identities of people already connected to the property or making a personal confrontation appear to have been committed by an unknown robber.

This illustrates why circumstantial anomalies need to be handled carefully. An unexplained feature can justify further investigation without proving whichever explanation appears most dramatic.

## The investigation itself looked beyond the first suspects

The [Mallove case]({{ 'mallove-case/' | relative_url }}) also demonstrates why uncertainty about one prosecution should not automatically be converted into evidence for a different motive. Early investigators arrested Gary McAvoy and Joseph Reilly, but subsequent investigation excluded them as sources of DNA profiles relevant to Mallove’s death, and those charges were abandoned. Years later, information from new witnesses redirected investigators towards Schaffer, Foster and Brown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawBrown v. Commissioner of Correction:: 2025:: Connecticut Appellate Court Decisions:: Connecticut Case Law:: Connecticut Law...</span></span></span>

The authorities were still actively pursuing the cold case in 2009. Connecticut State Police announced a $50,000 reward and said investigators had developed some credible information but needed additional facts. FBI records show that Norwich police supplied the FBI’s National Center for the Analysis of Violent Crime with the entire Mallove case file in July 2009; the record says new suspects had emerged and additional forensic testing was anticipated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.ct.gov/despp/press-releases/2009/50000-reward-being-offered-from-homicide-investigation-norwich-2004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.ct.gov">[CT.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.ct.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">50000 reward being offered from homicide investigation norwich 2004</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CT.gov50000 Reward Being Offered from Homicide Investigation Norwich 2004...</span></span></span>

That history is important because it demonstrates genuine investigative uncertainty without establishing a research motive. A wrongful lead, weak prosecution or later challenge to a conviction can undermine confidence in a particular account of **who committed a crime**. It does not automatically supply affirmative evidence of **why the crime occurred**.

That distinction has become especially relevant because Brown’s case later developed a serious legal problem. In January 2025, the Connecticut Appellate Court held that prosecutors had failed to disclose material information concerning an understanding with cooperating witness Foster. The court ordered Brown’s murder and conspiracy convictions vacated and directed that he receive a new trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawBrown v. Commissioner of Correction:: 2025:: Connecticut Appellate Court Decisions:: Connecticut Case Law:: Connecticut Law...</span></span></span>

The ruling therefore matters to the reliability of Brown’s conviction and Foster’s role as a witness. It does not, however, identify cold fusion, unconventional energy, a government agency, an energy company or any other research-related actor as the motive for Mallove’s killing.

## What discovery would change the assessment?

Because motive is inferential, new evidence could change the picture substantially. The most consequential discovery would be material that joins the presently separate worlds of Mallove’s scientific work and his homicide.

Imagine, for example, that authenticated correspondence showed Mallove telling several people that an identifiable person had threatened him over a forthcoming energy demonstration. Telephone records then showed that person communicating with one of the killers immediately before and after the murder, while financial records revealed an unexplained payment. If the killer subsequently described the same scientific objective in a private communication, the research-motive hypothesis would become much stronger because several independent evidential chains would converge.

Likewise, evidence that perpetrators deliberately searched for specific research records could transform the interpretation of the crime scene. If investigators established that scientific notebooks or experimental data disappeared while readily saleable property was ignored, and those materials could then be traced to someone with a reason to suppress the work, the theft would acquire a very different meaning.

Conversely, evidence merely showing that Mallove expected breakthroughs, criticised scientific institutions or had enemies within intellectual disputes would remain weak on motive. Those facts establish potential reasons somebody *might* have disliked his activities. Criminal attribution requires showing that such hostility travelled from an identifiable actor through an identifiable mechanism to the people who killed him.

## How the Mallove record compares with that threshold

The surviving public record is asymmetric. On the **property-related side**, there is a location-specific relationship between Mallove and former occupants, witness accounts connecting implicated people to the scene and its aftermath, admissions described in court records, removal of personal possessions, and the movement of Mallove’s van.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/court-of-appeals/2025/ac46658.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawBrown v. Commissioner of Correction:: 2025:: Connecticut Appellate Court Decisions:: Connecticut Case Law:: Connecticut Law...</span></span></span>

On the **research-suppression side**, there is strong evidence that Mallove was deeply involved in controversial unconventional-energy work, that he believed cold fusion had been unfairly marginalised, that renewed official attention to cold fusion was occurring shortly before his death, and that colleagues considered his loss exceptionally damaging to their field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asa3.org/archive/asa/200403/0244.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asa3.org">[American Scientific Affiliation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asa3.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Scientific Affiliation ASA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Scientific AffiliationASA - March 2004: Credibility for cold fusion?March 25, 2004...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 25, 2004</span></span></span> What is missing from the publicly documented case is the crucial connecting mechanism: a threat tied to the murder, an intermediary, a payment, operational communications, research-focused surveillance, selective theft of scientific material, or a corroborated admission that Mallove was targeted because of his work.

That absence does not prove that a research motive was impossible. It sets the evidential status of the claim. Mallove’s homicide is established; aspects of responsibility have undergone substantial legal dispute; his importance to unconventional-energy research is established; but the proposition that he was murdered **to suppress that research** remains unproven.

For claims about suspicious deaths among UFO, [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) or unconventional-energy researchers more broadly, Mallove therefore provides a useful standard. The meaningful question is not whether a victim worked on controversial science shortly before dying. It is whether evidence can trace a continuous path from **the research, to a threatened interest, to the perpetrators, to the act itself**. Until that path exists, homicide and research-suppression motive remain two different propositions.
