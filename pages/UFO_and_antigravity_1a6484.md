## Introduction

Claims that UFO and [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers have been murdered, driven to suicide or made to disappear have circulated for decades. The historical record does contain several striking deaths and disappearances involving people associated with UFO investigation, unconventional propulsion or gravity-modification research. What it does **not** presently establish is a single, coordinated programme of eliminating such researchers.

The cases vary sharply. Atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald, one of the most scientifically credentialled UFO investigators of the 1960s, died by suicide in 1971 after an earlier suicide attempt. Physicist Ning Li genuinely withdrew from public view after pursuing controversial superconducting gravity research, but later reporting based on her family found that she had not vanished into a secret programme: she suffered severe injuries after being struck by a vehicle and died in 2021. Eugene Mallove was unquestionably murdered, but criminal proceedings tied his killing to a dispute surrounding a rental property rather than his unconventional-energy work. Amy Eskridge's 2022 death is more difficult to assess publicly because comparatively little official documentation has been released and allegations that she feared for her safety resurfaced in 2026.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/obit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Huntsville Business Journal...</span></span></span>

The most useful way to approach the subject, therefore, is not to start with a supposed "death list". It is to examine what each person actually researched, what is reliably known about the death or disappearance, where the suspicious interpretation originated and whether independent evidence supports a connection.

## Four cases at the centre of the story

### James E. McDonald: a real UFO scientist, but weak evidence of foul play

James Edward McDonald is unusually important because there is no serious dispute over either his scientific [credentials]({{ 'credentials/' | relative_url }}) or his sustained UFO research. He was an atmospheric physicist and professor at the University of Arizona whose conventional work included cloud physics, weather modification and atmospheric processes. During the 1960s he also became one of America's most prominent advocates for rigorous scientific investigation of unidentified flying objects. His surviving papers include correspondence, interviews, UFO reports and extensive [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) material. An American Meteorological Society obituary confirms his academic career and his death on 13 June 1971.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/obit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legacy.com+3files.ncas.org+3Huntsville Business Journal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Huntsville Business Journal...</span></span></span>

Later conspiracy accounts have therefore suggested that his death removed an inconvenient scientific voice.

The documented circumstances, however, strongly complicate that interpretation. Historical accounts record that McDonald had already made a serious suicide attempt in May 1971, leaving him temporarily blinded. There is nevertheless a legitimate historical question underneath the conspiracy claim. McDonald encountered resistance and institutional hostility while investigating UFOs, and his archive documents his disputes over governmental handling of the subject. Institutional obstruction, professional stigma and homicide are not the same allegation, however. Evidence for the first does not automatically support the second.

### Ning Li: the famous "disappearance" that was not a disappearance

Ning Li became one of the most frequently cited names in antigravity lore because her scientific work was real and unusually provocative. Working at the University of Alabama in Huntsville with Douglas Torr, she explored possible gravitational effects associated with superconductors. A 1992 paper in the American Physical Society's *Physical Review B* examined gravitational effects in the magnetic behaviour of superconductors, placing at least part of the underlying research firmly in the peer-reviewed scientific record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.aps.org/prb/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevB.46.5489" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.aps.org">[Physical Review Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.aps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ning Li became one of the most frequently cited names in antigravity lore because her scientific work was real and unusually provocative...</span></span></span>

Li subsequently pursued more ambitious ideas involving high-temperature superconductors and possible gravity-modification effects. She left the university environment and continued work through AC Gravity LLC. Public information then became sparse. Over time, the absence of publications and interviews evolved online into a much stronger claim: Li had supposedly "disappeared" after discovering antigravity, perhaps into a classified US programme or after being removed by some interested party.

Reporting in Huntsville eventually supplied a much less exotic explanation. Journalist Noah Logan contacted Li's son, George Men, who said his mother had been struck by a vehicle while crossing the University of Alabama in Huntsville campus in 2014. According to that account, she suffered lasting brain damage and required care for the remaining years of her life. Her obituary records that she died peacefully on 27 July 2021, aged 79.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntsvillebusinessjournal.com/news/2023/07/30/solving-the-mystery-of-huntsvilles-brilliant-scientist-disappearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvillebusinessjournal.com">[Huntsville Business Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvillebusinessjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reporting in Huntsville eventually supplied a much less exotic explanation. Journalist Noah Logan contacted Li&#x27;s son, George Men, who sai...</span></span></span>

This case is especially instructive because the mystery was partly manufactured by an [information vacuum]({{ 'info-vacuums/' | relative_url }}). Li really did stop appearing publicly. Her research really was unusual. Huntsville really is a major US aerospace and defence centre. Those facts were enough for speculation to fill the intervening years. Once a family member supplied a mundane but tragic account, the original "missing antigravity scientist" narrative largely collapsed.

Nothing in the publicly available evidence proves that Li's early gravity research was correct, either. Her published physics and subsequent antigravity claims should not be conflated: establishing that a physicist conducted legitimate research involving gravitational phenomena is different from demonstrating a working gravity-cancellation technology.

## Older UFO deaths became templates for later conspiracy claims

The idea that unconventional researchers are deliberately silenced predates today's antigravity discussion. Morris K. Jessup and James McDonald became two early archetypes: intellectually serious men associated with UFO investigation whose deaths were subsequently reinterpreted through the mysteries they had studied.

Jessup was an author with a background in astronomy whose 1955 book *The Case for the UFO* discussed advanced propulsion and gravitational concepts. He also became entangled — largely through correspondence sent to him and an annotated edition of his book — with what later became known as the Philadelphia Experiment legend. His 1959 death from carbon-monoxide poisoning was ruled a suicide. UFO literature subsequently transformed the combination of his unusual interests, Navy contacts and death into suspicions that he had learned something dangerous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufouap.com/case-files/morris-jessup/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufouap.com">[UFOUAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufouap.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">morris jessup</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jessup was an author with a background in astronomy whose 1955 book The Case for the UFO discussed advanced propulsion and gravitational...</span></span></span>

The pattern is worth noticing because it recurs. First comes unusual research. Then some contact with military, government or aerospace organisations. If the individual subsequently dies young, withdraws from public view or experiences personal difficulties, the normal gaps in the historical record become interpreted as evidence of suppression.

That reasoning has a built-in weakness: the same absence of information is used both to create the mystery and to protect it from disproof. If records are available, unusual details are interpreted as evidence. If records are unavailable, their absence becomes evidence of secrecy. Such a structure can produce a compelling story without ever producing a falsifiable case.

## Why apparently compelling "researcher death lists" are difficult to interpret

A list of names can look statistically extraordinary without having been assembled by a statistically valid method. The central problem is selection after the fact. There is no fixed, independently defined population called "UFO and antigravity scientists" against which a mortality or disappearance rate can readily be calculated. Researchers can be added because they worked on UFOs, gravity, propulsion, nuclear science, energy, aerospace, classified programmes or simply an institution that once handled such work.

Once the category expands that far, millions of scientists, engineers, military personnel and technical workers become possible candidates. A death list can then select only the unusual outcomes while ignoring the overwhelming majority who retire, change fields, continue publishing or die from ordinary causes. The Guardian's examination of the 2026 controversy highlighted precisely this denominator problem, noting that striking clusters can emerge when observers begin searching retrospectively through a very large population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/apr/25/conspiracy-theory-ufo-scientists-white-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian+2Vanity Fair</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">That distinction became blurred in online retellings. Three recurring errors matter especially:

* **Research adjacency becomes research identity.** Someone who worked in aerospace, nuclear technology or a classified programme may be described later as an "UFO scientist" even when there is no evidence that UFOs formed part of his or her professional research.

## What would constitute stronger evidence of deliberate suppression?

A credible case for systematic targeting would need more than a sequence of unusual deaths. Investigators would look for independent links between cases: the same organisation, intermediary, threat source, surveillance activity, financial trail, classified programme, method of attack or documentary record.

For an individual case such as Eskridge's, particularly valuable evidence would include contemporaneous police and medical-examiner records, authenticated communications predating the death, documentation of reported threats, corroborating witnesses and evidence showing that an alleged threat concerned specific research rather than an unrelated personal issue. ## The strongest conclusion the record currently supports

There is a genuine historical intersection between UFO investigation, unconventional propulsion research, secrecy and several troubling personal outcomes. Some of the people involved were legitimate scientists rather than merely paranormal personalities. Some worked in areas close enough to defence and aerospace research that secrecy was unsurprising. And a small number of cases contain unresolved public-information gaps that warrant careful examination.

But the individual cases do not presently add up to demonstrated systematic assassination or disappearance.

James McDonald's death is strongly contextualised by a documented prior suicide attempt. Ning Li, perhaps the most famous "vanished antigravity scientist", was located through her family history and had suffered catastrophic injuries rather than disappearing into a clandestine programme. Eugene Mallove was murdered, but the resulting criminal prosecutions point towards a property dispute rather than scientific suppression.
