## Introduction

A death classified as suicide can leave a surprisingly thin public record even when police, coroners and forensic specialists have done substantial investigative work. The reason is procedural rather than mysterious: if investigators conclude that no other person committed a crime, there may be no arrest, indictment, prosecution or trial. That removes the stages of the justice system most likely to put witness testimony, expert opinions, forensic disputes and exhibits into an open courtroom. A medicolegal finding of suicide is itself an official determination, but it is not the equivalent of a judicial verdict after adversarial testing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-guide/alabama/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcfp.org">[rcfp.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcfp.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reporters Committee Open Government Guide Alabama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reporters CommitteeOpen Government Guide Alabama - Reporters CommitteeJuly 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2025</span></span></span>

That distinction is particularly important when assessing disputed deaths associated with UFO or unconventional-propulsion research. A sparse court record does not make allegations of murder or suppression more credible. Equally, the absence of a trial record should not be mistaken for evidence that every underlying investigative detail has been publicly examined. The two questions — what authorities concluded and what evidence outsiders can independently inspect — are different.

## How prosecutions create public records

Criminal prosecution acts as a powerful mechanism for moving information from investigative files into a more visible legal setting. Once someone is charged, the prosecution and defence must prepare competing accounts of what happened. That process can bring forensic reports, scientific testing, photographs, physical evidence and expert analysis into formal proceedings.

The federal system illustrates the mechanism clearly. Rule 16 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires disclosure to the defence, subject to important limits, of categories including documents and objects, reports of physical or mental examinations, and scientific tests or experiments that meet the rule's requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/html/uscode18a/usc_sec_18a_03000016----000-_combined.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornell.edu.</span></span></span> Discovery is not synonymous with public disclosure — material exchanged between lawyers does not automatically become accessible to everyone — but prosecution creates pathways by which important evidence may later appear in motions, hearings, testimony or exhibits.

Trial adds another layer of transparency. The US Supreme Court held in *Richmond Newspapers v Virginia* that the public and press have a constitutional right to attend criminal trials, subject to overriding interests that can justify closure. The Court subsequently recognised a qualified First Amendment right of access to certain preliminary criminal hearings as well. Federal criminal rules also ordinarily require trial witnesses to give their testimony in open court.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/448/555/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: supreme.justia.com">[justia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">supreme.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia LawRichmond Newspapers, Inc. v. Virginia &#124; 448 U.S. 555 (1980) &#124; Justia U.S. Supreme Court CenterJuly 2, 1980...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1980</span></span></span>

Court filings themselves can provide a documentary trail. Federal courts make many documents filed in criminal cases publicly accessible, although privacy rules, sealing orders and restrictions on particular criminal records remain important exceptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scd.uscourts.gov/filing/publicaccess.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scd.uscourts.gov">[Southern District Court CA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scd.uscourts.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Southern District Court CASCD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Public Access to Documents in Electronic Case Files...</span></span></span> Alabama likewise operates Alacourt ACCESS, through which the public can obtain information and documents from state trial-court cases, including criminal cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pa.alacourt.com/default.aspx?loc=alacourt.gov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pa.alacourt.com">[Alacourt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pa.alacourt.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ACCESS V2.0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ACCESS V2.0</span></span></span>

The result is cumulative. A prosecuted homicide can generate an indictment, motions challenging evidence, expert reports discussed in filings, hearings, sworn testimony, trial exhibits, rulings, a verdict and sometimes an appellate opinion. Conflicting interpretations may be questioned in public by opposing lawyers. None of that guarantees that every investigative fact becomes public, but it can leave researchers with several independent records against which claims can be checked.

## Why suicide cases often stop short of court

A suicide determination belongs first to the medicolegal death-investigation system, not to the criminal trial process. The National Institute of Standards and Technology describes manner of death as a classification based on the circumstances and postmortem findings known to the death-investigation authority, usually using the categories accident, homicide, natural, suicide and undetermined. Crucially, it says the classification does **not** imply a legal or judicial conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/43646" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">manner of death &#124; NIST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">manner of death &#124; NIST...</span></span></span>

A recent National Academies review makes the same distinction: manner of death was developed for death certification and vital statistics and is not itself a legal conclusion, even though coroners and medical examiners may later testify about it in court. The review also notes that forensic practitioners can disagree over manner-of-death classifications in particular cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK620159/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strengthening the U.S. Medicolegal Death Investigation System - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

That means a finding of suicide can effectively end the route towards a homicide prosecution when investigators find no evidence sufficient to implicate another person. There is then no defendant whose liberty is at stake, no criminal case requiring the prosecution to prove homicide beyond reasonable doubt, and normally no defence lawyer with an incentive and procedural opportunity to challenge the state's interpretation before a judge or jury.

This should not be confused with an absence of investigation. Coroners and medical examiners investigate deaths specifically to determine their circumstances, and the precise structure varies considerably among US states. Alabama uses a county-based coroner system, while the Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences (ADFS) supplies forensic pathology and investigative services to law-enforcement agencies. ADFS says its death-investigation discipline performs examinations, provides consultations and can give testimony about its findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/phlp/php/coroner/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdc.gov.</span></span></span>

The difference lies in what happens **after** that work. If no prosecution follows, the investigative evidence does not receive the additional exposure produced by criminal litigation. Witnesses may have given detailed statements without ever testifying publicly. Detectives may have considered alternative explanations without writing a public-facing account of each one. Laboratory findings may exist without becoming trial exhibits. The evidential record can therefore be much richer inside official files than the searchable public record suggests.

## Investigative files are not the same as court files

Alabama provides a particularly useful example because different categories of records receive markedly different treatment. State law says that law-enforcement investigative reports and related materials — expressly including field notes and witness statements — are not public records and are privileged from disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-12/chapter-21/article-1/division-1/division-1/section-12-21-3-1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on justia.com.</span></span></span> That matters whenever later controversy centres on questions such as what witnesses told detectives, what investigators observed at the scene or which alternative scenarios police considered.

ADFS records operate under a different framework. The department says final case reports from its investigations are public records and provides a procedure for requesting them. It also states that a certified report will not be issued until the ADFS case is complete, while reports connected with a pending criminal investigation can remain unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adfs.alabama.gov/resources/attorneycorner/public-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adfs.alabama.gov">[Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adfs.alabama.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Forensic Sciences Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of Forensic Sciences Alabama Department of Forensic Sciences</span></span></span> The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press likewise identifies Alabama forensic autopsy reports as generally public, while noting restrictions connected with pending investigations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-guide/alabama/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcfp.org">[Reporters Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcfp.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reporters Committee Open Government Guide Alabama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reporters CommitteeOpen Government Guide Alabama - Reporters CommitteeJuly 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2025</span></span></span>

These distinctions help explain an apparent paradox. A suicide case may have an autopsy or final forensic report that is legally obtainable while still lacking public access to the much broader police file surrounding it. Conversely, difficulty locating a report on the internet does not establish that the report does not exist or is legally secret. “Not online”, “not publicly obtainable” and “never created” are three different propositions.

Nor would prosecution automatically expose everything. Courts can seal sensitive material; privacy protections can require redaction; criminal records involving victims, medical information, national-security information or other sensitive subjects may receive special treatment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scd.uscourts.gov/filing/publicaccess.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scd.uscourts.gov">[Southern District Court CA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scd.uscourts.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Southern District Court CASCD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Public Access to Documents in Electronic Case Files...</span></span></span> Grand-jury proceedings are another major exception to courtroom openness: Alabama law expressly provides for their secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-12/chapter-16/article-8/division-2/section-12-16-214/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on justia.com.</span></span></span> The meaningful comparison is therefore not “secret suicide investigation versus completely transparent murder trial”, but a generally thinner administrative-investigative record versus the additional public-record opportunities created when criminal litigation occurs.

## Amy Eskridge shows why the distinction matters

The death of [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researcher Amy Eskridge in Huntsville in June 2022 has become a prominent example of this problem. Recent reporting describes her death as officially ruled a suicide involving a self-inflicted gunshot wound, while controversy has grown around claims attributed to her before her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/amy-eskridge-update-ufo-scientists-old-texts-cast-doubt-on-cause-of-death-would-never-kill-myself-101776897906208.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hindustantimes.com">[Hindustan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hindustantimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hindustantimes.com.</span></span></span> Those later claims can make the underlying investigation of intense public interest, but they do not themselves establish that the suicide determination was wrong.

The important point for evaluating the controversy is narrower: there was no homicide prosecution through which a competing theory of Eskridge's death had to be proved in open court. Consequently, a researcher cannot expect to find the documentary footprint that would accompany such a case — a murder indictment, prosecution and defence motions, publicly examined homicide witnesses, competing forensic experts, trial exhibits and a verdict addressing criminal responsibility.

That absence should be interpreted symmetrically. It is **not evidence that homicide occurred**; the straightforward explanation is that authorities classified the death as suicide and no homicide prosecution followed. But the lack of criminal proceedings also means there is no trial transcript in which the evidence supporting that conclusion was subjected to cross-examination. For readers trying to distinguish documented fact from later UFO-related speculation, this is an important limitation on what the public record can establish by itself.

The most useful evidence therefore comes from the records actually produced by the death investigation: forensic reports, death certification, incident information and whatever other official material is lawfully releasable. Their evidential value should be assessed individually rather than treating the absence of a court case as either proof of a conspiracy or proof that every disputed question has already been publicly resolved.

## How the record gap fuels later controversy

Thin records create an asymmetry that becomes especially visible years later. A criminal trial constructs a narrative under institutional pressure: prosecutors must support charges, defence lawyers can attack weaknesses, witnesses can be questioned and judges rule on contested evidence. A closed suicide investigation may reach a firm official conclusion without ever generating that public adversarial narrative.

That leaves later researchers assembling events from fragments. An obituary may establish a date of death but say nothing about manner. A news report may state the official conclusion without reproducing the forensic basis for it. Friends may offer recollections that were never tested under oath. Social-media posts may then place all three on the same apparent evidential level even though they are not equivalent.

The danger runs in both directions. Suspicion can fill documentary gaps with unsupported theories, particularly when a deceased researcher worked in an unusual or secrecy-associated field. But defenders of an official conclusion can also overstate what the accessible record demonstrates. An official suicide classification is meaningful evidence from the authority charged with investigating the death; it is not, simply by virtue of that label, a public trial in miniature. Manner-of-death classifications are medicolegal determinations rather than judicial findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK620159/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strengthening the U.S. Medicolegal Death Investigation System - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

For the broader history of allegedly suspicious deaths and disappearances connected with [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) research, this mechanism explains why some cases remain unusually resistant to independent reconstruction. The decisive question is not merely whether a death was called suicide. It is **which underlying records survive, which are legally accessible, and which facts ever entered a forum where [competing explanations]({{ 'competing-explanations/' | relative_url }}) could be publicly tested**. Where no prosecution or trial occurred, the answer may be: considerably fewer than readers expect.
