## Introduction

For much of the post-war period, a scientist who treated UFO reports as a legitimate research problem risked something more ordinary, and more demonstrable, than secret physical retaliation: ridicule, reputational damage, difficulty attracting institutional support and association with a subject widely regarded as pseudoscientific. Atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald is the clearest historical example. He remained a serious scientist with recognised [credentials]({{ 'credentials/' | relative_url }}) while publicly arguing that some UFO reports deserved systematic investigation, yet he worked in an intellectual climate increasingly hostile to that proposition. The 1969 [Condon Report]({{ 'condon-report/' | relative_url }}) helped reinforce the view that further UFO research was unlikely to advance science, while recent NASA and academic studies still identify stigma as an obstacle to reporting and research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/mccarthy/obit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Mc Carthy Dissertation: James E. ## McDonald was not an outsider to science

James Edward McDonald is useful precisely because he cannot easily be dismissed as someone whose scientific identity was created by UFO research. His career pre-dated his public campaign on the subject. He held a master's degree from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and a doctorate in physics from Iowa State University, served as a naval aerologist during the Second World War and joined the University of Arizona's Institute of Atmospheric Physics in 1954. At his death in 1971 he was a professor of atmospheric sciences and a Fellow of the American Meteorological Society. That background gave McDonald's UFO advocacy unusual significance. He was not merely asking scientists to accept stories of extraordinary craft. His recurring argument was that there existed a residue of reports, sometimes involving trained observers, radar or multiple witnesses, that had not received investigation commensurate with their potential scientific importance. By the late 1960s he was giving technical presentations, examining historical case files and challenging the adequacy of official investigations. He addressed a congressional symposium in 1968 and, in December 1969, presented "Science in Default: Twenty-Two Years of Inadequate UFO Investigations" at an American Association for the Advancement of Science symposium.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isaackoi.com/ufo-history/ufo/19691226-aaas-ufo-symposium/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isaackoi.com">[Isaac Koi Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isaackoi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">19691226 aaas ufo symposium</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isaac Koi Archive1969.1226 AAAS UFO Symposium &#124; Isaac Koi ArchiveDecember 26, 1969...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 26, 1969</span></span></span>

McDonald therefore illustrates a particularly important kind of professional risk. A credentialled researcher did not have to lose his qualifications or formal position to become identified, in the eyes of colleagues and the public, with a subject carrying a heavy reputational burden. Once that identification occurred, arguments about individual cases could easily become arguments about the researcher's judgement.

## How UFO inquiry acquired an institutional handicap

The stigma surrounding UFO research was not generated by one decision. It accumulated through the interaction of poor-quality reports, hoaxes, popular entertainment, sensational claims, official scepticism and a scientific incentive structure in which the subject offered substantial reputational risk with uncertain academic reward.

Government-sponsored assessments contributed to that environment. The most consequential was the University of Colorado study directed by physicist Edward Condon and published in 1969. Condon's conclusion was emphatic: the previous 21 years of UFO study had produced nothing that added to scientific knowledge, and further extensive investigation probably could not be justified in the expectation of scientific advancement. He suggested that scientists who accepted the report's conclusions would direct their talents elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-i.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Section I: Conclusions &amp; Recommendations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Section I: Conclusions &amp; Recommendations...</span></span></span>

That was more than a finding about particular sightings. Coming from a university project funded by the US Air Force and then accepted by much of the mainstream scientific establishment, it supplied an authoritative reason for laboratories, departments and funders not to make UFOs a research priority.

McDonald disputed the report vigorously. His objection was not simply that Condon had failed to endorse an extraterrestrial explanation. He argued that the project had not adequately examined the strongest cases and that its broad recommendation against further research was not justified by the material presented. Contemporary scientific criticism was not limited to McDonald: a 1969 *Physics Today* article by Gerald Rothberg likewise addressed what its title called "Where the Condon Committee Went Wrong".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3035312" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on doi.org.</span></span></span>

None of this proves that the [Condon project]({{ 'condon-project/' | relative_url }}) was designed to destroy careers. It does show how institutional judgements can alter the cost-benefit calculation facing researchers. Once a prestigious inquiry concludes that a field is scientifically unproductive, a younger scientist considering a grant application, dissertation or publication has to ask why scarce career time should be spent there rather than on a subject recognised by established journals and funding bodies.

There was also an earlier official desire to reduce unproductive public fixation on UFOs. The CIA-sponsored Robertson Panel of 1953 viewed the flood of reports partly as a Cold War problem: excessive or false reports could burden warning systems and potentially be exploited by an adversary. CIA's historical account says the panel recommended educating military personnel, researchers and the public to identify common astronomical, meteorological and aviation phenomena that generated false reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/how-to-investigate-a-flying-saucer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How To Investigate a Flying Saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How To Investigate a Flying Saucer</span></span></span>

That policy context is sometimes presented as proof that every later instance of scientific scepticism was centrally engineered. The evidence supports a narrower conclusion. US security agencies genuinely worried about the social and military effects of UFO reporting and favoured reducing false alarms. That could reinforce a culture of dismissal, but it does not establish that sceptical scientists were acting under orders, much less that researchers who persisted faced covert physical elimination.

## Stigma could operate without any formal ban

The most effective professional stigma rarely requires an explicit rule saying, "Do not research this." It works through expectations.

A scientist deciding whether to enter a controversial subject must consider whether colleagues will take the work seriously, whether journals will review it fairly, whether funding is available and whether association with the topic will weaken confidence in unrelated work. These are especially powerful constraints in disciplines where appointments and promotions depend heavily on peer evaluation.

Historical UFO research presented an unusually difficult version of that problem. The empirical material was heterogeneous: eyewitness reports sat alongside photographs, radar records, misidentifications, deliberate hoaxes and extraordinary claims that were often impossible to reconstruct experimentally. A conscientious researcher could therefore spend considerable time establishing merely that a case was unresolved, without being able to show that the underlying cause was exotic. That made it easy for critics to ask what scientific return justified the effort.

The situation created a self-reinforcing loop. Because respected scientists hesitated to enter the field, much investigation remained in the hands of private UFO organisations and enthusiasts. Their dominance then made the topic appear still further removed from mainstream science. Poor evidence, sensational publicity and occasional credulity strengthened the association. Researchers who wanted better evidence consequently faced greater reputational risk for trying to obtain it.

Jacques Vallée's recollections provide a revealing example of this climate. Although he established a substantial career in computing, he was cautious about publicly tying his institutional position to his early UFO work. *Wired* reported that he did not want his Northwestern University affiliation highlighted on a book jacket and recalled scientists privately showing greater interest than they were willing to express publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/jacques-vallee-still-doesnt-know-what-ufos-are" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jacques Vallée Still Doesn&#x27;t Know What UFOs Are</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jacques Vallée Still Doesn&#x27;t Know What UFOs Are</span></span></span>

Such cases are difficult to quantify retrospectively, but the mechanism is straightforward: no conspiracy is necessary when researchers themselves anticipate that public association with a subject may damage credibility.

## Modern surveys show the reputational problem did not simply disappear

One reason the historical stigma deserves to be taken seriously is that it can now be measured, albeit imperfectly.

A 2023 study published in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* surveyed tenured and tenure-track academics across 14 disciplines at 144 high-research-activity US universities. Of 1,460 respondents included in the analysis, only 3.84 per cent reported having conducted UAP-related research, yet more than a third expressed at least some interest in doing so. Nearly two-thirds regarded academic evaluation of newly released UAP information as "Very Important" or "Absolutely Essential", while 37.26 per cent gave those ratings to the need for more UAP research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-01746-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

The contrast is instructive. Interest in examination was substantially greater than existing participation. Funding and respected peers also mattered: 54.86 per cent said funding would make them more inclined to conduct UAP research to at least some degree, while 43.4 per cent said they would be more inclined if a reputable scholar in their own discipline did so.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-01746-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

A follow-up analysis published in 2024 asked more directly about professional consequences. Some 69.04 per cent of faculty reported at least some concern that colleagues would ridicule them or give them a hard time for UAP research, while 52.67 per cent expressed some concern that such work could jeopardise tenure or promotion. Assistant professors, whose careers are generally most exposed to peer judgement, showed the highest concern about tenure or promotion, at 73.41 per cent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03351-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

Yet the same survey also provides an important corrective to exaggerated accounts of academic persecution. When respondents were asked whether a colleague's UAP research would negatively affect their own tenure or promotion vote, 61.92 per cent said no, 27.95 per cent said maybe and only 7.4 per cent said yes. The authors therefore concluded that researchers' fear of career consequences might be greater than colleagues' declared willingness to impose them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03351-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

Those results are not a precise measurement of the academic climate of McDonald's 1960s. The survey also had a low 3.9 per cent response rate, which the authors identify as its principal limitation. It nevertheless provides contemporary evidence for the central mechanism: researchers can perceive a subject as professionally hazardous even where there is no explicit institutional prohibition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03351-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

## NASA now treats stigma as a data-quality problem

The clearest institutional reversal came from NASA's 2023 Independent Study Team on unidentified anomalous phenomena, the modern governmental term that includes what earlier decades generally called UFOs.

NASA did not endorse an extraterrestrial explanation. Its report instead stressed that available observations were too inconsistent and poorly curated to support definitive conclusions. But it explicitly identified stigma as part of the measurement problem. The panel found that the negative perception surrounding UAP reporting obstructed data collection and that NASA's participation could help reduce a stigma that was probably causing reports and information to be lost before scientists could examine them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=69d1cddfd9ee7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA should therefore explore the viability of developing or acquiring such a crowdsourcing system as part of its strategy. That is an important distinction. Destigmatising investigation does not mean lowering evidential standards. In NASA's formulation, the reason to reduce ridicule is almost the opposite: better reporting makes conventional explanations easier to establish and genuinely anomalous cases easier to isolate.

Modern projects such as the Galileo Project have adopted the same basic strategy by shifting attention away from anecdote towards calibrated, multimodal instruments. Its published methodology describes wide-field and narrow-field cameras, radar-related measurements, radio receivers, microphones and environmental sensors designed to identify ordinary artefacts and corroborate unusual detections across several independent channels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The lesson is less dramatic than either believers or dismissive sceptics sometimes suggest. A topic can carry substantial stigma and still contain many mistaken reports. Removing stigma is therefore not evidence that extraordinary UFO claims are true. It is a way of improving the process by which claims are tested.

## McDonald's case shows resistance, not proof of a lethal campaign

Within the wider subject of allegedly suspicious deaths or disappearances among UFO and unconventional-propulsion researchers, McDonald presents a crucial evidential boundary.

His career supports the proposition that serious UFO investigation could expose a scientist to an unusually hostile professional environment. He publicly challenged influential official conclusions, argued that mainstream science had neglected significant reports and became closely identified with a field many colleagues considered scientifically unrewarding. The broader historical record, followed by modern survey research and NASA's own discussion of stigma, makes professional marginalisation entirely plausible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/mcdon2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[cufon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Computer UFO Network New Page 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Computer UFO Network New Page 1</span></span></span>

But that evidence cannot simply be carried over into a claim that McDonald was killed because of his UFO work. Professional ridicule, funding difficulty, institutional resistance, intelligence-agency interest and homicide are different propositions requiring different evidence.

This distinction matters because conspiracy narratives often join them into a single continuum: a scientist was mocked, therefore powerful institutions feared his research; institutions feared his research, therefore they may have monitored him; if they monitored him, an unexpected death becomes suspicious; and suspicion is then treated as evidence of murder. Each step requires independent support. Evidence for the first step cannot substitute for evidence for the last.

The historically defensible conclusion is consequently narrower but still significant. UFO investigation acquired a genuine reputational penalty within much of twentieth-century science. James McDonald encountered that environment while trying to move the subject into formal scientific debate, and the later Condon consensus helped make sustained academic engagement less attractive. Modern research indicates that fear of ridicule and career consequences remains measurable even after renewed government and scientific interest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-i.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Section I: Conclusions &amp; Recommendations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Section I: Conclusions &amp; Recommendations...</span></span></span>

That makes stigma relevant background when evaluating the lives and careers of researchers later incorporated into "suppressed scientist" narratives. It can explain why research was abandoned, why witnesses remained quiet, why projects struggled for institutional legitimacy and why a controversial investigator might become professionally isolated. What it cannot do, without separate forensic or documentary evidence, is turn isolation into proof of assassination.

    return encodeURIComponent(String(value || '').replace(/\s+/g, ' ').trim()).replace(/%20/g, '+');
  }
  function buildUrl(query, reference, marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket];
    if (!cfg) return '#';
    var template = String(cfg.url_template || 'https://www.ebay.com/sch/i.html?_nkw={query}');
    var url = template.replace('{query}', encodeQuery(query || '')).replace('{query_plain}', String(query || ''));
    var joiner = url.indexOf('?') >= 0 ? '&' : '?';
    var params = [];
    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
