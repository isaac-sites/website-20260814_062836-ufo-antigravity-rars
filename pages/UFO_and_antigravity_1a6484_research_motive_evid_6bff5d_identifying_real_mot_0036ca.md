## Introduction

A credible suppression theory needs an actor, not merely an interest. In allegations that UFO, unconventional-propulsion or [antigravity]({{ 'antigravity/' | relative_url }}) researchers were silenced, phrases such as “the government”, “the military-industrial complex”, “oil interests” or “intelligence” are too broad to establish motive. The useful question is narrower: **which identifiable organisation or person knew what the researcher was doing, possessed something concrete to protect, and demonstrated concern about disclosure or continued research?**

The historical record shows that such actors can sometimes be identified. US defence agencies have statutory mechanisms for restricting inventions on national-security grounds; the Department of Defense directly funded Ning Li's gravity research; and Air Force counter-intelligence personnel demonstrably took an interest in Paul Bennewitz when his observations touched a sensitive military environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/35/181" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[cornell.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute35 U.S. Code § 181</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secrecy of certain inventions and withholding of patent &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information InstituteJuly 19, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 19, 1952</span></span></span> What the record does **not** establish is the crucial further proposition that these interests produced killings. Identifying a genuine motive to control information is therefore an important filter, but it is the beginning of a suppression case rather than its conclusion.

## From vague interests to identifiable actors

The most common weakness in alleged scientist-suppression narratives is a jump from technological significance to murderous motive. If effective gravity control really existed, it might have military, aerospace and commercial consequences. That proposition can identify broad constituencies that *could* care about the research, but it cannot tell us who actually did.

A stronger analysis moves through increasingly specific levels. First comes **general interest**: a military service would predictably care about a radically new propulsion system. Next comes **documented institutional knowledge**: a particular defence office funded, reviewed or received information about the research. More significant still is **documented restrictive authority or behaviour**: classification, a secrecy order, publication restrictions, counter-intelligence activity or an attempt to redirect somebody away from sensitive information. Only after those steps does it become reasonable to investigate whether adverse conduct had any connection to a researcher's death or disappearance.

Thomas Townsend Brown's Project Winterhaven is useful because it shows the difference. Brown did not merely speculate privately about electrogravitics. His 1952–53 proposal explicitly sought a joint-services research and development contract and envisaged a Department of Defense relationship involving a prime contractor and multiple research organisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thomastownsendbrown.com/hydro/winter.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thomastownsendbrown.com">[Thomas Townsend Brown]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thomastownsendbrown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas Townsend Brown Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas Townsend BrownLibrary - ElectrohydrodynamicsJanuary 1, 1953...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1953</span></span></span> A later congressional hearing record reproduced material describing Brown's proposal to the military and the contemporary interest surrounding electrogravitics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/107/chrg/CHRG-107shrg80649/CHRG-107shrg80649.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CHRG 107shrg80649</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">S. HRG. 107–564November 11, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 11, 2025</span></span></span>

That establishes potential military awareness and a conceivable strategic interest. It does not establish a campaign to silence Brown. Indeed, there is an important alternative explanation for institutional reluctance: subsequent research has generally treated the Biefeld–Brown force as an electrohydrodynamic phenomenon associated with charged particles rather than demonstrated control of gravity. Experimental and theoretical work has reproduced the force while investigating ionic mechanisms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.1393" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv An analysis of the Brown-Biefeld effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An analysis of the Brown-Biefeld effectNovember 5, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2010</span></span></span> An agency declining or restricting a speculative propulsion proposal is therefore not, by itself, evidence that officials secretly believed it worked.

This distinction matters throughout the subject. **A credible candidate actor is one for whom knowledge can be demonstrated independently of the alleged conspiracy.** Contracts, grant files, security clearances, patent proceedings, correspondence and counter-intelligence records are substantially stronger evidence than reasoning backwards from a researcher's death to whichever institution appears most powerful.

## Records that show who knew about the work

Ning Li provides one of the clearest examples of how documentary evidence can narrow the field without proving a sinister outcome. Li's work at the University of Alabama in Huntsville concerned claimed gravitational effects associated with superconductors. After leaving academia and forming AC Gravity, her company received $448,970 in US government funding in 2001 for an experimental effort intended to test theoretical predictions about generating gravitational effects with a superconducting disc. Contemporary programme information identifies the awarding organisation as the US Army Aviation and Missile Command and gives an effective period from April 2001 to September 2002.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufos-scientificresearch.blogspot.com/2019/02/anomalous-aerospace-phenomena-and-anti.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufos-scientificresearch.blogspot.com">[UFOs Scientific Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufos-scientificresearch.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs Scientific Research Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs Scientific ResearchUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena - scientific research: Anomalous Aerospace Phenomena; and &quot;anti-gravity&quot;February...</span></span></span>

That evidence changes the motive question substantially. It is unnecessary to speculate that an unspecified intelligence organisation somehow discovered Li's research: an identifiable component of the defence establishment was paying for it. Later reporting found that Li gave a 2003 presentation, “Measurability of AC Gravity Fields”, in a defence-connected setting and that her public publication trail then largely ceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntsvillebusinessjournal.com/news/2023/07/30/solving-the-mystery-of-huntsvilles-brilliant-scientist-disappearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvillebusinessjournal.com">[Huntsville Business Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvillebusinessjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Huntsville Business JournalUncovering The Mystery Of Huntsville’s Brilliant Anti-gravity Scientist - Huntsville Business Journal...</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence about what happened next comes not from conspiracy literature but from Li's son, George Guangyu Men. In a 2023 investigation by the *Huntsville Business Journal*, Men said that his mother obtained a top-secret clearance and could no longer discuss her work as she had while in academia. The reporter also described unsuccessful Freedom of Information Act attempts to obtain results from the 2001 defence-funded work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntsvillebusinessjournal.com/news/2023/07/30/solving-the-mystery-of-huntsvilles-brilliant-scientist-disappearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvillebusinessjournal.com">[Huntsville Business Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvillebusinessjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Huntsville Business JournalUncovering The Mystery Of Huntsville’s Brilliant Anti-gravity Scientist - Huntsville Business Journal...</span></span></span>

Those facts support a relatively modest but important conclusion: **there was an identifiable national-security actor with both knowledge of Li's research and an institutional reason to control information about it.** They make “classified defence work” considerably more plausible than a vague claim that powerful interests must have noticed her.

They simultaneously weaken the more dramatic suppression story. Li did not mysteriously vanish after her grant. According to her son's account, she remained in Huntsville and continued [classified work]({{ 'classified-work/' | relative_url }}). In 2014 she was struck by a vehicle while crossing a road on the University of Alabama in Huntsville campus; her husband witnessed the accident. She suffered serious lasting injuries and died in 2021 after years of care.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntsvillebusinessjournal.com/news/2023/07/30/solving-the-mystery-of-huntsvilles-brilliant-scientist-disappearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvillebusinessjournal.com">[Huntsville Business Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvillebusinessjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Huntsville Business JournalUncovering The Mystery Of Huntsville’s Brilliant Anti-gravity Scientist - Huntsville Business Journal...</span></span></span> The same evidence that identifies the Department of Defense as an organisation with a genuine secrecy interest therefore provides no demonstrated motive for eliminating Li. If anything, continued employment would suggest that the institution's immediate interest was in **retaining and restricting access to expertise**, not destroying it.

That is an important corrective to suppression theories. When an organisation can obtain secrecy through contracts, classification, security clearances or employment restrictions, homicide is not automatically the most rational—or evidentially supported—mechanism.

## The state already has mechanisms for restricting technology

The strongest evidence that US national-security institutions sometimes have a motive to prevent technical information becoming public does not come from UFO cases at all. It is written into law.

Under 35 U.S.C. §181, publication of a patent application or issuance of a patent can be withheld when an interested government agency considers disclosure detrimental to national security. The provision applies both to inventions in which the government has a property interest and, under specified procedures, to privately developed inventions in which it does not. Orders can be renewed when the responsible agency determines that the national interest continues to require secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/35/181" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute35 U.S. Code § 181</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secrecy of certain inventions and withholding of patent &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information InstituteJuly 19, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 19, 1952</span></span></span>

This is not merely dormant statutory language. US Patent and Trademark Office figures obtained through Freedom of Information Act requests showed thousands of secrecy orders in force in the 2000s and 2010s. At the end of fiscal year 2018 there were 5,792, including orders affecting inventions developed without a government property interest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/invention-secrecy-2018/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federation of American Scientists Invention Secrecy Hits Recent High</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American ScientistsInvention Secrecy Hits Recent High - Federation of American ScientistsOctober 31, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 31, 2018</span></span></span> Earlier figures likewise showed that defence agencies were major originators of secrecy orders; in fiscal year 2009, for example, the Navy accounted for 39 new orders and the National Security Agency for 12.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/invention_secrecy_2009/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federation of American Scientists Invention Secrecy at Highest in a Decade</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American Scientists Invention Secrecy at Highest in a Decade</span></span></span>

This gives researchers a much more precise model of what an authentic government motive looks like. The motive is not necessarily “this invention threatens the existing order”. It can be as ordinary and documentable as **preventing a potentially militarily useful technology from becoming available to foreign governments**.

It also changes what evidence should be sought in an alleged antigravity suppression case. If a researcher really possessed a breakthrough considered militarily sensitive, investigators should look for the institutional traces that comparable sensitive technologies generate: patent-security review, government contracts, export-control issues, classified programme participation, security clearances, publication review, restricted technical reports or correspondence with an identifiable defence office. The absence of all such traces does not prove that no covert interest existed, but it makes claims of intense official concern harder to substantiate.

Most importantly, documented secrecy mechanisms show why **evidence of secrecy cannot simply be converted into evidence of murder**. The government already possesses lawful administrative tools for restricting dissemination. A theory proposing a much more extreme intervention has to explain why those tools were insufficient and provide evidence that somebody decided to go beyond them.

## When concern becomes adverse intent

The evidential threshold rises sharply when the claim moves from “an organisation wanted information controlled” to “an organisation wanted a person silenced”. Funding and classification establish knowledge and interests; they do not establish [hostility]({{ 'hostility/' | relative_url }}).

The Paul Bennewitz affair is instructive precisely because it provides something unusually difficult to find in alleged UFO suppression cases: an identifiable counter-intelligence actor associated with deliberate intervention against a named civilian. Bennewitz lived near Kirtland Air Force Base and used electronic equipment while investigating unusual activity around the military complex. Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI) Special Agent Richard Doty became involved with him. Doty's association with the Kirtland case is documented, and Doty later acknowledged feeding Bennewitz UFO-related disinformation intended to divert him from information AFOSI wanted protected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://disclosdex.com/people/richard-doty" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosdex.com">[Disclosdex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosdex.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Richard Doty &#124; Disclosdex</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Richard Doty &#124; Disclosdex...</span></span></span>

The case should not be stretched beyond what it demonstrates. Bennewitz was not an antigravity scientist who was assassinated, and Doty's later extraordinary UFO claims are themselves problematic because he has openly described participating in disinformation. But as a **mechanism example**, the affair is valuable: it illustrates the evidential difference between imagining that an agency might interfere and identifying personnel, an operational reason for concern and actual interference.

In a suspected scientist-suppression case, comparable evidence would be highly significant. A warning from an identified security officer, documented surveillance, instructions to cease publication, deliberate dissemination of false information, threats traceable to an interested party, efforts to seize technical material or communications discussing how to neutralise a researcher would move a theory from abstract motive towards adverse intent.

Without that transition, even authentic government attention remains ambiguous. An agency can investigate somebody because it wants their technology, fears espionage, needs to protect an existing classified programme, suspects a security breach or simply wants to understand what the person has observed. Those motives may produce secrecy and intrusive conduct without implying an intention to cause physical harm.

## Commercial disruption is a weaker motive unless someone acted on it

Claims involving aerospace, energy or defence companies require the same discipline. A genuine gravity-control technology would obviously have potentially disruptive economic consequences. Yet saying that an invention “would destroy the aerospace industry” or “would make fossil fuels obsolete” does not identify a perpetrator.

The problem is that revolutionary technology can create opposite incentives simultaneously. An incumbent aerospace company might lose value if its existing products became obsolete, but it could gain enormously by acquiring the new technology. A defence contractor might prefer secrecy, but also have a powerful incentive to employ the inventor, obtain intellectual property or win government development contracts. A government might fear uncontrolled proliferation while simultaneously spending money to develop the same capability.

Brown's Project Winterhaven makes this tension unusually visible. His proposal envisaged bringing research institutions and contractors into a government-supported development structure rather than presenting industry and the inventor as natural enemies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thomastownsendbrown.com/hydro/winter.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thomastownsendbrown.com">[Thomas Townsend Brown]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thomastownsendbrown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas Townsend Brown Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas Townsend BrownLibrary - ElectrohydrodynamicsJanuary 1, 1953...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1953</span></span></span> Li's case similarly shows the Department of Defense funding experimental gravity research rather than simply attempting to make it disappear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufos-scientificresearch.blogspot.com/2019/02/anomalous-aerospace-phenomena-and-anti.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufos-scientificresearch.blogspot.com">[UFOs Scientific Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufos-scientificresearch.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs Scientific Research Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs Scientific ResearchUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena - scientific research: Anomalous Aerospace Phenomena; and &quot;anti-gravity&quot;February...</span></span></span>

Consequently, a commercial-[motive claim]({{ 'motive-claim/' | relative_url }}) becomes persuasive only when general economic exposure can be tied to a specific conflict: an acquisition offer that was rejected, litigation over ownership, a patent dispute, termination of funding following a disclosure disagreement, corporate espionage, documented pressure on a publisher, threats, or internal correspondence describing the researcher's work as a problem requiring action.

Without evidence of that kind, “established industry had billions to lose” is not a case-specific motive. It is an economic hypothesis that could be applied to almost any proposed disruptive invention.

## Foreign intelligence is plausible in principle but needs the same proof

Advanced propulsion also creates an obvious foreign-intelligence hypothesis. If one state believed another state's researcher had developed a militarily transformative propulsion or energy technology, obtaining the research, recruiting the scientist or preventing a rival from exploiting it could all be rational objectives.

Yet this possibility can become a convenient evidential escape hatch. Once domestic institutions fail to provide a convincing suspect, an unspecified foreign service can be inserted into the story precisely because its activities are difficult to observe. That makes the theory harder to falsify rather than better supported.

The Li case again demonstrates the distinction. Her work was funded through a US defence relationship, and her son described subsequent classified employment. Those are positive pieces of evidence identifying who demonstrably knew about her work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntsvillebusinessjournal.com/news/2023/07/30/solving-the-mystery-of-huntsvilles-brilliant-scientist-disappearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntsvillebusinessjournal.com">[Huntsville Business Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntsvillebusinessjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Huntsville Business JournalUncovering The Mystery Of Huntsville’s Brilliant Anti-gravity Scientist - Huntsville Business Journal...</span></span></span> By contrast, nationality, international scientific contacts or the general fact that foreign intelligence services seek advanced technology would not establish that a particular foreign service targeted her.

For foreign-intelligence motive to become case-specific, investigators would need evidence such as documented approaches to the scientist, counter-intelligence [warnings]({{ 'warnings/' | relative_url }}), unusual attempts to acquire restricted material, intercepted communications, prosecutions, expulsions, financial transfers or testimony independently corroborated by records. The evidential standard should not become lower merely because the proposed actor operates secretly.

## Amy Eskridge shows why motive claims need independent corroboration

The death of Huntsville researcher Amy Eskridge illustrates the difficulty especially clearly. Her 2022 obituary confirms that she was president and chairwoman of the Institute for Exotic Science and describes her scientific interests and University of Alabama in Huntsville background.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.legacy.com/us/obituaries/huntsville/name/amy-eskridge-obituary?id=35311909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legacy.com">[Legacy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legacy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">amy eskridge obituary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">amy eskridge obituary</span></span></span> Her work and public statements have subsequently become incorporated into allegations that researchers connected with unconventional propulsion were being targeted.

For the narrow question of motive, however, the publicly accessible evidence is much less developed than in Li's case. There is no comparably clear public record establishing that a named government programme possessed Eskridge's technology, regarded her personally as a security problem and decided to intervene against her. Recent reporting on renewed allegations surrounding deaths of scientists has also recorded a sharply different perspective from Eskridge's family: they said she suffered chronic pain and cautioned against treating scientists' deaths as inherently mysterious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ksl.com/article/news/utah/police-and-courts/deaths-of-several-scientists-in-recent-months-sparks-federal-investigation/51486507" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ksl.com">[KSL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ksl.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ksl.com.</span></span></span>

This does not settle every disputed detail surrounding her death. It does establish why retrospective motive construction is hazardous. A researcher's interest in exotic propulsion, proximity to NASA or defence institutions, and expressed fears about suppression can justify investigating whether a particular organisation had taken an adverse interest in them. They cannot substitute for evidence that such an organisation actually did so.

The decisive records would be those identifying the other side of the alleged conflict: **Who contacted her? Who issued an instruction? Who wanted particular information withheld? What programme or company was threatened? What action did that actor take?** Until those questions produce corroborated answers, the identity of a supposed suppressor remains an allegation rather than an evidential finding.

## A motive that can actually be tested

Across [UFO and antigravity]({{ 'ufo-and-antigravity/' | relative_url }}) suppression claims, the most useful distinction is therefore not between “believers” and “sceptics”, but between different strengths of evidence.

An actor moves from speculative to credible when the record can answer three increasingly demanding questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Who knew?** A contract, briefing, security file, meeting, patent review, correspondence or funding relationship identifies the person or organisation with actual knowledge of the work.
2. **What did they need to protect?** The evidence identifies a concrete interest—classified capabilities, technical intelligence, intellectual property, an existing secret programme or prevention of foreign access—rather than a generic desire to preserve power.
3. **What shows adverse intent?** Surveillance, threats, disinformation, publication restrictions, coercive approaches or communications about stopping the individual show that passive interest became intervention.

</div>

Only after those points are established does the alleged death or disappearance become relevant to a stronger causal inquiry. Even then, investigators still need evidence connecting the actor's adverse intent to the event itself.

The available record demonstrates that governments have sometimes regarded advanced technologies as sufficiently sensitive to restrict their publication, that defence agencies have funded unconventional gravity research, and that military counter-intelligence has interfered with at least some civilians investigating activity around sensitive installations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/35/181" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[cornell.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute35 U.S. Code § 181</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secrecy of certain inventions and withholding of patent &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information InstituteJuly 19, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 19, 1952</span></span></span> Those facts make secrecy and information control historically real mechanisms rather than purely imaginary ones.

They do **not**, however, establish a documented programme of murdering antigravity or UFO researchers. That distinction is the central test. The strongest suppression theory will not be the one with the most powerful hypothetical enemy, but the one that can name an actor, demonstrate what that actor knew, identify what it stood to protect, and show through independent records that its concern progressed from secrecy or competition to adverse action against the particular researcher.
